**Caveats/flags on 1a:**
- Egypt: many archaeologists use "Chalcolithic" loosely for the entire Predynastic (a period increasingly also just called "Predynastic" or treated as continuous Neolithic-to-Naqada), rather than as a sharply bounded stage. The 3800–3700 BC starting figure is sourced only via a Wikipedia summary of a primary radiocarbon paper (Dee et al., Oxford, 2013) which was not itself fetched — flagged as a locator-only date, not independently confirmed.
- Levant: internal boundaries (Late Neolithic→Chalcolithic, Chalcolithic→EB I) are explicitly described in the literature as "imprecisely dated," with radiocarbon evidence not settling whether Chalcolithic and EB I overlap chronologically. See [ISAC/U. Chicago](https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/intro_to_cultural_chronology_chalco.pdf).
- Anatolia: at least three incompatible sub-periodizations are in active use (Central, Eastern/Caucasus, Western Anatolia each differ by centuries), and some general sources round the whole period to "6000–3000 BC" — treat single-figure Anatolian ranges with caution.

### 1b. Is there a defensible pan-regional / "Old World" range?

**No — be direct: the term is irreducibly regional.** The most authoritative source located on this exact question is a dedicated historiographic paper:

> "In contemporary dating schemes, the definition of the Copper Age varies according to regional and national traditions... in contemporary usage across Europe, the definition of the Copper Age varies according to regional and national traditions, and the term is even eschewed in some countries, which can engender confusion... there is no general agreement about what the Copper Age actually is."
[Roberts, "The 'Copper Age'—A History of the Concept," Journal of World Prehistory, Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10963-019-09134-z)

Consistent with this, this research found:
- A span of **c. 6500 BC (earliest copper mineral/native-copper use, Balkans) to c. 1400 BC (terminus of some South Asian Chalcolithic cultures)** if one tried to bound "the Chalcolithic" globally — an ~5,000-year window that overlaps the Neolithic, the entire Bronze Age, and part of the Iron Age elsewhere. This is not usable as a single dataset entry.
- Regional Chalcolithic sequences do not line up even approximately: the Balkans/Levant/Anatolia/Mesopotamia cluster is largely a 6th–4th millennium BC phenomenon; the South Asian one is a 3rd–1st millennium BC phenomenon; Iberia/Britain treat the "Chalcolithic" as a brief 25th–22nd century BC phase associated specifically with Bell Beaker communities ([Bournemouth Univ., "Is there a British Chalcolithic?"](https://assets.bournemouth.ac.uk/news-archive/conservation/conferences/chalcolithic/contentonly_1_6024_6024.html)).
- A named academic debate exists over the "Chalcolithic frontier" — whether Northwest Europe (Britain/Ireland) has a Chalcolithic at all, or lies definitionally beyond it — which by itself shows the term is not treated as universal even within Europe: [Academia.edu paper on the "Chalcolithic frontier" in Northwest Europe](https://www.academia.edu/392381/Drawing_boundaries_and_building_models_investigating_the_concept_of_the_Chalcolithic_frontier_in_Northwest_Europe).
- The multi-regional Oxford Handbook volume explicitly organizes the Chalcolithic Levant discussion by parallel, non-synchronized regional columns (Southern Levant / Syria / Lebanon / Egypt / Cyprus each with different absolute dates for "equivalent" stages): [Oxford Academic, "Introduction to the Levant During the Chalcolithic Period: Regional Perspectives"](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/34497/chapter/292699456).

**Conclusion for 1b:** There is no defensible single pan-regional "Old World Chalcolithic" date range. Any global figure (e.g., "6500–2000 BC" as sometimes given in generalist tertiary sources) is an artifact of taking the earliest regional start and latest regional end and does not describe any coherent shared period of technology, society, or chronology.

### 1c. Position relative to Neolithic and Bronze Age

- **In every region sourced, the Chalcolithic is presented as sitting between the regional Neolithic and the regional Bronze Age, not overlapping cleanly with a single global boundary.** It functions as a genuine third stage layered into the standard "Stone→Bronze→Iron" three-age model, per the historical origin of the concept: Evans (1881) proposed "a transitional Copper Age between the Neolithic and the Bronze Age" — see [Wikipedia, Three-age system — locator only, cites Evans directly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-age_system).
- **Against the dataset's current spine** (Neolithic ending / Bronze Age starting at exactly 3,300 BC with no gap): the sourced regional Chalcolithic ranges show that this single "3,300 BC" seam is far too late for the Balkans/Levant/Anatolia/Mesopotamia (where the Chalcolithic runs roughly 5500–3700/3100 BC, i.e., the "gap" the dataset currently elides is real and multi-century in the Old World core) and far too early for South Asia (where regional "Chalcolithic" cultures don't even begin until c. 3000–2000 BC and run past 1200 BC into the dataset's Iron Age band).
- In the Balkans/Anatolia/Mesopotamia core zone, the Chalcolithic **replaces** the space the dataset's current spine assigns jointly to the tail of the Neolithic and the start of the Bronze Age — it does not sit alongside them, it sits **inside** that seam. Concretely: Uruk (treated as Late Chalcolithic in northern-Mesopotamian LC terminology) runs to c. 3100 BC ([Wikipedia, Uruk period — locator only, cites primary chronology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uruk_period)), meaning the regional Bronze Age proper does not begin until after that, not at 3,300 BC.
- In the Levant specifically, several scholars argue the Chalcolithic and EB I **temporally overlap** rather than cleanly succeed one another, though the ISAC source states radiocarbon data does not actually support this overlap claim — the boundary is disputed, not settled: [ISAC/U. Chicago](https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/intro_to_cultural_chronology_chalco.pdf).
- In South Asia, "Chalcolithic" cultures (Ahar, Malwa, Jorwe) are explicitly Bronze/Copper-Age-contemporary or later phenomena that **run concurrently with, rather than before,** the mature Bronze Age urbanism of the Indus/Harappan zone elsewhere in the subcontinent — see the regional culture-sequence sources above. This means "Chalcolithic" in South Asia is not simply "the period before the Bronze Age" but a technologically-defined stage that coexists with Bronze Age societies elsewhere in the same region.

**Conclusion for 1c:** The Chalcolithic does not "slot between" Neolithic and Bronze Age at one shared date in the way the dataset's clean 3,300 BC seam implies. It is a regionally-variable wedge whose width and position differ so much between regions that inserting it as a single global interval would create either overlaps or gaps with the existing Neolithic/Bronze Age boundary depending on the region chosen.

### 1e. Earliest securely dated copper metallurgy/smelting

**Belovode, Rudnik mountain, Eastern Serbia (Vinča culture) — c. 5000 BC, currently the earliest securely dated evidence of copper smelting anywhere.**

> "The first smelting event so far documented at Belovode occurs at c. 5000 BC, which makes it the earliest securely dated record of extractive metallurgy, anywhere."
[Radivojević et al., "On the origins of extractive metallurgy: new evidence from Europe"](http://wwwhomes.uni-bielefeld.de/tkohers/Bronzegu%DF/Rohstoffe/Radivojevic,%20On%20the%20origins%20of%20extractive%20metallurgy%20new%20evidence%20from%20Europe.pdf)

Corroborated:
> "The c. 5 g of copper slag from the site of Belovode dated to c. 5000 bc and published in Radivojević et al. (2010) represent the earliest recorded copper production evidence in Eurasian metallurgy."
[Cambridge, "Inventing Metallurgy in Western Eurasia: a Look Through the Microscope Lens"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/F99DD8BDE33F603786CF81FB9871D316/S0959774314001097a.pdf/inventing-metallurgy-in-western-eurasia-a-look-through-the-microscope-lens.pdf)

> "At Belovode, the project revealed chronologically and contextually secure evidence for copper smelting in the 49th century BC. This confirms the earlier interpretation of c. 7000-year-old metallurgy at the site, making it the earliest record of fully developed metallurgical activity in the world."
[OAPEN open-access book, "The Rise of Metallurgy in Eurasia"](https://library.oapen.org/handle/20.500.12657/58979)

**Method:** pyrometallurgical copper smelting from ore (not just cold-hammering of native copper) — attested by copper slag, malachite beads, and ceramics with bone-ash lagging in a securely dated stratigraphic horizon at the Vinča-culture settlement of Belovode; associated with intensive mining activity at nearby Rudna Glava from around the same date: [UCL Discovery, "Paint It Black: the rise of metallurgy in the Balkans"](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1462418/2/Rehren.1462418_Paint_it_Black_revised_draft-1.pdf).

**Distinguish from earlier cold-worked native copper use:** simple hammering of native (unsmelted) copper is attested much earlier and elsewhere — e.g., the North American Great Lakes "Old Copper Complex" from around 6,500 BC, and native-copper artefacts in the Balkans from before 5000 BC — but this is a different, simpler technology (no smelting, no chemical transformation of ore) and is explicitly distinguished by the sources from true extractive/smelting metallurgy: [Wikipedia, Chalcolithic — locator only, for the Old Copper Complex framing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalcolithic).

**Independent, separately-originated smelting traditions** are documented for Iberia (Cerro Virtud, Almería, 4910–4460 cal BC) and southeastern Iran (Tal-i Iblis, second half of 6th/first half of 5th millennium cal BC), supporting a multi-centre rather than single-origin model of early metallurgy: [Academia.edu, "Chalcolithic copper smelting"](https://www.academia.edu/8164398/Chalcolithic_copper_smelting).

### 1f. Chalcolithic vs Copper Age vs Eneolithic — naming or substantive difference?

**Primarily a naming difference driven by national/regional scholarly traditions, though the underlying etymologies differ and usage is not perfectly interchangeable.**

- "Chalcolithic" (Greek chalkos + lithos, "copper-stone") and "Eneolithic"/"Aeneolithic" (Latin aeneus + Greek lithos, same meaning) are philological synonyms; "Copper Age" is the English descriptive equivalent.
- Direct sourced statement: "the terms Eneolithic and Chalcolithic are generally used as synonyms of Copper Age, but taken philologically would denote the period when copper (or bronze) is used alongside stone" — [Roberts, "The 'Copper Age'—A History of the Concept," Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10963-019-09134-z).
- Regional/national preference is well documented for the Balkans specifically: "Eneolithic" is preferred in the former Yugoslavia and Romania, "Copper Age" in Hungary, and "Chalcolithic" is the term the cited Springer paper's authors themselves prefer — but the same paper also shows these are not always exact chronological synonyms: former-Yugoslav "Eneolithic" begins with the *start of metal use* (mid–late 5th millennium BC), which Bulgarian archaeologists would instead call the *Middle-to-Late* Chalcolithic (i.e., after metal had already been in use for centuries) — [Springer, "Early Balkan Metallurgy"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10963-021-09155-7).
- In the Russian/former-Soviet and broader Eurasian steppe tradition, "Eneolithic" is the standard term and, per the Chalcolithic Wikipedia locator entry, some steppe regions (e.g., much of Russia) are described as having no "well-defined Copper Age between the Stone and Bronze Ages" at all — suggesting that even where the label is used, it does not always denote a sharply bounded independent stage.
- A modern Wikipedia summary (locator only) states plainly: "Today, Copper Age, Eneolithic, and Chalcolithic are used synonymously to mean Evans's original definition of Copper Age" — [Wikipedia, Chalcolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalcolithic) — consistent with, though not independently verifying beyond, the peer-reviewed Roberts (2019/2021) paper's more nuanced regional account.

**Conclusion for 1f:** Treat the three terms as effectively interchangeable at the level of a dataset label (they denote the same underlying concept — copper use alongside continued stone-tool use, preceding tin-bronze alloying), but flag that "Eneolithic" carries a slightly different regional-chronological anchoring in some traditions (start-of-metal-use vs. metal-already-established), so if the dataset ever needs to align a specific "Eneolithic X culture" date range against a "Chalcolithic Y period" date range in the same region, check which convention the source culture-name uses.

## 2. EPIPALAEOLITHIC

### 2a. Meaning, date range, and regional research traditions

**Definition:** "final Upper Palaeolithic industries occurring at the end of the final glaciation which appear to merge technologically into the Mesolithic" — a period characterized by microlithic stone-tool technology, generally dated from c. 20,000 BP to 10,000 BP in the Levant, later in Europe. [Wikipedia, Epipalaeolithic — used here only as locator for the definition; corroborated by the peer-reviewed sources below]

Corroborated by peer-reviewed sources:
- "Epipalaeolithic archaeological cultures emerge as early as c. 23,000 cal bc in southwestern Asia, continuing until c. 10,000 cal bc" — [Oxford Academic, "The Natufian Archaeological Culture in Southwestern Asian..."](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/59635/chapter/505050944).
- "Southwestern Asian Epipalaeolithic archaeological cultures are... defined, like European Mesolithic cultures, by the appearance of microliths in chipped-stone tool assemblages" and the period is "bracketed by the Last Glacial Maximum and the Pleistocene–Holocene transition" — same source.

**Regional research traditions using "Epipalaeolithic":**
- **West Asia/Levant** — the term of choice; "Mesolithic" is very rarely applied here. Confirmed directly: an Oxford Academic chapter explicitly frames the term as the standard one for this region, and the Cambridge Core "Quaternary of the Levant" chapter treats "Epipalaeolithic" as the unmarked standard label for this period and region — [Cambridge Core, "The Early and Middle Epipalaeolithic of Cisjordan"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/quaternary-of-the-levant/early-and-middle-epipalaeolithic-of-cisjordan/DAF2AC6AFB4441CF6C18D7B48C192B09).
- **Wider Near East / North Africa** — also standard, per the Iberomaurusian/Capsian sourcing in 2e below.
- **Europe** — used comparatively rarely, mostly for the earlier part of what is otherwise called "Mesolithic," and especially in southern Europe (Iberia, Franco-Cantabrian region, southeast Europe) where continuity from the Upper Palaeolithic is emphasized rather than a sharp post-glacial break: "In the south of Europe where Mesolithic occupation followed that of the Palaeolithic, the term 'Epipalaeolithic'... has been widely used and still appears today... In the north, however, the term Mesolithic highlighted the apparent dynamism and distinctiveness of societies that succeeded in expanding into new areas" — [Univ. of Leeds/White Rose eprints, "Mesolithic Europe: glimpses of another world"](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/3523/1/spikinsp1_mesolithic.pdf).

### 2b. Is Epipalaeolithic a synonym for Mesolithic, partial overlap, or distinct concept? (Key question)

**This is a genuine and long-standing terminological ambiguity in the literature, not a settled equivalence — treat as regionally-conditioned overlap, not a clean synonym, and definitely not two separate global periods that would double-count the same span everywhere.**

The clearest sourced framing:
> "Southwestern Asian cultures are called Epipalaeolithic, while European cultures are called Mesolithic, despite the shared microlithic defining characteristic." The page explicitly does **not** state the terms are exact synonyms.
[Oxford Academic, "The Natufian Archaeological Culture in Southwestern Asian..."](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/59635/chapter/505050944)

Two different scholarly logics for using the two terms are documented:
1. **Geographic/regional-tradition split** — "Epipalaeolithic" for West Asia/Near East/North Africa (and sometimes Southeast Europe), "Mesolithic" for (mainly northern/western) Europe, describing the *same underlying kind of period* in different places. Under this logic the terms are near-synonyms applied by convention to different regions, and a global dataset should **not** carry both as separate entities for the same span, because that would double-count.
2. **Conceptual/functional split** (used by some authors) — "Epipalaeolithic" for hunter-gatherer groups whose technology continues a Palaeolithic trajectory and are **not** followed locally by an agricultural (Neolithic) transition, reserving "Mesolithic" specifically for cultures that **are** clearly succeeded by the Neolithic Revolution (the Natufian is given as the example of a Mesolithic-by-this-logic culture that is elsewhere called Epipalaeolithic) — see the Wikipedia Mesolithic locator entry, and independently corroborated in an Indian postgraduate archaeology course text: "societies that continued a Paleolithic way of life... or whose tool traditions continue relatively unchanged from Late Pleistocene to Holocene times, are referred to the Epipaleolithic, a term originally suggested by Obermaier as a synonym for Mesolithic" — [INFLIBNET/eacharya, epgp course material](https://epgp.inflibnet.ac.in/epgpdata/uploads/epgp_content/S000001AN/P001116/M013243/ET/145761001810ET.pdf).
3. A German-language peer-reference source frames it as: north of the Alps "Mittelsteinzeit (Mesolithikum)" applies from the start of the Holocene (c. 9,600 BC), while further south "Epipaläolithikum" is used and does not coincide with the same chronological window, extending in North Africa (Iberomaurusien) from 20,000 BP to 8,000 BC or later — [German Wikipedia, Epipaläolithikum — locator only](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epipal%C3%A4olithikum).

**Practical conclusion for the dataset (directly answering the double-counting concern):**
- **Do not model a separate global "Epipalaeolithic" node alongside the existing "Mesolithic (Eurasia)" node** — in the region where the dataset's Mesolithic node exists (Eurasia) the same time-window is, in West Asia specifically, called "Epipalaeolithic" by convention. Adding both would describe the same underlying span twice under different labels for overlapping/adjacent territory.
- The one place this reasoning does *not* fully apply is where "Epipalaeolithic" is used for a **longer or differently-bounded span** than the dataset's Mesolithic (10,000–5,000 BC) — e.g., the Levantine Epipalaeolithic starts as early as c. 23,000 BC (see 2c), which is *earlier* than the dataset's Mesolithic start (10,000 BC) and falls inside the dataset's existing Palaeolithic band (3.3 Ma–10,000 BC). This means a Levantine Epipalaeolithic entity, if added, would need to span across the dataset's existing Palaeolithic/Mesolithic boundary, not sit only inside the Mesolithic band.

### 2d. Is "Epipalaeolithic" used in Europe, and how does it relate to Mesolithic there?

**Yes, but as a secondary/minority term, concentrated in southern Europe, and generally used for the earlier part of what is otherwise called the Mesolithic — not as the dominant regional label.**

- "In Europe, Epipalaeolithic is used, though not very often, to refer to the early Mesolithic" — [Wikipedia, Epipalaeolithic — locator only](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epipalaeolithic).
- Peer-reviewed corroboration: "In the south of Europe where Mesolithic occupation followed that of the Palaeolithic, the term 'Epipalaeolithic' (a continuation or culmination of the Palaeolithic) has been widely used and still appears today (cf. Straus, Valdeyron, Pluciennik, Bonsall)... In the north, however, the term Mesolithic highlighted the apparent dynamism and distinctiveness of societies" — [Univ. of Leeds/White Rose eprints, "Mesolithic Europe: glimpses of another world"](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/3523/1/spikinsp1_mesolithic.pdf).
- Iberia specifically: "Depending on the terminology preferred by any particular source, the Mesolithic begins after the Epipaleolithic, or includes it. If the Epipaleolithic is not included in it, the Mesolithic is a relatively brief period in Iberia" — [Wikipedia, Prehistoric Iberia — locator only](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prehistoric_Iberia). This is an important structural point: in the Iberian tradition specifically, some scholars nest "Epipalaeolithic" as a sub-phase *inside* "Mesolithic," while others treat them as sequential, non-overlapping phases — usage is not standardized even within one regional tradition.
- A German-language academic reference gives an explicit numeric boundary for the Alpine/Central European split: north of the Alps, "Mittelsteinzeit (Mesolithikum)" is used from the start of the Holocene, c. 9,600 BC; the "Epipaläolithikum" ends with the beginning of the Neolithic around 6000 BC in the German/Alpine framing, and separately covers North Africa's Iberomaurusien from 20,000 BP to 8,000 BC or later — [German Wikipedia, Epipaläolithikum — locator only](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epipal%C3%A4olithikum).

**Conclusion for 2d:** In Europe, "Epipalaeolithic" is not a separate period from "Mesolithic" — it is an alternative/overlapping label applied inconsistently (sometimes as a synonym, sometimes as an earlier sub-phase, sometimes reserved for southern Europe) for parts of the same broad post-Upper-Palaeolithic, pre-Neolithic span the dataset already covers with "Mesolithic (Eurasia)."

## 3. RECOMMENDATION

### Chalcolithic / Copper Age

**Recommended model: (iii) several separate regional entries — not a global era, not a single "Mesolithic (Eurasia)"-style regionally-qualified single entity.**

Justification:
- Section 1b found a direct peer-reviewed source stating there is "no general agreement about what the Copper Age actually is" across even just European national traditions ([Roberts 2019/2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10963-019-09134-z)), and the sourced regional date ranges in 1a diverge by **thousands of years** (Balkans/Levant/Anatolia/Mesopotamia cluster in the 6th–4th millennia BC; South Asia in the 3rd–1st millennia BC; Iberia/Britain in a narrow 25th–22nd century BC slice). This is a fundamentally different situation from "Mesolithic (Eurasia)," where the regional qualifier ("Eurasia") captures one relatively coherent, single, roughly-contiguous span (10,000–5,000 BC) that simply doesn't extend to other continents — a single qualifier suffices there.
- For the Chalcolithic, one regional qualifier cannot capture the variance: "Chalcolithic (Old World)" would still be misleading because "Old World" spans both the 6th-millennium-BC Balkans and the 1st-millennium-BC South Asian cultures, an eleven-times-longer and non-contiguous span.
- Recommended minimum entity set, each separately dated per section 1a: Chalcolithic (Southeast Europe/Balkans), Chalcolithic (Levant/Southern Levant), Chalcolithic (Anatolia), Chalcolithic (Mesopotamia/Iran), Chalcolithic (South Asia), Chalcolithic/Predynastic (Egypt) — flagged with `n.a.` on the Egypt start date pending direct primary-source confirmation (see 1a caveat).
- Each entity should carry a note, parallel to the existing Mesolithic note, stating which large world regions have **no** Chalcolithic entity at all (sub-Saharan Africa outside limited Niger/Mauritania exceptions, East Asia in the standard sense, the Americas, Australia — see 1d), to prevent a future author assuming universality.
- Do not attempt to also add a single umbrella "Chalcolithic (global)" node — per 1b, this would misrepresent the state of the literature, which is explicit that the category is regionally contingent, not a global stage.

### Epipalaeolithic

**Recommended model: (iv) not modelled as a new global or Old-World node — it double-counts the existing "Mesolithic (Eurasia)" entity for most of its extent — BUT a regionally-scoped "Epipalaeolithic (Levant/Near East)" entity is defensible and useful specifically because part of its span (c. 23,000–10,000 BC) falls inside the dataset's existing Palaeolithic band, not just inside Mesolithic.**

Justification:
- Section 2b found that "Epipalaeolithic" and "Mesolithic" are used by different regional traditions to describe substantially the same kind of period (post-Upper-Palaeolithic, microlithic, pre-agricultural-transition hunter-gatherer adaptations), with the key documented split being geographic convention (Near East/North Africa/southern Europe use "Epipalaeolithic"; northern/western Europe use "Mesolithic") rather than a difference in what is being described. A direct peer-reviewed source states the two culture-groups are named differently "despite the shared microlithic defining characteristic" ([Oxford Academic, Natufian chapter](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/59635/chapter/505050944)).
- Adding a separate global "Epipalaeolithic" entity spanning, say, 20,000–10,000 BC across "Eurasia" alongside the existing "Mesolithic (Eurasia)" (10,000–5,000 BC) would create a genuinely confusing double structure: for the 10,000 BC boundary point specifically, the two entities would appear to be adjacent-but-distinct periods when in fact, in the Levant, they are literally the same continuous cultural sequence under two different names depending on which side of an arbitrary 10,000 BC line and which scholarly tradition is asked.
- However, section 2c shows the Levantine Epipalaeolithic specifically starts as early as c. 23,000 cal BC — thousands of years before the dataset's Mesolithic node begins (10,000 BC) and squarely inside the dataset's existing Palaeolithic band (3.3 Ma–10,000 BC). This is the one place a distinct, narrowly-scoped entity is genuinely informative: **"Epipalaeolithic (Levant/Near East), c. 23,000–10,000 BC"** would tell a user that within the Palaeolithic band, the Levant/Near East has a named, well-studied sub-stage that is NOT visible at all under the current spine, and that this sub-stage's *end* coincides with where "Mesolithic (Eurasia)" begins — i.e., it should be presented explicitly as the regional precursor/renaming that feeds directly into the Mesolithic node at the 10,000 BC seam, not as a parallel global era.
- This entity should explicitly carry the Natufian, Kebaran, and Geometric Kebaran phase dates from section 2c as its internal sub-phases/children, since the dataset already has a standalone Natufian entity that this would then correctly nest as the Late Epipalaeolithic phase.
- Do not add "Epipalaeolithic" for Europe or North Africa as additional separate entities: for Europe it is a minority/alternative label for the same Mesolithic span already in the dataset (2d); for North Africa (Iberomaurusian, Capsian) it describes cultures whose date ranges (23,000–5,400 cal BC, per 2e) already overlap both the Palaeolithic and Mesolithic bands in ways best handled as culture-level entities (as the dataset presumably already handles Natufian) rather than as a new top-level period node.

## Recency flags

- **Balkan/Southeast European Chalcolithic chronology has been substantially revised downward/earlier in the last ~5–10 years** by new AMS dating programs (Varna, Pietrele) — sources explicitly note that "new data from Varna (Bulgaria) and Pietrele (Romania) suggest that start and end of the SE European Chalcolithic have to be dated deeper into the 5th millennium BC" than older schemes assumed: [Academia.edu, "Chronological Modelling of the Chalcolithic Settlement Layers at Tell Yunatsite"](https://www.academia.edu/49153711/Chronological_Modelling_of_the_Chalcolithic_Settlement_Layers_at_Tell_Yunatsite_Southern_Bulgaria).
- **Iranian Chalcolithic (Central Zagros, Dalma period) chronology has been actively re-dated in 2023–2025** with new radiocarbon programs; the most recent (November 2025) study explicitly refines the regional start date to "5200/5100 BCE" versus earlier estimates: [Oxford ORA/Radiocarbon 2025, "Absolute Chronology of the Dalma Period in Northwestern Iran"](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:45a3fcc2-4640-4850-a0f0-cbd8b69aefa0/files/rst74cs87p).
- **The Capsian/Iberomaurusian internal sequence (Typical Capsian preceding Upper Capsian) is described as "currently being challenged"** in a 2024/2025 Oxford paper, meaning the phase ordering — not just the absolute dates — may shift again soon: [Oxford ORA, "On the Validity of the Capsian and Iberomaurusian Entities..."](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:dbfe4054-ccf0-4942-ae06-6f4ced56c27e/files/r41687k297).
- **Belovode's status as the world's earliest securely dated copper smelting site is explicitly flagged by a British Museum archaeologist as provisional**: "Although Belovode now stands as the world's oldest known copper-smelting site, that status probably won't last" — [Science News, "Serbian site may have hosted first copper makers"](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/serbian-site-may-have-hosted-first-copper-makers). Treat the c. 5000 BC Belovode date as current best evidence, not a permanently settled fact.
- **Australian archaeology has moved away from using "Stone Age" terminology at all** in recent decades, which is relevant if the dataset's authoring pass considers applying any three-age-system label to Australia — the professional consensus now explicitly rejects this framing on interpretive as well as technical grounds: [The Conversation, 2025](https://theconversation.com/australian-archaeologists-dropped-the-term-stone-age-decades-ago-and-so-should-you-47275).
