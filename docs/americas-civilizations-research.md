# Pre-Columbian Americas: Naming, Dating, and Sourcing Research

**Purpose:** A rigorously-sourced reference for a historical dataset covering major pre-Columbian American civilizations. Every date below is sourced. Where scholars disagree, the disagreement is reported rather than resolved. Conventions and misconceptions are explicitly flagged.

**House rules applied:** Institutional/academic sources preferred (Britannica, university presses, museums, Smithsonian, peer-reviewed work); Wikipedia used only where no stronger institutional source was found on a point (mainly for the Haudenosaunee dating debate and some naming etymology details, which are unusually well-referenced there); disagreements are reported with named proponents, not averaged; conventions and misconceptions are flagged inline.

---

## PART 1 — NAMING (Priority 1)

### 1. Aztec vs. Mexica

**(a) Endonym:** The people who built the empire centered at Tenochtitlan called themselves **Mexica** (and more specifically "Culhua-Mexica," stressing a genealogical link to the earlier city-state of Colhuacan) according to [Britannica's Aztec entry](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Aztec). Those specifically of the capital city are sometimes distinguished as "Mexica Tenochca" or "Tenochca," per [Smarthistory](https://smarthistory.org/introduction-mexica/). Migration-legend accounts (Crónica Mexicáyotl, Aubin Codex tradition) hold that the god Huitzilopochtli renamed the group from "Azteca" to "Mexitin/Mexica" during their journey from the mythical homeland of Aztlán, discussed at [Wikipedia's Aztlán article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aztl%C3%A1n) — meaning even the "Azteca" self-designation, if it existed at all, is itself embedded in a legendary narrative rather than a plain historical record.

**(b) Common English name — coiner, date, meaning:** "Aztec" was not the term used by 16th-century Spanish chroniclers or the people themselves as a general ethnonym. Its use as a broad category was originated in the modern sense by the Prussian naturalist and historian **Alexander von Humboldt in 1810**, who used it as a collective label for the peoples linked to the Mexica state and the Triple Alliance, per [Wikipedia's Aztecs article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aztecs), corroborated by [Smarthistory](https://smarthistory.org/introduction-mexica/) and [Indigenous Mexico](https://www.indigenousmexico.org/articles/mexica-or-aztec-how-the-mexicas-were-renamed). The term was then popularized to a mass English-reading audience by the American historian **William H. Prescott** in his enormously influential 1843 book *History of the Conquest of Mexico*, per [Smarthistory](https://smarthistory.org/introduction-mexica/) and [FAMSI (John Pohl)](http://www.famsi.org/spanish/research/pohl/pohl_aztec1.html). Literally, "Aztec" (Nahuatl *aztecatl*, plural *aztecah*) means "person/people from Aztlán." The meaning of "Aztlán" itself is not firmly settled — proposed glosses include "place of herons" and "place of whiteness" — and per [Wikipedia's Aztlán article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aztl%C3%A1n) some analysts doubt the word represents ordinary Nahuatl morphology at all, suggesting the toponym may be legendary/etymologically opaque rather than a real, locatable place-name.

**(c) Do descendants/scholars reject the name?** "Aztec" is not typically described as actively rejected the way "Anasazi" or "Tarascan" are; rather, scholarly literature treats it as a loose, retrospective umbrella term that was never an ethnic self-designation, per [Wikipedia's Aztecs article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aztecs). Many contemporary scholars (e.g., in Smarthistory's Mexica overview) now prefer "Mexica" specifically for precision when discussing the people of Tenochtitlan and its allies.

**(d) Modern coinage with no ancient referent?** Partially. "Aztec" as a broad civilizational label is a 19th-century historiographic construct (Humboldt/Prescott), even though the word "Azteca" itself does appear in Mexica migration legends as a term for their remote ancestral group — so it has *some* pre-contact linguistic pedigree, just not as the name the empire-era people used for themselves or their state.

**Aztec Empire vs. Triple Alliance:** The precise political structure — an alliance of the city-states Tenochtitlan, Texcoco, and Tlacopan, formed in 1428 against the Tepanec state of Azcapotzalco — is more accurately called the **Triple Alliance** (Nahuatl *Excan Tlatoloyan*); "Aztec Empire" is the retrospective, popular label for the same polity, per [Britannica's account of empire establishment](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Aztec/Establishment-of-the-Aztec-empire) and the [Wikipedia Aztec Empire article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aztec_Empire). **Convention flag:** treat "Aztec Empire" as the popular/retrospective name and "Triple Alliance" as the more historically precise term for the actual governing arrangement.

### 2. Inca vs. Tawantinsuyu

**(a) Endonym:** The polity's own name for itself was **Tawantinsuyu** (also spelled Tahuantinsuyu), Quechua for "the four regions/parts together" (*tawa* = four, *-ntin* = collective/group suffix, *suyu* = region), referring to the four quarters (Chinchaysuyu, Antisuyu, Qullasuyu, Kuntisuyu) that converged at the capital, Cusco. Sourced to the [Wikipedia Inca Empire article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_Empire), [Britannica's Tahuantinsuyu entry](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tahuantinsuyu), and the [Smithsonian's National Museum of the American Indian](https://americanindian.si.edu/inkaroad/engineering/activity/four-suyus.html).

**(b) Common English name — meaning, and misapplication:** "Inca" (Inka) is properly a **title meaning "ruler" or "lord"** in Quechua, not originally an ethnonym for the whole people or the state — it applied to the ruling class, a group estimated at roughly 15,000–40,000 people governing an empire of perhaps 10 million, per the [Wikipedia Inca Empire article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_Empire). The supreme ruler specifically bore the title **"Sapa Inca"** ("the only Inca"), confirmed in multiple dictionary and reference sources including [Collins Dictionary](https://www.collinsdictionary.com/dictionary/english/inca).

**(c/d) Convention flag:** Modern historiography conventionally calls the whole empire the "Inca Empire," which — strictly — misapplies a ruler's/nobility's title to the entire state and population. This is best understood as an entrenched scholarly and popular convention, not an active controversy comparable to Anasazi or Tarascan; the Inca nobility itself did apparently use "Inca" as a broader in-group/gentilicio term according to the chronicler Garcilaso de la Vega, discussed at [palabraquechua.com](https://palabraquechua.com/inca/), which somewhat softens the "pure misnomer" framing. No organized descendant-community rejection of the term "Inca" comparable to the Anasazi or Tarascan cases was found in the sources consulted.

### 3. Anasazi vs. Ancestral Puebloan

**(a) Endonym:** No true self-designation is recoverable. The [National Park Service's Mesa Verde history](https://www.nps.gov/parkhistory/online_books/smith/chap13.htm) states explicitly that "no one knows what they called themselves." The Hopi's own term for their ancestors is **"Hisatsinom"** ("our ancestors"), an internal Puebloan alternative rather than a recovered ancient endonym.

**(b) Common name — etymology, coiner, meaning:** "Anasazi" is a **Navajo (Diné) word**, not a term used by the ancient people it describes or by their Puebloan descendants. It combines Navajo *anaa'í* ("enemy") and *bizází* ("their ancestors"), most often glossed as "**enemy ancestors**" or "ancient enemies" — per [Wikipedia's Ancestral Puebloans article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancestral_Puebloans), [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/Anasazi?pronunciation&lang=en_us&dir=a&file=anasaz01), and the [Colorado Encyclopedia](https://coloradoencyclopedia.org/article/ancestral-puebloans-four-corners-region). The term entered archaeology via rancher/amateur excavator **Richard Wetherill**, who explored Mesa Verde in 1888–89 alongside Navajo guides/laborers, and was later formalized as the standard archaeological label through **Alfred V. Kidder's 1927 Pecos Classification** system, per the [Indian Pueblo Cultural Center](https://indianpueblo.org/what-does-anasazi-mean-and-why-is-it-controversial/).

**(c) Active rejection:** Yes — Pueblo peoples, including the Hopi Tribe, have actively rejected "Anasazi" since the early 1990s as derogatory and not their own designation. The National Park Service and Mesa Verde formally shifted their official terminology to "Ancestral Puebloan" beginning in that period; see [NPS Mesa Verde history](https://www.nps.gov/parkhistory/online_books/smith/chap13.htm) and a contemporaneous [1997 Seattle Times report](https://archive.seattletimes.com/archive/19971109/2571328/is-anasazi-now-politically-incorrect----hopi-people-others-are-bothered-by-term) quoting Hopi Cultural Preservation Office director Leigh Kuwanwisiwma objecting to the term.

**Genuine internal disagreement on nuance:** [Archaeology Magazine](https://archive.archaeology.org/0607/news/insider.html) documents a live disagreement even within Navajo circles about the term's precise connotation: archaeologist David Breternitz's negative "enemy ancestors" framing is disputed by the Navajo Nation's own language office (Eddie Tso), who has argued the term is comparatively "non-threatening" and closer to "those who came before us." This is a genuine etymological/connotative dispute, not merely an outsider-vs-insider disagreement.

**(d) Modern coinage with no ancient referent:** Yes, unambiguously — the people themselves left no known self-designation, and "Anasazi" originates entirely from a different (Navajo) ethnolinguistic community describing a different, earlier people.

### 4. Iroquois vs. Haudenosaunee

**(a) Endonym:** **Haudenosaunee**, meaning "people of the longhouse" or "those who build the longhouse" (from the Seneca *Hodínöhšö:ni:h*), per the [Wikipedia Haudenosaunee article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haudenosaunee), the [Haudenosaunee Confederacy's own official site](https://www.haudenosauneeconfederacy.com/who-we-are/), and a [Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian educator's guide](https://americanindian.si.edu/sites/1/files/pdf/education/haudenosauneeguide.pdf).

**(b) Common name — competing etymologies (genuine scholarly dispute):** "Iroquois" is a French colonial-era coinage with **no settled etymology** — sources report at least four competing derivations with no consensus:
  - From Huron (Wyandot) *irinakhoiw*, meaning "black snakes" or "real adders" — widely characterized as an insult originating from an enemy nation, per [Britannica Kids](https://kids.britannica.com/students/article/Haudenosaunee/599889) and the [New York State Museum](https://nysm.nysed.gov/mohawk-haudenosaunee-village/haudenosaunee-longhouse).
  - From the Haudenosaunee's own oratorical closing phrase, "hiro kone"/"hiro" + "koué" (roughly "I have spoken" plus a ceremonial cry), per the [Wikipedia Haudenosaunee article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haudenosaunee), citing colonial governor Cadwallader Colden's account.
  - From Basque *hilokoa* ("killer people"), per [tekeli.li's onomastic survey](https://tekeli.li/onomastikon/America/North/Iroquois.html).
  - Horatio Hale's theory tracing it to Huron, cognate with Mohawk *ierokwa* ("they who smoke").

**(c) Pejorative status:** Most sources characterize "Iroquois" as **derogatory in origin**, since it likely derives from the language of the Huron, a rival/enemy people allied with the French. See the [Wikipedia Haudenosaunee article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haudenosaunee) and [Colgate University's library guide](https://libguides.colgate.edu/nativeamerican/Haudenosaunee), which states directly that "Iroquois is a derogatory term." **Flag:** the exact original meaning is not settled scholarship — only that most candidate derivations trace to an outside, likely hostile, source rather than to the people's own language.

**(d)** Not a modern archaeological coinage — it is a genuine early colonial-era exonym, still in wide historical and popular use, but understood by scholars and the nations themselves to be an externally imposed name.

### 5. Olmec — the clearest documented misnomer in the dataset

**(a) Endonym:** No confirmed self-designation survives. A possible later reference is "Tamoanchan," and some modern scholars use the invented technical alternative **"Tenocelome"** ("mouth of the jaguar") to sidestep the misnomer, per the [Wikipedia Olmecs article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olmecs). Neither is a recovered contemporary endonym.

**(b) Origin and meaning of "Olmec":** "Olmec"/"Olmeca" is a **Nahuatl word meaning "rubber people"** (*olli*, "rubber," + *mecatl*, "people"). Critically, it was **originally the Aztec/Mexica name for the Gulf Coast inhabitants who were their own contemporaries in the 15th–16th centuries** — the rubber-producing peoples the Mexica knew, as recorded in Bernardino de Sahagún's 16th-century Florentine Codex. It was **not** a name for, or applied by, the much earlier Formative-period civilization archaeologists now call "Olmec." Sourced to [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Olmec), the [Wikipedia Olmecs article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olmecs), and [Dumbarton Oaks](https://www.doaks.org/resources/olmec-art/introduction).

**(c/d) Confirmed, acknowledged misnomer — the standout case:** The term was borrowed for the ancient archaeological culture by the German-Mexican archaeologist **Hermann Beyer in 1929**, reviewing fieldwork by Frans Blom and Oliver La Farge, who reused the Aztec-era geographic label for a people who had lived in the same *place* roughly **2,000 years earlier**. Dumbarton Oaks' scholarly resource on Olmec art states explicitly: *"the term Olmec is now far more commonly used for the Formative culture (1200–500 BCE) than for its older namesake"* — meaning the field has openly acknowledged that a name for one people (the historical Gulf Coast rubber-traders known to the Aztecs) was repurposed for an unrelated, far older civilization; see [Dumbarton Oaks](https://www.doaks.org/resources/olmec-art/introduction), corroborated by [Times Higher Education](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/books/mystery-of-the-rubber-people/164557.article). This is explicitly **flagged in the scholarly literature itself** as a misapplied name, making it the single best-documented case of acknowledged misnomer among all entities in this dataset. Separately, Alfonso Caso in 1942 gave the civilization the honorific nickname **"La Cultura Madre"** ("Mother Culture") of Mesoamerica — an interpretive label, not an ethnonym, and itself a live scholarly debate about the Olmecs' foundational role versus a "sister cultures" model (not independently verified in this research pass; flagged for further sourcing if needed).

### 6. Toltec — a genuine and unresolved scholarly split

**(a) Endonym:** No securely dated ancient self-designation survives independent of later Aztec-era legendary material. "Toltec" derives from Nahuatl *Tollan* ("Place of Reeds"), and the term itself may describe an idealized cultural status ("urbanite," "cultured person") rather than an ethnic self-name, per [Britannica's Toltec entry](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Toltec).

**(b/c) Historicist vs. skeptical positions — report both, do not adjudicate:**
  - **Historicist position**, tracing to 19th-century scholars Veitia, Orozco y Berra, Brasseur de Bourbourg, and Clavigero, and more recently associated with archaeologist **Richard Diehl**: holds that a real Toltec archaeological horizon exists, centered on the site of Tula, Hidalgo, identifiable through material markers such as Mixteca-Puebla iconography and Tohil Plumbate and Silho/X-Fine Orange ceramics, and that a critical comparative reading can extract real history from the later, legend-encrusted Aztec accounts. Per the [Wikipedia Toltec article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toltec) and the [Oxford Handbook of Mesoamerican Archaeology](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/27948/chapter-abstract/211887484).
  - **Skeptical/"mythologist" position**, originating with **Daniel Garrison Brinton** and revived by scholars since the 1990s: argues that "Toltec" as it appears in Aztec-era sources is largely a mythical or philosophical construct symbolizing the might and sophistication of earlier civilizations in general, not a record of one specific historical empire, and that treating these narratives as literal history is misguided. Per the [Wikipedia Toltec article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toltec).

A closely related and unresolved question is whether Tula, Hidalgo is even *the* "Tollan" of Aztec legend, or whether "Tollan" functioned as a generic honorific applied to multiple great cities across Mesoamerican history (Teotihuacan, Tula, Cholula, Chichén Itzá, even Tenochtitlan itself) — the so-called "single Tollan vs. many Tollans" problem, discussed in ["The Enduring Toltecs"](https://www.academia.edu/32079948/The_Enduring_Toltecs_Journal_of_Archaeological_Method_and_Theory_).

**This dispute is genuinely irreconcilable with current evidence** — it is not a matter of one camp being better-sourced, but of fundamentally different views on how much historical reality can be recovered from a legendary corpus. **Recommendation:** any dataset entry for "Toltec" should explicitly present both positions rather than asserting a single Toltec state with confident dates.

### 7. Taino and Purépecha/Tarascan

**Taino — a modern scholarly coinage:**
**(a)** No 16th-century Spanish source uses the word "Taino" to describe a people or ethnicity.
**(b)** The term was coined in **1836 by Constantine Samuel Rafinesque**, originally to classify a *language*, not an ethnic or political group, per [tainoleadershipsummit.com](https://www.tainoleadershipsummit.com/) and a [paper in Novelty Journals](https://www.noveltyjournals.com/upload/paper/Terms%20and%20Terminology%20Conflicts-1524.pdf). Twentieth-century anthropologists (in a documented "explosive" adoption phase, roughly 1901–1955) then extended it broadly as a cultural/ethnic label across multiple, historically distinct Caribbean island populations that Spanish colonial sources actually described as separate groups.
**(c)** There is a live and unresolved controversy: one revisionist position argues, per the same Novelty Journals source, that "the tainos never existed as a race, language or tribe" and that the category is a modern academic "fixation" rather than a real historical people — this is a genuine, contested minority position, not a settled finding, and should be flagged as such rather than adopted or dismissed.
**(d)** Yes — "Taino" is confirmed as a 19th-century scholarly coinage with no attested pre-modern use as a group self-designation.

**Purépecha/Tarascan:**
**(a)** The endonym is **Purépecha** (also P'urhépecha), from *p'urhé-* ("to stroll/walk") plus a nominal suffix, and per the [Wikipedia Purépecha Empire article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pur%C3%A9pecha_Empire) may historically have designated commoners specifically rather than the elite or the whole ethnic group — itself an internal complication worth flagging. The polity's actual state name was **"Irechecua Tzintzuntzani"** ("Kingdom of Tzintzuntzan").
**(b)** "Tarascan"/"Tarasco" is a confirmed **Spanish exonym**, from Purépecha *tarascue*/*tarhaskwa*, meaning "father-in-law" or "son-in-law," per the [Wikipedia Tarascan article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarascan) and [Dictionary.com](https://www.dictionary.com/browse/tarascan). Multiple competing origin stories exist: one holds that natives offered daughters in marriage to Spaniards and addressed them with in-law kinship terms, which then stuck as an ethnonym; a second, sourced to [Indigenous Mexico](https://www.indigenousmexico.org/articles/michoacan-from-kingdom-to-colony-to-sovereign-state-1324-2015) citing the friar Martín de Coruña and Professor Verástique, holds the term was originally used **mockingly** by natives toward Spaniards who habitually violated native women, and was then mistakenly adopted by the Spanish themselves as an ethnic label — a term Verástique describes as carrying "pejorative connotations of loathsomeness and disgust." A third theory (via Sahagún/Relación de Michoacán) derives it from the tutelary deity Taras/Tharas Upeme.
**(c)** Yes — "Tarascan" is explicitly disfavored by modern Purépecha communities and by the Mexican government's own indigenous-affairs body (historically the CDI), which favors "P'urhépecha," per the [French Wikipedia P'urhépechas article](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/P'urh%C3%A9pechas).
**(d)** Not an archaeological coinage; it is a genuine Spanish colonial exonym with a probably pejorative origin, still in wide historical use despite disfavor.

## PART 2 — DATES AND SOURCES (Priority 2)

For each entity: best-supported range, primary citations, and any dispute or convention flag.

### Mesoamerica

**Olmec:** **c. 1200–400 BCE** is the Britannica-consistent range, per [Britannica's Olmec entry](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Olmec) — the earliest major site, San Lorenzo, dates to roughly 1150 BCE per [Britannica's San Lorenzo entry](https://www.britannica.com/place/San-Lorenzo-ancient-city-Mexico), and La Venta flourished c. 900–400 BCE. **Minor variance flag:** some sources push the start earlier, to 1500 BCE ([Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org/humanities/world-history/world-history-beginnings/ancient-americas/a/the-olmec-article), [EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/agriculture-and-agribusiness/olmecs)) based on early ritual deposits — this is a difference in periodization convention (when does "Olmec" begin as a cultural label), not a live scholarly fight.

**Zapotec:** Broad civilizational range **c. 700 BCE – 1521 CE**, per the [Wikipedia Zapotec civilization article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zapotec_civilization). Monte Albán, the Zapotec capital, was founded/rose to prominence around 500 BCE per the [World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/timeline/Zapotec_Civilization/), with its Classic-period floruit dated to roughly 300–900 CE per [Britannica's Monte Albán entry](https://www.britannica.com/place/Monte-Alban) (the same source notes initial construction possibly as early as the 8th century BCE). The Zapotec called themselves **"Be'ena'a"** ("The People," sometimes rendered "people who came from the clouds"), per the [Wikipedia Zapotec civilization article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zapotec_civilization) — an endonym worth noting even though Zapotec naming was not in the Priority 1 list.

**Teotihuacan:** **c. 100 BCE – 550 CE** is the widely cited range for the city's monumental florescence, per [Britannica's Teotihuacán entry](https://www.britannica.com/place/Teotihuac%C3%A1n) and the detailed ceramic-phase chronology of the [PPC Teotihuacan Archaeological Project](https://ppcteotihuacan.org/en/teotihuacan-in-numbers/). The area was settled earlier, by roughly 400 BCE, per Britannica. **Dispute/variance flag:** sources differ on the exact date of the city's collapse/burning — Britannica gives c. 550 CE while other sources cite 600 or even 750 CE (e.g., the [World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/timeline/Teotihuacan/)); this reflects genuine ongoing uncertainty about the collapse's precise timing and cause, not merely rounding.

**Toltec:** Conventional dates place the Tula-centered horizon at roughly **900–1150 CE**, but per the naming discussion above, whether this describes a coherent historical empire or a partly retrospective legendary construct is itself disputed among historicist (Diehl et al.) and skeptical (Brinton-tradition) scholars — see [Wikipedia's Toltec article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toltec). **This date range should be flagged as attached to a contested entity, not just a contested date.**

**Maya (overall) and periods:**
- Preclassic start varies by source: 1000 BCE per the [Wikipedia History of the Maya civilization article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Maya_civilization), 1500 BCE per [Britannica's Maya entry](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Maya-people), or as early as 2000 BCE per some specialist sources (e.g., [Dr. Diane Davies, Maya Archaeologist](https://www.mayaarchaeologist.co.uk/public-resources/maya-world/maya-history/)). **Flag:** this is a genuine periodization-convention variance across otherwise reputable sources, not a settled single start date — a dataset should not present "2000 BCE" as uncontested.
- **Classic period: 250–900 CE** is well-supported and broadly consistent across [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Maya-people), [Smarthistory](https://smarthistory.org/maya-intro/), and the [World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/timeline/Maya_Civilization/).
- **Postclassic period end date varies significantly by what "end" means:** 1519 (first Spanish contact, per [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/pre-Columbian-civilizations/The-collapse-of-Classic-Maya-civilization)), 1521 (fall of Tenochtitlan/Aztec Empire, sometimes used as a rough Mesoamerica-wide marker), or **1697** — the date of the **fall of Nojpetén, capital of the independent Itza Maya kingdom of Petén Itzá**, the last independent, unconquered Maya (and indeed the last independent unconquered native) polity in the Americas. Confirmed by multiple independent sources: [Wikipedia's Spanish conquest of the Maya article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_conquest_of_the_Maya), the [Wikipedia Nojpetén article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nojpet%C3%A9n), the [Wikipedia Petén Itzá kingdom article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peten_Itza_kingdom), and Stanford University Press's description of Grant Jones's monograph, ["The Conquest of the Last Maya Kingdom"](https://www.sup.org/books/history/conquest-last-maya-kingdom). The assault occurred on **March 13, 1697**, led by Martín de Ursúa y Arizmendi. **This confirms 1697 as a defensible, well-documented endpoint for "Maya" in the political-independence sense — it is not an error, but it should be labeled explicitly as marking the end of independent Maya political sovereignty, not the end of Maya civilization or people, who of course continue to exist today.**

**Aztec Empire / Triple Alliance:** **1428–1521 CE.** The Triple Alliance was formed in 1428 under the Mexica ruler Itzcóatl, allying Tenochtitlan, Texcoco, and Tlacopan against the Tepanec state of Azcapotzalco; the empire ended with the Spanish conquest, culminating in the surrender of the last Mexica ruler, Cuauhtémoc, on August 13, 1521. Consistently dated across [Britannica's account of empire establishment](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Aztec/Establishment-of-the-Aztec-empire), the [Wikipedia Aztec Empire article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aztec_Empire), and [Britannica's Aztec Empire timeline](https://www.britannica.com/summary/Aztec-Empire-Timeline). **Distinct from city founding:** Tenochtitlan itself was founded significantly earlier, in **1325 CE** (traditionally, and symbolically, dated to March 13, 1325 — a date fixed retrospectively in 1925 for the city's 600th anniversary rather than attested in a contemporary record), per [Britannica's Tenochtitlan entry](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tenochtitlan) and the [Wikipedia Tenochtitlan article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenochtitlan). The dataset's 1428–1521 range correctly reflects the *political entity* (Triple Alliance/empire), not the city's founding — this distinction matters for the naming discussion above, since "Aztec" as commonly used often conflates city, people, and empire.

**Purépecha/Tarascan:** **c. 1300–1530 CE** is well supported. The polity was founded by the ruler Tariácuri, who established a capital at Pátzcuaro around 1300 (some sources say 1325 — a minor variance), per the [Wikipedia Purépecha Empire article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pur%C3%A9pecha_Empire) and the [World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/Tarascan_Civilization/). It ended with the execution of the last king, Tzintzicha Tangáxoan II, by Nuño de Guzmán on **February 14, 1530**, per [lineasdeltiempo.com](https://www.lineasdeltiempo.com/p/imperio-purepecha-tarascos.html) — though some sources place loss of effective independence earlier, around 1521–1522, with 1530 marking final destruction rather than initial subjugation.

**Tenochtitlan (city, Priority 3 addition):** Founded **1325 CE** (traditional date), destroyed by Spanish forces under Hernán Cortés on **August 13, 1521**, per [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tenochtitlan) and the [Wikipedia Tenochtitlan article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenochtitlan). At its height in 1519, population is estimated at roughly 400,000 — the largest residential concentration in Mesoamerican history, per Britannica.

**Monte Albán (Priority 3 addition):** Zapotec capital; founded/rose to regional prominence c. 500 BCE, Classic-period floruit c. 300–900 CE, per [Britannica's Monte Albán entry](https://www.britannica.com/place/Monte-Alban) and the [World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/timeline/Zapotec_Civilization/).

**Tikal (Priority 3 addition):** Major Classic Maya city; flourished roughly **600–900 CE**, reaching its architectural and artistic peak in that window, with an estimated core population of 10,000 (outlying population up to 50,000) around 700 CE. The last dated inscription is from 889 CE, though small populations lingered afterward. Per [Britannica Kids' Tikal entry](https://kids.britannica.com/students/article/Tikal/338459).

### Andes / South America

**Norte Chico/Caral:** **c. 3000–1800 BCE** (Late Archaic period) is exceptionally well-supported by peer-reviewed radiocarbon dating — notably [Haas, Creamer, and Ruiz's 2004 paper in *Nature*](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature03146), presenting 95 radiocarbon dates confirming this range, and a related [2006 paper in *Current Anthropology*](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/506281). Caral specifically has been dated to roughly **2627 BCE** using shicra-bag radiocarbon dating in research led by Ruth Shady (published in *Science*, 2001), discussed at [CountryReports.org](https://www.countryreports.org/articles/caral-supe) and [History.com](https://www.history.com/articles/caral-peru-norte-chico-oldest-civilization-western-hemisphere), which notes this makes Caral roughly contemporary with Egypt's Step Pyramid. **This is one of the best-sourced, least-disputed ranges in the entire dataset.**

**Chavín:** **c. 900–200 BCE** per [Britannica's Chavín entry](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Chavin) and [Britannica's Chavín de Huántar entry](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chavin-de-Huantar). The Old Temple was built roughly 900–750 BCE, the New Temple around 500 BCE, per [Smarthistory](https://smarthistory.org/chavin-de-huantar/) and the [World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/timeline/Chavin_Civilization/). **Minor variance, not a real dispute:** Wikipedia gives a broader site-occupation span (before 1200 BCE to roughly 400–500 BCE) that measures physical occupation of the site rather than the period of cultural florescence that Britannica's 900–200 BCE range describes — different measurement definitions, not disagreement about facts.

**Nazca:** Most-cited range is **c. 100 BCE – 800 CE**, per the [Wikipedia Nazca culture article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazca_culture) and [CountryReports.org](https://www.countryreports.org/articles/the-nazca-lines). **Genuine source variance on the end date:** Britannica gives **c. 200 BCE – 600 CE** for the culture proper, per [Britannica's Nazca entry](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Nazca); the British Museum gives 200 BCE–650 CE for the "Nasca Lines" tradition specifically, per the [British Museum's Central Andes timeline](https://www.britishmuseum.org/exhibitions/peru-journey-time/timeline-central-andes-south-america); a University of Massachusetts specialist overview by Donald Proulx gives 100 BCE–650 CE, per [people.umass.edu](https://people.umass.edu/~proulx/online_pubs/Nasca_Overview_Zurich.pdf). The end date genuinely ranges across sources from roughly 500 to 800 CE depending on which decline phase is counted as the culture's true end — this should be flagged as unresolved, not averaged.

**Moche:** **c. 100–800 CE** is the most commonly cited range, per [Britannica's Moche entry](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Moche) (1st to 8th century CE) and the [World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/timeline/Moche_Civilization/). Internal phase chronology (Moche I–V) is well-documented, e.g. at [HistoryTools.org](https://www.historytools.org/stories/the-moche-civilization-of-ancient-peru-artisans-warriors-and-priests-of-the-northern-coast). **Genuine dispute on the collapse date:** some sources place the Moche collapse around 550–600 CE (French Wikipedia notes many historians formerly placed the end around 600 CE, but that more precise excavation now points to c. 700 CE, per [French Wikipedia's Moche article](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moche_(culture))); Encyclopedia.com places societal collapse between 600 and 750 CE, tied to the onset of the Middle Horizon (540–900 CE), per [Encyclopedia.com's Moche entry](https://www.encyclopedia.com/humanities/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/moche). A 2024 peer-reviewed Bayesian chronological reassessment exists in [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1040618224001642), signaling this remains an active area of chronometric revision.

**Tiwanaku:** Occupation and influence are generally dated **c. 500–1000 CE** at peak, per the [World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/timeline/Tiwanaku/) and [Live Science](https://www.livescience.com/26792-tiwanaku.html). However, Britannica gives a notably wider range, noting some scholars date the earliest remains to the Early Intermediate Period (c. 200 BCE–200 CE) while others suggest evidence in artifacts from the 2nd millennium BCE, per [Britannica's Tiwanaku entry](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tiwanaku). The [Wikipedia Tiwanaku polity article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiwanaku_polity) states the site itself was founded around 110 CE, with the state proper lasting c. 600–1000 CE (sometimes extended to 1150 CE if raised-field agricultural use, rather than urban occupation, is the measure). Encyclopedia.com states the site was the dominant Titicaca Basin society from "at least 100 CE to approximately 1200 CE," per [Encyclopedia.com's Tiwanaku entry](https://www.encyclopedia.com/humanities/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/tiwanaku). **Flag:** dates vary substantially (100–1200 CE at the widest) depending on whether "founding," "monumental peak," or "final abandonment of any activity" is being measured — a dataset entry should specify which definition it is using.

**Wari (Huari):** **c. 600–1000 CE** is the most Britannica/EBSCO-consistent range for the empire proper, per [Britannica's Huari entry](https://www.britannica.com/place/Huari) and the [Tiffiny Tung/Oxford Companion to Archaeology chapter](https://tiffinytung.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/2012_tung_wariempire_oxfordcompaniontoarchaeology.pdf). **Variance flag:** the [World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/Wari_Civilization/) extends the start to c. 450 CE; the site of Wari itself may have been founded as early as 400 CE and gained prominence by 550 CE per the [Wikipedia Wari archaeological site article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wari_(archaeological_site)); collapse dates range from c. 1000 to as late as 1100–1200 CE across sources (compare the [Wikipedia Wari Empire article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wari_Empire), giving 1100 CE, against the [Spanish Wikipedia Imperio wari article](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperio_wari), giving 1200 CE for final dissolution). A 2016 *Science* news piece on skeletal evidence documents the violent, drought-linked nature of the roughly-1000-CE collapse, per [Science.org](https://www.science.org/content/article/waris-grisly-end-fall-south-american-empire).

**Chimú:** **c. 900–1470 CE** is the most consistent range across sources, per [Britannica's Chimú entry](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Chimu) (which dates the Chan Chan-based kingdom to c. 1100–1470 CE, a narrower "kingdom" framing than the 900 CE "culture" framing used elsewhere), the [Wikipedia Chan Chan article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chan_Chan), and [EBSCO's Chimú culture entry](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/social-sciences-and-humanities/chimu-culture). The Chimú state (Chimor) was conquered by the Inca under Túpac Inca Yupanqui around **1470 CE**, per the [World History Encyclopedia's Chan Chan entry](https://www.worldhistory.org/timeline/Chan_Chan/) and [Lumen Learning](https://courses.lumenlearning.com/suny-hccc-worldcivilization/chapter/chimu/). **Flag:** Britannica's narrower 1100–1470 CE range for the "kingdom" (as opposed to 900–1470 CE for the broader culture) reflects a real distinction between when Chimú material culture emerges and when a centralized Chimor state existed — worth preserving as a distinction rather than collapsing into one number.

**Inca Empire:** **1438–1533 CE** is very well and consistently supported: the earliest date confidently assignable to Inca dynastic/imperial history is **1438 CE**, when **Pachacuti Inca Yupanqui** seized the throne and began rapid territorial expansion, per [Britannica's Inca history entry](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Inca/History) and [Britannica's Pachacuti biography](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Pachacuti-Inca-Yupanqui). The empire effectively ended with the Spanish capture of Cusco in November 1533, following the execution of Atahualpa in August 1533, per the same Britannica source. **Important caveat, not a dispute but a naming/scope issue:** the *Kingdom of Cusco* (a smaller predecessor polity, not yet an empire) is traditionally said to have been founded far earlier — legendarily by Manco Cápac in the early 1200s CE, per the [Wikipedia History of the Incas article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Incas) — so 1438 marks the start of *imperial* expansion under Pachacuti, not the origin of Inca rulership or the Cusco polity itself. A final Inca rump state (the Neo-Inca State at Vilcabamba) persisted until 1572, when it too fell to the Spanish, per [Britannica Kids' Inca entry](https://kids.britannica.com/students/article/Inca/275040) — worth flagging as a coda beyond the conventional 1533 end date.

**Machu Picchu (Priority 3 addition):** Widely attributed to the reign of Pachacuti, with construction conventionally placed around **1450 CE** (with continued building activity possibly extending into the reign of his successor Túpac Inca Yupanqui, 1472–1493), per [Britannica's Machu Picchu entry](https://www.britannica.com/place/Machu-Picchu). **Notable revision:** a 2021 radiocarbon study led by Richard Burger (Yale University), using AMS dating of human remains, concluded occupation actually ran from **c. 1420 to 1530 CE** — somewhat earlier than the traditional Pachacuti-reign-based estimate — per the [Wikipedia Machu Picchu article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machu_Picchu), which is itself citing the peer-reviewed Burger study. This is a good example of radiocarbon science revising a chronology that had previously rested mainly on inferred royal-reign dates. UNESCO World Heritage listing came in 1983.

**Qhapaq Ñan / Inca road system (Priority 3 addition):** UNESCO's official inscription (2014, as a transnational cultural route across Argentina, Bolivia, Chile, Colombia, Ecuador, and Peru) describes the network as covering **"more than 30,000 kilometres,"** per the [UNESCO World Heritage Qhapaq Ñan listing](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1459/). **Total length estimates vary substantially** across sources, from about 23,000 km to 40,000 km to as much as 60,000 km depending on what is counted as part of the network and how much predates the Inca themselves (parts of the system incorporated pre-Inca Andean roads built over more than 2,000 years), per the [Wikipedia Inca road system article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_road_system) and [UNESCO's own site page](https://whc.unesco.org/en/qhapaqnan/), which separately notes the network "connected various production, administrative and ceremonial centres constructed over more than 2,000 years of pre-Inca Andean culture" — meaning the road system itself is not solely an Inca achievement, but substantially an accumulation and integration of earlier Andean infrastructure.

### North America

**Mississippian culture:** Most sources give **c. 800–1600 CE**, e.g., [HistoryAndCulture.org](https://www.historyandculture.org/historic-timelines/pre-columbian-era-timeline/mississippian-culture) and [StudentsOfHistory.com](https://www.studentsofhistory.com/mississippian-culture). Britannica gives a slightly earlier start, "about 700 CE," extending "to the time of arrival of the first European explorers," per [Britannica's Mississippian culture entry](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Mississippian-culture); Britannica Kids gives an end point as late as "about 1700," per [Britannica Kids' Mississippian Culture entry](https://kids.britannica.com/kids/article/Mississippian-Culture/399546). **Flag:** the end date is inherently fuzzy because Mississippian societies did not end at a single conquest event the way the Aztec or Inca empires did — they gradually transformed and declined at different times regionally, so any single end-year is a convention, not a discrete historical event.

**Cahokia (Priority 3 addition, and distinct from Mississippian broadly):** Britannica states Cahokia was **first occupied in 700 CE and flourished for approximately four centuries (c. 950–1350 CE)**, reaching a peak population of **as many as 20,000** — the most extensive urban center in prehistoric North America north of Mexico and the primary center of Middle Mississippian culture, per [Britannica's Cahokia Mounds entry](https://www.britannica.com/place/Cahokia-Mounds). Population estimates vary widely across sources — Britannica itself separately notes a scholarly range "from a peak of 8,000 to 40,000," and the [Wikipedia Cahokia article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cahokia) cites a range of 6,000 to 40,000. The apex of the city's growth is often placed around 1050–1100 CE, per [Live Science](https://www.livescience.com/22737-cahokia.html). Site abandonment by roughly 1350 CE is consistent across sources. **This is a genuinely major omission if not already a distinct entry** — Cahokia was the largest pre-Columbian settlement north of Mexico and merits its own entity, not just inclusion under "Mississippian" generally.

**Ancestral Puebloan (Anasazi):** **c. 100–1600 CE** per [Britannica's Ancestral Pueblo culture entry](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Ancestral-Pueblo-culture), which divides the span into six developmental (Pecos Classification) periods: Late Basketmaker II (100–500 CE), Basketmaker III (500–750 CE), Pueblo I (750–950 CE), Pueblo II (950–1150 CE), Pueblo III (1150–1300 CE), and Pueblo IV (1300–1600 CE). **Flag — internal sub-period boundaries vary noticeably by source**, even though the overall 100–1600 CE envelope is broadly stable: [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/dictionaries-thesauruses-pictures-and-press-releases/ancestral-pueblo-anasazi) gives Pueblo I as 700–900 CE (vs. Britannica's 750–950 CE) and Pueblo IV ending at 1500 CE (vs. Britannica's 1600 CE); the [Wikipedia Archaic–Early Basketmaker period article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaic%E2%80%93Early_Basketmaker_period) uses yet another set of boundaries (e.g., Pueblo I 750–900, Pueblo II 900–1150, Pueblo III 1150–1350, Pueblo IV 1350–1600). This reflects real regional and disciplinary variation in where archaeologists draw period boundaries — not a factual dispute about events, but a genuine convention/definitional inconsistency worth flagging for anyone trying to pin down single-year boundaries.

**Chaco Canyon (Priority 3 addition):** Britannica dates Chaco as an administrative, economic, and ceremonial center of the Ancestral Pueblo people from **850 to 1250 CE**, per [Britannica's Chaco Culture National Historical Park entry](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chaco-Culture-National-Historical-Park). UNESCO's own listing gives a tighter window, describing Chacoan society as reaching its height **"between about 1020 and 1110"** within a broader 9th-to-13th-century span, per the [UNESCO World Heritage Chaco Culture listing](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/353/). The National Park Service's own site materials describe "the cultural flowering of the Chacoan people" as beginning in the mid-800s and lasting "more than 300 years," per the [NPS Chaco Culture history page](https://www.nps.gov/chcu/learn/historyculture/index.htm). Pueblo Bonito, the largest and most-studied Great House, was built in stages roughly between 850 and 1150 CE, per the [Wikipedia Pueblo Bonito article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pueblo_Bonito). Abandonment is generally linked to a severe multi-decade drought beginning around 1130 CE, per the [Wikipedia Chaco Culture National Historical Park article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaco_Culture_National_Historical_Park).

**Mesa Verde (Priority 3 addition):** Ancestral Puebloan (Basketmaker) settlement in the Mesa Verde area began around **550 CE**, per [Britannica's Mesa Verde National Park entry](https://www.britannica.com/place/Mesa-Verde-National-Park) and the [National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/articles/mesaverde.htm). The famous cliff dwellings themselves (e.g., Cliff Palace) were built comparatively late in the occupation sequence, mostly between roughly **1150/1190 and 1280/1300 CE**, per Britannica and NPS visitor materials ([NPS Cliff Dwelling Tours page](https://www.nps.gov/meve/planyourvisit/cliff_dwelling_tours.htm)). The region was substantially depopulated by around 1300 CE, following the severe "Great Drought" of 1276–1299 CE, per Britannica. Peak population is estimated at roughly 5,000, per Britannica.

**Haudenosaunee Confederacy — founding date is a live, well-documented, and genuinely unresolved scholarly dispute.** This deserves to be treated as a headline case of scholarly disagreement rather than resolved to a single date:

- **Mainstream/archaeological-consensus position:** Most academic historians (per the [Wikipedia Great Peacemaker article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Peacemaker), citing archaeologist Dean Snow's work) place the Confederacy's founding in a range from roughly **1450 to 1600 CE**, often anchored to a documented solar eclipse of **June 28, 1451**, which is treated as a plausible match to oral accounts of a "sign in the sky" at the founding council. In 1982, Dean Snow argued that mainstream archaeological evidence did not support any founding date connected to an eclipse earlier than 1350 CE, explicitly ruling out 1142 CE on archaeological grounds, per the same Wikipedia source. The [WorldAtlas overview](https://www.worldatlas.com/politics/iroquois-great-law-of-peace-and-its-influence-on-the-united-states-constitution.html) confirms "there is no scholarly consensus on the date," with 1450–1650 as the commonly cited academic range and 1451 as a frequent point of reference.
- **Minority/revisionist position (Mann and Fields, 1997):** Historian **Barbara A. Mann** (University of Toledo) and astronomer **Jerry L. Fields** published *"A Sign in the Sky: Dating the League of the Haudenosaunee"* in the *American Indian Culture and Research Journal* (21:2, 1997, pp. 105–163), archived at [eScholarship.org](https://escholarship.org/content/qt27g1b5px/qt27g1b5px.pdf?v=lg) and summarized at [EBSCO Research Starters](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/iroquois-confederacy-established). They argue that Haudenosaunee oral tradition describing a "sign in the sky" (a "Black Sun") at the ratification council held at Ganondagan (near modern Victor, New York) refers to a **total solar eclipse**, and that combining astronomical eclipse-path calculations with oral tradition and documentary sources points to a single candidate date: **August 31 (some sources say August 22), 1142 CE** — over three centuries earlier than the mainstream 1450s estimate. This is affirmed in a 2024 [CBC News report](https://www.cbc.ca/news/indigenous/solar-eclipse-haudenosaunee-confederacy-1.7163865) timed to a solar eclipse, in [EarthSky's astronomy coverage](https://earthsky.org/human-world/solar-eclipse-marks-beginning-of-iroquois-confederacy/), and in a [Smithsonian Air and Space Museum piece](https://airandspace.si.edu/editorial/airspaces8ep12).
- **Direct scholarly rebuttal exists:** A dedicated critical response, ["Can a Myth Be Astronomically Dated?"](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/0f04m96b) (UC eScholarship, 1999), argues explicitly that the Mann and Fields methodology and its precision claims are not adequately supported, and that there is no serious evidence the League was *not* founded on some other date either — i.e., the astronomical argument is not as dispositive as its proponents present it.
- **Wide range of other historical estimates exist beyond these two poles:** Lewis H. Morgan (19th-century ethnologist) proposed 1459 CE; some sources describe estimates as early as the late 11th century via independent oral-history triangulation (per a [Reddit AskHistorians thread from a specialist contributor](https://www.reddit.com/r/AskHistorians/comments/hfdl11/the_founding_of_the_iroquois_confederacy_is/), noting four candidate total/near-total eclipses at Ganondagan — 1142, 1390, 1451, and 1536 — with 1142 and 1390 considered the best fits to the oral tradition's other details); Wikipedia's own summary states the confederacy was "likely formed between 1142 and 1660, but there is little agreement on the date," per the [Wikipedia Haudenosaunee article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haudenosaunee).
- **Recommendation for the dataset:** Present this as an active range of **1142–1600 CE**, explicitly naming the 1142 date (Mann and Fields, based on oral tradition plus the August 1142 eclipse) against the more conventional 1450–1600 CE range (Dean Snow and the mainstream archaeological/anthropological position, often anchored to the 1451 eclipse), and note that a scholarly rebuttal of the 1142 dating methodology exists. Do not present either as settled.

**Poverty Point culture (Priority 3 addition):** A major, distinctly early North American earthwork-building culture in present-day Louisiana, dated to roughly **1700–1100 BCE** by Britannica, describing a settlement of 4,000–5,000 people, per [Britannica's Poverty Point National Monument entry](https://www.britannica.com/place/Poverty-Point-National-Monument); the World History Encyclopedia gives the same 1700–1100 BCE range, per [WorldHistory.org's Poverty Point entry](https://www.worldhistory.org/Poverty_Point/). This predates the Mississippian culture by roughly two millennia and predates Olmec-era Mesoamerican civilization — it is a genuinely major omission for a North American pre-Columbian dataset, being one of the earliest known large-scale earthwork/mound-building complexes in the Americas.

## PART 3 — MAJOR MISSING ENTITIES (Priority 3): Summary Recommendation

The following entities are documented above with dates and citations and are recommended for inclusion if not already present, given their historical significance relative to the thinness of North/Meso/South American coverage otherwise:

1. **Cahokia** — largest pre-Columbian city north of Mexico; c. 700/950–1350 CE; population estimates ranging 6,000–40,000 depending on source. See [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Cahokia-Mounds).
2. **Chaco Canyon** — major Ancestral Puebloan ceremonial/administrative/trade center; c. 850–1250 CE, height c. 1020–1110 CE. See [UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/353/).
3. **Mesa Verde** — Ancestral Puebloan settlement and cliff-dwelling complex; occupation c. 550–1300 CE, cliff dwellings themselves c. 1150/1190–1280/1300 CE. See [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Mesa-Verde-National-Park).
4. **Machu Picchu** — Inca royal estate; conventionally c. 1450 CE (Pachacuti), revised by 2021 radiocarbon study to c. 1420–1530 CE occupation. See [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Machu-Picchu) and the [2021 Yale-led study as reported on Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machu_Picchu).
5. **Tenochtitlan** — Aztec/Mexica capital, distinct from the empire itself; founded 1325 CE, destroyed 1521 CE. See [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tenochtitlan).
6. **Monte Albán** — Zapotec capital; founded/rose c. 500 BCE, Classic floruit c. 300–900 CE. See [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Monte-Alban).
7. **Tikal** — major Classic Maya city; floruit c. 600–900 CE, peak population c. 700 CE. See [Britannica Kids](https://kids.britannica.com/students/article/Tikal/338459).
8. **Poverty Point culture** — early Louisiana earthwork complex; c. 1700–1100 BCE, among the oldest known large-scale mound sites in the Americas. See [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Poverty-Point-National-Monument).
9. **The Inca road system (Qhapaq Ñan)** — transnational Inca/pre-Inca infrastructure network, c. 23,000–40,000+ km depending on source, integrating over 2,000 years of pre-Inca Andean road-building; UNESCO-listed 2014. See [UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1459/).
10. **The Columbian Exchange / epidemic depopulation** — Not a civilization but an essential cross-cutting context for every post-1492 end date in this dataset. The term "Columbian Exchange" was coined by historian **Alfred W. Crosby** in his 1972 book of the same name, per [Britannica Kids](https://kids.britannica.com/students/article/Columbian-Exchange/632098) and the [Smithsonian Magazine interview with Crosby](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/alfred-w-crosby-on-the-columbian-exchange-98116477/). Pre-contact population estimates for the Americas are themselves a major, unresolved scholarly dispute, ranging from roughly **8 million to over 100 million** depending on methodology, with modern estimates converging somewhat around 45–60 million, per the [Wikipedia Population history of the Indigenous peoples of the Americas article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_history_of_the_Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Americas), which itself cites the competing figures of Alfred Kroeber (low, pre-1960s), Henry Dobyns (high, 1966 estimate of 90–112 million based on assuming ~95% epidemic mortality), and William Denevan (a middle estimate of c. 55 million in 1976). Estimates of overall depopulation from disease (plus war, enslavement, and social disruption) in the century-plus following 1492 commonly range from **75% to 95%**, per [Oxford Research Encyclopedia of American History](https://oxfordre.com/americanhistory/view/10.1093/acrefore/9780199329175.001.0001/acrefore-9780199329175-e-3?print) and the [Wikipedia Indigenous peoples of the Americas article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Americas) — **this is a genuinely disputed range, not a settled figure, and should never be presented as a single precise percentage.** This context is essential background for interpreting nearly every "end date" in the dataset (Aztec 1521, Inca 1533, Maya 1697, etc.) since these political conquest dates dramatically understate the timeline and scale of the underlying demographic collapse, which continued for a century or more after each political conquest.

## Overall Recommendations for Dataset Authors

1. **Do not present single "winning" dates where genuine scholarly disagreement exists.** The clearest cases requiring dual/ranged presentation rather than a single number: the Haudenosaunee founding (1142 vs. 1450s), the reality and scope of a "Toltec" polity (historicist vs. skeptical positions), the Nazca/Moche/Wari/Tiwanaku end dates (each varying by 100–200+ years across reputable sources), and pre-Columbian American population figures underlying any Columbian Exchange framing.
2. **Flag conventions explicitly, especially:** the 1428 Aztec Empire start date (marks the Triple Alliance, not Tenochtitlan's 1325 founding); the 1438 Inca Empire start date (marks Pachacuti's imperial expansion, not the much older legendary Kingdom of Cusco); Maya period boundaries generally, which are archaeological conveniences rather than events the Maya themselves marked.
3. **Flag misconceptions explicitly, especially:** that "Aztec" was the empire's own name for itself (it was not — "Mexica" was); that "Inca" properly names the people/state rather than the ruler; that "Olmec" was the ancient civilization's own name or contemporary term (it is a borrowed, chronologically displaced Aztec-era label for a different, later people, applied retrospectively by 20th-century archaeologists); that "Anasazi" is a neutral archaeological term (it is a Navajo-language exonym rejected by descendant Pueblo communities); that "Taino" is an attested indigenous ethnonym (it is an 1836 scholarly coinage originally for a language).
4. **The strongest, least-disputed date range in the entire dataset is Norte Chico/Caral (c. 3000–1800 BCE),** underpinned by extensive peer-reviewed radiocarbon work; this can be presented with high confidence and minimal hedging.
5. **No entity researched here rose to the level of "irreconcilable — recommend not authoring."** Even the most contested cases (Haudenosaunee founding date, Toltec historicity) have well-documented, clearly attributable competing positions that can be responsibly presented side by side with named proponents; the material supports authoring a dataset, provided disputes are reported as such rather than resolved.
