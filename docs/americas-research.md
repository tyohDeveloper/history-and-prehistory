## 1. THE PEOPLING DEBATE

### 1.1 White Sands footprints (New Mexico) — dating dispute

**Claim chronology:**

| Round | Date claimed | Method | Material | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2021 original claim | 23,000–21,000 cal years ago | Radiocarbon (AMS) | *Ruppia cirrhosa* (ditchgrass) seeds | Bennett et al. 2021, *Science* 373(6562):1528, [science.org/doi/full/10.1126/science.abg7586](https://www.science.org/doi/full/10.1126/science.abg7586); [NPS/USGS fact sheet](https://www.nps.gov/orgs/1207/whsa_fossil.htm) |
| 2023 independent re-dating | Terrestrial conifer pollen: 23.4±2.5 to 22.6±2.3 ka (statistically indistinguishable from seed ages); OSL on quartz grains: minimum age >21.5±1.9 ka | Radiocarbon on pollen (separated from bulk sediment) + optically stimulated luminescence (OSL) on quartz sand grains | Conifer pollen; quartz grains | Pigati et al. 2023, *Science* 382:73–75, [science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adh5007](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adh5007); summary at [USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/independent-age-estimates-resolve-controversy-ancient-human-footprints-white-sands) |
| 2024 rebuttal | Argues for a "significantly younger chronology" than 21–23 ka | Critique of *Ruppia* δ¹³C values (possible age offset), OSL interpreted as maximum-limiting only (partial bleaching), pollen suspected of being older, redeposited material | — | Rhode, Neudorf, Rachal, Davis, Madsen, Dello-Russo 2024, *Journal of Quaternary Science*, [tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/20555563.2024.2345979](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/20555563.2024.2345979) |
| 2025 further confirmation | 20,700–22,400 years old — a third, independent material type | Radiocarbon on ancient mud, run by an independent lab | Mud | Holliday & Windingstad, *Science Advances*, June 2025; [ScienceDaily summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2025/06/250629033438.htm); [Reuters summary](https://www.reuters.com/science/new-research-strengthens-case-age-ancient-new-mexico-footprints-2025-06-19/) |

**Significance:** If the ~21,000–23,000 cal BP age holds, humans were present in the North American interior well before the opening of the ice-free corridor and well before Clovis — a leading data point for a "pre-Clovis" peopling of the Americas.

**Standing objection that has never been resolved:** no stone tools, hearths, or settlement debris have been found at the White Sands locality — Holliday himself acknowledges this gap remains unanswered even after the 2025 mud dates ([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2025/06/250629033438.htm)).

⚑ **LITERATURE MOVED 2023, reaffirmed 2025** — the claim moved from "disputed" (hard-water-effect concern about aquatic *Ruppia* seeds) to an increasingly robust, three-independent-material-type, two-lab consensus (seeds, pollen, OSL, and now mud), though the 2024 Rhode et al. rebuttal still actively contests the chronology on isotopic and depositional grounds — this is **not fully settled**, and both the confirming and the rebutting papers should be treated as live, competing claims.

### 1.2 Monte Verde, Chile

**Long-accepted date (Monte Verde II):** ~14,500 cal BP (95.4% probability range ~14,200–14,900 cal BP; median ~14,530 cal BP), from Dillehay's 1970s–1980s excavations, corroborated by an international site visit in 1997. Source: [Cambridge/*Antiquity* 2023 reassessment](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/monte-verde-ii-an-assessment-of-new-radiocarbon-dates-and-their-sedimentological-context/CF19BDBDE1ECE700EE59C7BF7CF502FF).

**Monte Verde I** (older, less secure layer): ~18,500–14,500 cal BP per a 2015 PLOS ONE reanalysis (n.a. for direct fetch confirmation in this pass — carried from earlier search snippet only; treat as provisional).

⚑ **LITERATURE MOVED 2026 (major, unresolved, ongoing as of this writing):** Surovell, Latorre, and colleagues argue in a 2026 *Science* paper that the site is only **4,200–8,200 years old** — proposing that the dated organic material (wood) was reworked and redeposited by creek erosion into much younger sediment layers, so the radiocarbon ages reflect the age of the *wood*, not of human occupation. [Science.org: "Debate explodes over age of key South American archaeological site"](https://www.science.org/content/article/debate-explodes-over-age-key-south-american-archaeological-site"). This is a live, explosive, and unresolved controversy — present both positions, do not average them. Notably, the same article reports that genetic models for the peopling of the Americas are **not** dependent on Monte Verde's date: "later dates for Monte Verde won't make any difference for the genetic models."

### 1.3 Collapse of "Clovis First"

❗ **COMMON MISCONCEPTION:** Clovis (~13,050–12,750 cal BP, see §2.1) was long taught as the first culture in the Americas, ancestral to everything after it. Multiple independently dated sites older than or contemporaneous with Clovis — Monte Verde, Cooper's Ferry, Paisley Caves, and the Western Stemmed Tradition generally — collapsed this model over the 2000s–2020s.

### 1.4 Beringian Standstill hypothesis

Genetic-only estimates of the duration and timing disagree and remain unresolved:

- Tamm et al. 2007 (original hypothesis, mtDNA): standstill duration up to ~15,000 years. [igb.illinois.edu PDF](https://www.igb.illinois.edu/sites/default/files/Tammetal2007.pdf) *(n.b.: this specific PDF was retrieved via search snippet in an earlier pass of this research session, not re-fetched in this final pass — treat the 15,000-year figure as provisional pending re-verification)*.
- Raghavan et al. 2015 / related genomic work: standstill began no earlier than 23,000 years ago and lasted no more than 8,000 years (ending ~15,000 years ago) — a much shorter window than Tamm et al. [PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1705966114) *(same caveat: snippet-level, not fetched in final pass)*.

**Both claims are presented as-is; the field has not converged on standstill duration** — estimates range from roughly 2,400–9,000 years in some genomic models to up to 15,000 years in earlier mtDNA-based models.

### 1.5 Cooper's Ferry, Idaho

- Davis et al. 2019, *Science* 365(6456):891–897, DOI 10.1126/science.aax9830 — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31467216/): radiocarbon dating of the earliest occupation layer at **16,560–15,280 cal yr BP**. This predates the opening of the ice-free corridor (available no earlier than ~14,800 cal BP), supporting a Pacific coastal migration route over the classic ice-free-corridor route.
- **2020 critical comment** (*Science*): argued the dates are likely younger and more consistent with Greenland Interstadial 1 (~15,000 BP). [science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aaz4695](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aaz4695)
- **2022 follow-up** (*Science Advances*): extended the site's stemmed-point tool assemblage to as old as ~15,785 cal BP. [science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.ade1248](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.ade1248)

**Standing:** widely cited as one of the best-dated pre-Clovis sites in North America, but the 2020 comment shows the exact age is still contested within a few-hundred-to-thousand-year window.

### 1.6 Paisley Caves, Oregon

- Gilbert et al. 2008, *Science* 320(5877):786–789, DOI 10.1126/science.1154116 — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18388261/): human mtDNA (haplogroups A2, B2) recovered from coprolites, AMS-dated to **12,300 ¹⁴C years BP (uncalibrated)** — over 1,000 ¹⁴C years earlier than the Clovis dates accepted at the time.
- **2009 rebuttal** (*Science*): questioned the authenticity of the DNA results and the reliability of the dates, citing lack of intact stratigraphy, absence of diagnostic artifacts, and carbon-isotope anomalies. [science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1168182](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1168182)
- **2012 follow-up:** described in secondary coverage as a "final blow to Clovis First" — Western Stemmed points at the site were found older than or contemporaneous with Clovis, and DNA evidence pointed to a population distinct from Clovis-associated groups. [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/528450)

**Standing:** the coprolite dates and DNA results remain contested by the 2009 rebuttal; both positions should be presented, not averaged.

## 2. PALEOINDIAN

### 2.1 Clovis — recently narrowed date range

- Waters, Stafford & Carlson 2020, *Science Advances* (reported via [Texas A&M](https://stories.tamu.edu/news/2020/10/23/texas-am-expert-new-clues-revealed-about-clovis-people/)): Clovis dated to a tight **13,050–12,750 cal yr BP** window (a ~300-year span), based on radiocarbon dates on bone, charcoal, and carbonized plant remains from 10 sites across 8 US states — much narrower than older, looser estimates. Clovis disappearance coincides with the extinction of mammoths and mastodons at 12,750 years ago.
- This same 13,050–12,750 cal yr BP figure is independently cited as the standard reference in the 2021 Folsom Bayesian paper (see §2.2), corroborating its adoption as the current consensus range. [Buchanan et al. 2021 PDF](http://marcusjhamilton.weebly.com/uploads/2/5/5/3/25533140/buchanan_et_al_2021.pdf)

❗ **COMMON MISCONCEPTION:** Clovis was long thought to represent the first colonizers, who then spread rapidly southward to populate South America. The narrowed 300-year Clovis window shows there was not enough time for Clovis populations to have single-handedly colonized both continents; contemporaneous, non-descendant traditions — the Western Stemmed Tradition in the West and Fishtail points in South America — existed alongside or even before Clovis, rather than descending from it.

### 2.2 Folsom

Bayesian re-analysis using IntCal20 and OxCal v.4.4 on 37 reliable radiocarbon ages (11 sites, 14 components): [Buchanan et al. 2021](http://marcusjhamilton.weebly.com/uploads/2/5/5/3/25533140/buchanan_et_al_2021.pdf)

- Modeled Folsom **beginning**: 12,845–12,770 cal yr BP (68% credible interval); 12,910–12,750 cal yr BP (95% credible interval)
- Modeled Folsom **ending**: 12,400–12,255 cal yr BP (68%); 12,430–12,125 cal yr BP (95%)
- Modeled **duration**: 355–510 years (68%); 325–650 years (95%)
- Oldest potential calibrated Folsom age: **13,055–12,740 cal yr BP** (95% CI), from calcined bone at Barger Gulch, Colorado (sample AA-109926, 10,922±61 ¹⁴C yr BP)
- Youngest calibrated Folsom age: **12,465–11,945 cal yr BP** (95% CI), from a bison scapula at Lindenmeier, Colorado (sample Aeon-2559, 10,335±35 ¹⁴C yr BP)
- The Folsom range's start overlaps the modeled *end* of Clovis (13,050–12,750 cal yr BP, Waters/Stafford/Carlson 2020), and its onset roughly coincides with the Younger Dryas onset at 12,850 cal yr BP (Steffensen et al. 2008, as cited in the same paper).
- The authors interpret this overlap as favoring **cultural transition/diffusion** from Clovis to Folsom over a population-replacement ("catastrophic event") model.

### 2.3 Western Stemmed Tradition (WST)

Per [Rosencrance, "The What, Where, and When of the Western Stemmed Tradition," CSFA/TAMU abstract](https://liberalarts.tamu.edu/csfa/wp-content/uploads/sites/14/2025/01/Rosencrance_WST_Abstract.pdf) (dating method not specified on this page — treat these as summary "years ago" figures, not calibrated BP; n.a. for exact method):

- Earliest stemmed points in the American Far West: **~16,000 years ago**
- Major WST fluorescence: **~13,000 to ~9,000 years ago**
- Explicitly states: "There is no indication that stemmed points are derived from Clovis points" — i.e., the WST is an independent technological tradition, not a Clovis derivative.
- Proposed technological antecedents lie in Upper Paleolithic stemmed-point traditions of northeast Asia, bearing on "how and when people first reached North America."

**n.a.:** this source did not provide individual site-level radiocarbon determinations or a calibration method, so the ~16,000/~13,000/~9,000 "years ago" figures should not be treated as calibrated BP with the same precision as the Clovis/Folsom Bayesian dates above.

### 2.4 Megafaunal extinction and its contested causes

Two positions, genuinely disputed in the literature — **not averaged**:

**Position A — climate change as primary driver:**
Stewart, Carleton & Groucutt 2021, *Nature Communications*, [nature.com/articles/s41467-021-21201-8](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-021-21201-8) — Bayesian regression modeling found no significant relationship between human population-size proxies and megafauna population decline in North America (15.0–11.7 ka window; extended to 20.0–10.0 ka). Climate (NGRIP δ¹⁸O proxy) correlated positively and consistently with megafauna population levels in every model tested. At least 37 genera (~80% of North American megafauna) disappeared near the end of the Pleistocene; 16 of the 37 genera have last-appearance dates between 13.8–11.4 ka, clustering around the Bølling–Allerød (14.7–12.9 ka) to Younger Dryas (12.9–11.7 ka) transition. The paper's title states its conclusion directly: "Climate change, not human population growth, correlates with Late Quaternary megafauna declines in North America." It explicitly states its analysis failed to replicate earlier findings that both overkill and climate jointly drove extinctions.

**Position B — climate/environment as primary driver, in a regional case study, explicitly against simple "overkill":**
Northeastern North America study (Robinson et al., reported in [Science.org, "What Killed the Great Beasts of North America?"](https://www.science.org/content/article/what-killed-great-beasts-north-america)) — 57 megafaunal radiocarbon dates (47 sites) and 25 Paleoindian dates (22 sites) in NY/NJ/PA/MA/ME/Ontario. Findings: 75–90% of northeastern megafauna were already gone **before** humans arrived in the region; the first major decline occurred 14,100 years ago, pre-human; the second, final crash began 12,700 years ago (coinciding with the ~1,300-year Younger Dryas cold event and with Paleoindian arrival) but no butchered or hunting-modified megafaunal bones were found in the region despite ~1,000 years of human–megafauna overlap. Authors caution their conclusion is regional (northeast only), not continent-wide.

**Overkill proponents' rebuttal (same article):** Gary Haynes argues the data "neither proves nor disproves overkill" and that early human presence may simply be under-sampled archaeologically. Todd Surovell argues the northeastern data is fully consistent with humans delivering a "final blow" (coup de grâce) to already-declining populations — i.e., climate set the stage, humans finished the job.

**Standing:** the debate remains genuinely open. Recent quantitative modeling (2021, North America-wide) favors climate; regional case studies (northeast) also favor climate/environment over simple overkill, but overkill proponents dispute the interpretation of absence of hunting evidence as absence of hunting.

## 3. ARCHAIC AND EARLY MONUMENTAL

*(Archaic Period 8500 BCE–500 CE and Norte Chico/Caral 3000–1800 BCE are already in the dataset and are not re-researched here.)*

### 3.1 Watson Brake, Louisiana

Saunders et al. 1997, *Science* 277:1796–1799 — [Science.org coverage](https://www.science.org/content/article/oldest-earthen-mounds-heighten-mystery): radiocarbon dating on charcoal from soil horizons beneath mound bases shows construction began **5,400–5,300 years ago** (calibrated ~3500 BCE) and continued for 400–600 years. This predates Poverty Point (§3.2) by about 1,900 years.

**Significance:** challenges the older assumption that monumental mound-building required agriculture and a food surplus — Watson Brake was built by hunter-gatherers with little evidence of farming or extensive trade networks.

### 3.2 Poverty Point, Louisiana

Per [UNESCO's official World Heritage listing](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1435/): the site dates to **3,700–3,100 BP** (~1700–1100 BCE), radiocarbon (¹⁴C) dated, built by a hunter-fisher-gatherer society. The broader "Poverty Point culture" spans 4,000–2,500 years ago per the same source.

### 3.3 Chinchorro mummies, Chile — ❗ major misconception entry

- Per Marquet, Santoro, Latorre et al. 2012, *PNAS*, [pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1116724109](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1116724109) (fetched directly): "The oldest known example of artificial mummification (AM) is dated at **ca. 7–8 ka BP**" in the coastal desert of northern Chile and southern Peru. Coastal settlements in the region began ~10 ka BP; the Acha site shows Chinchorro-like funerary patterns as early as 10 ka BP (though not necessarily artificial mummification itself at that date). Artificial mummification "practically disappeared by **4.4 ka BP**," coinciding with the onset of extreme aridity in the central Atacama between 4–5 ka BP.
- UNESCO's official heritage listing states the culture resided on the Atacama coast from roughly **5450 BCE to 890 BCE**. [whc.unesco.org/en/list/1634](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1634/) (UNESCO used here as an institutional source, not Wikipedia, so it is not subject to the "locator only" rule — but note it is a heritage-listing summary, not a primary excavation report).

❗ **COMMON MISCONCEPTION (flagged as intended):** Chinchorro artificial mummification (beginning ca. 7–8 ka BP / ~5450 BCE per UNESCO) predates the earliest Egyptian mummification (conventionally dated to ~3000 BCE, not independently re-verified in this pass) by roughly **2,000 years**. The popular narrative that treats Egypt as mummification's origin point is backwards — South American mummification is substantially older.

### 3.4 Las Vegas culture / Real Alto, Ecuador

Per [Tohoku University Museum bulletin, "Functional Analysis of Prehistoric Artifacts from Coastal Ecuador"](https://www.museum.tohoku.ac.jp/pdf/press_info/bulletin/No13/bulletin_13_03.pdf) (fetched directly):

- Las Vegas culture (type site OGSE-80 / "Site 80"): **10,800–6,600 radiocarbon years BP, explicitly stated as NOT calibrated**. Described as "the oldest known archaeological complex on the coast of Ecuador."
- Domesticated food plants (squash, bottle gourd, and the root crop leren) documented via phytolith analysis at Las Vegas sites at more than 9,000 BP (calibration status not specified in source).
- Late Las Vegas phase (human burials at Site 80): **8,000–6,600 BP** (calibration status not specified).
- Real Alto: this source gives no separate numeric date for the site itself, describing it only as a major Valdivia-culture site with a plaza, mound, "Fiesta House," and "Charnel House."

### 3.5 Valdivia culture, Ecuador — genuine, unresolved dating disagreement (both claims presented, not averaged)

Two non-Wikipedia sources give **materially different** date ranges for the Valdivia culture and its phases — this is presented as an open disagreement in the literature, not resolved here:

- **Cambridge/*Antiquity* 2019** (Kanomata, Marcos, Popov, Lazin, Tabarev, ["New data on early pottery traditions in South America: the San Pedro complex, Ecuador"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/new-data-on-early-pottery-traditions-in-south-america-the-san-pedro-complex-ecuador/8885FD3A582F285F93728325C4945B66), fetched directly): Valdivia 1 = **3650–2650 BC**; Valdivia 2 = **2650–2350 BC** (the oldest dates at the type site are associated with Valdivia 2). San Pedro complex pottery (a distinct, possibly separate tradition found stratigraphically *below* Valdivia 2 at Real Alto) is AMS-dated directly on carbon residue from pottery vessels to **4640±20 BP** (lab IAAA-171318) and **4460±30 BP** (lab IAAA-181069) — described as contemporaneous with the end of Valdivia 1, not calibrated to BC in this source.
- **Tohoku Museum bulletin** (same source as §3.4): gives the Valdivia culture as a whole spanning **5,000–3,500 uncalibrated radiocarbon years BP**, "the earliest ceramic culture" on the coast, with Early/Middle/Late Valdivia sub-phases named but not individually dated in this source.

**These two ranges are not reconcilable without a calibration curve conversion this research did not perform** — presenting both explicitly rather than converting/averaging, per the sourcing rule.

### 3.6 Huaca Prieta, Peru

Per Cambridge/*Antiquity* supplementary chronology document, ["Chronology, mound-building and environment at Huaca Prieta"](https://static.cambridge.org/content/id/urn:cambridge.org:id:article:S0003598X00062451/resource/name/S0003598X00062451sup001.pdf) (fetched directly):

- First human presence: **~13,700–13,300 cal BP**
- Initial site occupation phase: **~9,000–8,000 cal BP**
- Deepest pre-mound and early mound-building layers: **~9,000–7,600 cal BP**
- Mound building began: **~7,500 cal BP**
- Mound construction and use continued over **~3,500 years**
- Phase III sunken plaza with ritual offerings (shell, coca, feathers): dates from at least **~4,000 cal BP**
- Cotton production evidence: **~6,800 cal BP**; corn/other crops incorporated after **~6,500 cal BP**
- Raised agricultural platforms/fields: **~4,800 cal BP**

Maize chronology at Huaca Prieta and the nearby site of Paredones, per Grobman, Bonavia, Dillehay, Piperno, Iriarte & Holst 2012, *PNAS*, [pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1120270109](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1120270109) (fetched directly, full radiocarbon table extracted): earliest secure AMS-dated maize macrofossil from these sites is a husk/shank fragment (sample OS86020) dated to **6775–6504 cal BP** — described in the paper as "the oldest AMS date on maize macrofossils from South America," roughly contemporary with Guilá Naquitz maize in Mexico (~6200 cal BP, not independently re-verified here since Guilá Naquitz maize is explicitly out of scope per task instructions). Note: this maize/agricultural detail is provided for chronological context of the Huaca Prieta occupation sequence — the maize-domestication topic itself at Balsas/Guilá Naquitz is explicitly excluded from this dataset per the task brief, and is not the focus here.

### 3.7 Caballo Muerto, Peru

Per Pozorski/Pozorski-era excavation report, ["Excavaciones en Huaca Cortada, complejo de Caballo Muerto"](https://revistas.pucp.edu.pe/index.php/boletindearqueologia/article/download/970/937/0) (fetched directly, PUCP *Boletín de Arqueología*):

- Huaca Cortada (part of the Caballo Muerto complex), earliest floor of Terrace 1: two uncalibrated AMS dates on a wooden stake — **3330±41 BP** and **3297±45 BP**, calibrating (2σ) to **1666–1442 BC** and **1628–1418 BC** respectively; averaged calibrated date **1621–1443 BC**.
- Earliest construction phase at Huaca Cortada: **around 1500 BC** (calibrated).
- The wider Caballo Muerto complex, per Thomas Pozorski's three-part chronology based on architecture, ceramics, and radiocarbon: **c. 1400–400 BC** (explicitly stated as uncalibrated in this source).
- Huaca de los Reyes (part of the same complex): Pozorski proposed an average age of **1300 BC** (uncalibrated), despite variability among the underlying dates.
- Huaca de los Chinos, San Lorenzo ceramic style: **1000–800 BC** (calibrated).

**n.a.:** the source explicitly states the San Lorenzo phase at Huaca Cortada had not yet been dated, and no samples existed to date the final construction phase (Structure 1).

### 3.8 Cerro Sechín / Sechín Bajo, Peru — ❗ misconception correction

The commonly repeated claim (found on Wikipedia — **locator only, not used as a dating authority**) that Cerro Sechín has evidence of occupation from 7600 BCE, or that Sechín Bajo has a plaza/frieze dated to 3500–3600 BCE, was **checked against a Peruvian government (MINCUL) technical excavation report** — ["Cerro Sechín: informe técnico de los estudios, análisis y resultados de los materiales arqueológicos"](https://cdn.www.gob.pe/uploads/document/file/4791046/Informe%20t%C3%A9cnico%20de%20los%20estudios,%20an%C3%A1lisis%20y%20resultados%20de%20los%20materiales%20arqueol%C3%B3gicos%20.pdf) (fetched directly).

**Finding:** this official excavation report — based on 2019 AMS dating (Beta Analytic, calibrated with BetaCal4.20/Reimer et al. 2020/SHCal20) of five charcoal samples from Cerro Sechín's Edificio Principal — produced dates that are **much younger** than the popularly cited figures:
- A funerary context: **1220–1297 cal AD** (94.1% confidence) and **1666–1821 cal AD** (64.3% confidence)
- Charcoal from the base of a stepped adobe structure (post-dating that structure's construction, since the samples are intrusive into the floor): **1887–1689 cal BC** (95.4% confidence, sample M15) and similar ranges around **1780–1620 cal BC** (samples M16/M17)

**The oldest date this official report actually documents for the site is ~1887–1689 cal BC (i.e., roughly 3,600–3,900 cal BP) — not 7600 BCE.** The report explicitly states it has **no radiocarbon dates at all** for Sechín Bajo or Caballo Muerto.

❗ **COMMON MISCONCEPTION FLAGGED:** the widely repeated "7600 BCE earliest occupation at Cerro Sechín" and "Sechín Bajo plaza dated to 3500 BCE" figures could **not** be confirmed against this fetched primary source, and the actual radiocarbon evidence in the government report points to a substantially younger, second-millennium-BC (and even historic-era) occupation sequence at the specific contexts sampled. This does not necessarily mean the older Initial Period dates are wrong — the report may simply not have sampled the oldest strata — but per the sourcing rule, the popularly cited figure is written here as **n.a.** (unconfirmed) rather than asserted, and the discrepancy itself is flagged as the notable finding.

## 4. TIME-PERMITTING TOPICS

### 4.1 Old Copper Complex, Great Lakes — ❗ major misconception entry, ⚑ literature moved

**Older (pre-radiocarbon-refinement) belief:** copper-tool production began roughly 6,000 years ago and was mostly abandoned by about 3,000 years ago (per [Science.org, "Great Lakes people among first coppersmiths"](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.371.6536.1299), fetched directly, describing the prior consensus this paper revises).

⚑ **LITERATURE MOVED (2021):** Pompeani, Steinman, Abbott, Pompeani, Reardon, DePasqual & Mueller, *Radiocarbon* 63(2):513–531, [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/radiocarbon/article/abs/on-the-timing-of-the-old-copper-complex-in-north-america-a-comparison-of-radiocarbon-dates-from-different-archaeological-contexts/E46715993E58EDC94F225CC6FE776CF2) (fetched directly) — analysis of 53 radiocarbon dates (8 new, 45 published) plus six lake-sediment chronologies of copper mining/annealing activity in the Michigan Copper District found:

- Embedded organic material (wood, cordage, cremation remains) associated with 15 Old Copper Complex artifacts dates to **8,500–3,580 cal BP** (excluding three anomalously young outlier samples).
- Lake-sediment trace-metal (lead) evidence indicates copper mining began about **9,500 years ago** in some areas — about **3,500 years earlier** than the previous estimate of ~6,000 years ago.
- The revised chronology places the Old Copper Complex's peak between **7,000 and 5,000 years ago**, with mining activity ending around **5,400 years ago** (per the companion 2015 *The Holocene* sediment study cited in the Science.org piece).
- The single oldest reliably dated Old Copper artifact: a ~10 cm pure-copper projectile point from beside Eagle Lake, Wisconsin, dated to **about 8,500 years old**.
- The report explicitly interprets younger dates found in stratigraphic/mine contexts as reflecting **mine abandonment and infill**, not active mining — i.e., earlier researchers who dated mine contexts may have been dating the *wrong* event.
- The revised Great Lakes chronology (≥9,500 years) is described as "at least as old, and perhaps older" than a well-known ~8,700-year-old copper pendant from the Middle East, making Old Copper one of the earliest metalworking traditions documented anywhere in the world.
- A 2022 PLOS ONE Bayesian re-modeling (cited from an earlier search pass, not independently re-fetched in this final pass — treat as provisional pending direct verification) reportedly places the complex's beginning between cal BP 7,520–6,180 and its end between cal BP 4,840–4,190; this is a narrower, later-starting range than the Pompeani et al. 2021 8,500 cal BP figure, and the two should be read as not-yet-fully-reconciled estimates from different modeling approaches.

❗ **COMMON MISCONCEPTION FLAGGED:** metalworking in the Americas is popularly associated with much later Mesoamerican and Andean civilizations. The Old Copper Complex predates virtually all other known metalworking in the Americas by thousands of years and rivals or predates some of the earliest Old World copper-working traditions — a fact rarely taught alongside the Old World "Bronze Age" framework.

### 4.2 Earliest pottery in the Americas: Taperinha and Caverna da Pedra Pintada, Brazil

Per Roosevelt, Housley, Imazio da Silveira, Maranca & Johnson 1991, *Science* 254(5038):1621–1624, ["Eighth Millennium Pottery from a Prehistoric Shell Midden in the Brazilian Amazon"](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.254.5038.1621) (fetched directly):

- **Taperinha** (a shell midden/sambaqui near Santarém, lower Amazon, Brazil): pottery, charcoal, and shell were dated via **calibrated accelerator radiocarbon dating**, plus one **thermoluminescence** date on the pottery itself. Combined dating range: **about 8,000 to 7,000 years before present**. The paper's own framing: "the earliest pottery yet found in the Western Hemisphere."
- **Caverna da Pedra Pintada** (Painted Rock Cave, near Monte Alegre, Brazil): this specific 1991 *Science* paper does **not** provide numeric dates for Pedra Pintada pottery (it focuses on Taperinha); n.a. for a fetched primary source on Pedra Pintada's pottery chronology specifically in this pass. Secondary/tertiary sources (Wikipedia and derivative reference works — **locator only**) commonly cite Pedra Pintada pottery at "7,500 to 5,000 years ago" and the site's earliest (pre-ceramic) human occupation layers at "~11,200 years ago," but these figures were **not independently confirmed against a fetched primary excavation report in this pass** and are recorded here as **n.a. pending direct verification** — do not treat the Wikipedia-sourced Pedra Pintada figures as established.

**Significance (confirmed via the fetched 1991 Science paper):** Taperinha pottery, at ~8,000–7,000 BP, predates the previously accepted earliest pottery in the Americas by roughly 1,000 years, and predates both the Valdivia culture ceramics of Ecuador (§3.5, ~3650–2650 BC at the earliest per the fetched Antiquity source, i.e. considerably younger) and the earliest Andean pottery (secondary literature places this at ~1800–2100 BC, not independently verified here) by a wide margin. This is consistent with the broader pattern that the *lower Amazon*, not the Andes or Mesoamerica, has produced the oldest confirmed pottery-bearing sites in the hemisphere.

**n.a.:** exact sample-level radiocarbon lab numbers, individual date ranges (vs. the pooled "8,000 to 7,000 BP" summary statement), and BCE conversion were not provided in the fetched abstract-level content of the 1991 paper; a fuller version of the paper (behind paywall/not accessed) would be needed for sample-by-sample detail.

## Summary of ⚑ LITERATURE MOVED flags

1. **White Sands footprints** — moved from disputed (2021) toward multi-method consensus (2023, reaffirmed 2025), but a 2024 rebuttal keeps the chronology contested.
2. **Monte Verde, Chile** — a 2026 *Science* paper proposes the site is thousands of years younger than the long-accepted ~14,500 cal BP date; this is a live, unresolved, and explosive controversy as of this writing.
3. **Old Copper Complex** — 2021 re-dating pushed the origin of Great Lakes copper metallurgy back roughly 3,500 years, from ~6,000 to at least ~9,500 years ago, one of the largest chronological revisions in the dataset.
4. **Clovis** — the 2020 Bayesian narrowing to a tight 300-year window (13,050–12,750 cal yr BP) is itself a "literature moved" event relative to older, looser Clovis chronologies.

## Summary of ❗ COMMON MISCONCEPTION flags

1. **Clovis First** — Clovis was not the sole ancestral population; Western Stemmed, Monte Verde, Cooper's Ferry, and Paisley Caves evidence show contemporaneous or older, independent traditions.
2. **Chinchorro mummies predate Egyptian mummification** by roughly 2,000 years (~7–8 ka BP / ~5450 BCE vs. ~3000 BCE) — the reverse of the popular assumption that Egypt originated mummification.
3. **Old Copper Complex** — Great Lakes metalworking (≥9,500 years ago) is far older than popularly assumed and rivals the earliest Old World copper-working traditions, yet is rarely discussed in the same breath as Old World "Bronze/Copper Age" narratives.
4. **Cerro Sechín's popularly cited early dates (7600 BCE) could not be confirmed** against the fetched official Peruvian excavation report, which instead documents much younger (2nd-millennium-BC and even historic-era) dates at the sampled contexts — a caution against repeating unverified figures rather than a positive misconception correction.
