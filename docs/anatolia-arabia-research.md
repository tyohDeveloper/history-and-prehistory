## PART 1 — ANATOLIA

### 1. THE HITTITES

#### 1.1 Hittite Old Kingdom
- **Dates:** c. 1650–c. 1500 BCE (span given by Britannica); Brill's academic overview gives the full Hittite civilization span as "ca. 1650–1190 BCE" without subdividing precisely at this boundary.
- **Dating basis:** Textual/king-list (Hittite royal annals, Telipinu Proclamation), cross-referenced to Mesopotamian synchronisms — NOT radiocarbon or dendrochronological for the founding/period boundaries themselves.
- **Significance:** Founded by Hattusili I/Labarna lineage from Kussara; established Hattusa as capital; culminates in Mursili I's raid on Babylon (see 1.3).
- **Sources:** [Britannica, "Old Kingdom (Hittite history)"](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Old-Kingdom-Hittite-history); [Brill Academic, Ch. 5 on Hittite civilization](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004548633/BP000005.xml) (fetched).
- **Flags:** None beyond standard chronology-scheme caveats (see 1.3).

#### 1.2 Hittite Middle Kingdom
- **Dates:** n.a. as a precisely bounded period — described in fetched academic literature as an ill-defined "dark age" between the reign of Telipinu and the rise of Suppiluliuma I, roughly the 15th century BCE.
- **Dating basis:** Textual (fragmentary), with acknowledged gap in documentation.
- **Significance:** Transition/decline phase between Old and New Kingdoms; poorly attested compared to its neighbors.
- **Sources:** [Brill Academic, Ch. 5](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004548633/BP000005.xml) (fetched).
- **Flags:** Precise start/end dates are genuinely unresolved in the literature — do not assign firm boundary years.

#### 1.3 Sack of Babylon by Mursili I
- **Dates:** Chronology-scheme dependent, spanning roughly 230 years across proposed schemes:
  - Ultra-short chronology: 1499 BCE
  - Short chronology: 1531 BCE
  - Low-middle chronology: 1587 BCE
  - **Middle chronology: 1595 BCE** (most widely used/most supported)
  - Long chronology: 1651 BCE (favored by some astronomical reconstructions)
  - Ultra-long chronology: 1736 BCE
- **Dating basis:** Textual/king-list, tied to the broader unresolved Mesopotamian "chronology problem" (astronomical Venus-tablet observations of Ammisaduqa interpreted differently under each scheme). This is NOT independently radiocarbon- or dendro-dated as an event.
- **Significance:** Ended the Old Babylonian (Hammurabi) dynasty; a pivotal Bronze Age synchronism used to anchor much of 2nd-millennium-BCE Near Eastern chronology — hence its extreme sensitivity to which chronology scheme is adopted.
- **Sources:** [Wikipedia, "Babylonia"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonia) (fetched; **locator only** — used here solely to enumerate the competing chronology schemes, not as a dating authority); [Brill Academic, Ch. 5](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004548633/BP000005.xml) (fetched; states "1595 BCE" without specifying which chronology scheme it assumes).
- **Flags:** ⚑ COMMON MISCONCEPTION — treating "1595 BCE" as *the* uncontested date. It is the Middle Chronology figure only; per rules, both/all standings are presented above rather than averaged. State explicitly which chronology any downstream figure assumes.

#### 1.4 Hattusa (capital)
- **Dates of occupation/excavation:** Continuous German Archaeological Institute (DAI) excavation since 1931, with earlier precursor soundings by Hugo Winckler and Theodore Makridi in 1906–1907 and 1911–1912. Current excavation director: Andreas Schachner, since 2006.
- **Dating basis:** Archaeological-stratigraphic (ongoing DAI fieldwork).
- **Significance:** Capital of the Hittite Empire; UNESCO World Heritage Site; source of the Hittite royal archives.
- **Sources:** [BiblePlaces, "Hattusha"](https://www.bibleplaces.com/hattusha/); [DAI official history, Istanbul department](https://www.dainst.org/en/departments/istanbul/about-us/history); [Turkish Archaeological News, Hattusa](https://turkisharchaeonews.net/site/hattusa). *(Note: these three are search-located, not directly content-fetched in this pass; cited here only for excavation-history facts, not numeric dates beyond the fetched Brill source below.)*
- **Flags:** ⚑ COMMON MISCONCEPTION — the popular narrative that Hattusa was violently sacked/burned at the end of the Hittite Empire (c. 1180 BCE) is **now disputed by DAI archaeological evidence**. The fetched Brill chapter states current DAI evidence does *not* support violent destruction; the royal court likely relocated after the buildings were deliberately emptied. See section 1.6.

#### 1.5 Battle and Treaty of Kadesh
- **Battle date:** c. 1274 BCE (Egyptian regnal-year basis — Year 5 of Ramesses II, "Year 5, III Shemu, day 9" — under the "Low Chronology" of Egyptology; a "High Chronology" places it roughly 20 years earlier). Treated here as low-chronology-dependent; do not present 1274 BCE as chronology-independent fact.
- **Treaty date:** Year 21 of Ramesses II. The most-cited figure is **1259 BCE** for the treaty's conclusion, with the Egyptian date equated to **10 November 1259 BCE** under the Low Chronology (per the Egyptian date I Peret 21 of Year 21); one source states ratification "1258 BC." Treat 1259 BCE as the standard figure and 1258 BCE as a minor variant reflecting rounding/regnal-year-count conventions — do not average.
- **Dating basis:** Egyptian regnal-year/astronomical synchronization (Low Chronology), cross-checked against Hittite king-list; NOT radiocarbon.
- **Significance:** The Egyptian–Hittite Treaty (the "Eternal Treaty"/"Silver Treaty") is the oldest surviving text of an international peace treaty; a clay tablet copy is held in Istanbul, and a replica is displayed at UN Headquarters.
- **Sources:** [Wikipedia, "Egyptian–Hittite peace treaty"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian%E2%80%93Hittite_peace_treaty) (fetched via search snippet — **locator only**); [Wikipedia, "Ramesses II"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramesses_II) (locator only, states "10 November 1259 BC" under standard Low Chronology).
- **Flags:** Battle date is explicitly chronology-scheme-dependent (Low vs. High Egyptian chronology); do not present a single uncontested year.

#### 1.6 Collapse of the Hittite Empire (c. 1180 BCE)
- **Dates:** Brill academic source: final dissolution in the "first quarter of the 12th century BCE." A 2023 Nature-published Cornell study places a severe multi-year drought "coincident with Hittite collapse" specifically at **1198–1196 BCE**.
- **Dating basis:** For the drought/climate correlation — **dendrochronology plus stable-isotope analysis** (tree-ring width and δ¹³C/δ¹²C carbon-isotope ratios in juniper timbers from the Midas Mound Tumulus at Gordion, analyzed by the Cornell Tree-Ring Laboratory and Cornell Stable Isotope Laboratory). This is NOT the same technique as radiocarbon dating, though it draws on the same tree-ring archive discussed in Section 4. For the political collapse itself, dating basis remains textual/archaeological-typological.
- **Significance:** Marks the end of the Hittite New Kingdom/Empire and the broader Late Bronze Age collapse in Anatolia and the eastern Mediterranean; the Cornell study is among the first to link the collapse to a specific, precisely-dated climatic event.
- **Sources:** [Cornell Chronicle, "Rare drought coincided with Hittite empire collapse," Feb. 2023](https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2023/02/rare-drought-coincided-hittite-empire-collapse) (fetched); [Brill Academic, Ch. 5](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004548633/BP000005.xml) (fetched).
- **Flags:** ⚑ COMMON MISCONCEPTION — the popular idea of a violent "sack" or "burning" of Hattusa is explicitly contradicted by current DAI archaeological findings per the fetched Brill source: no evidence of violent destruction; buildings appear to have been deliberately emptied before abandonment, suggesting planned relocation of the royal court rather than conquest. This should be flagged wherever "collapse of the Hittite Empire" is discussed alongside "destruction of Hattusa" — they are not equivalent claims.

#### 1.7 Neo-Hittite / Syro-Hittite Successor States
- **Dates:** Arose following the 12th-century-BCE collapse (see 1.6) and were absorbed piecemeal into the Neo-Assyrian Empire between the mid-9th and early-8th centuries BCE, with the process essentially complete by the end of Sargon II's reign in **705 BCE**. Key dated absorption events per a fetched Oxford Academic chapter (Trevor Bryce):
  - Masuwari/Til Barsip seized by Shalmaneser III and renamed Kar-Shalmaneser: **856 BCE**.
  - Arpad besieged and captured by Tiglath-pileser III, becoming the first western state converted directly into an Assyrian province: **740 BCE**.
  - Patin/Unqi (province of Kullani(a)) — first Neo-Hittite kingdom incorporated into the Assyrian provincial system: **738 BCE**.
  - Damascus captured, Rasyan executed: **732 BCE**.
  - Hamath defeated at Qarqar, Yaubidi flayed, made an Assyrian province: **720 BCE**.
  - **Carchemish** — Sargon II's fifth regnal year — captured, King Pisiri deported, converted to Assyrian province: **717 BCE** (corroborated across multiple independent sources: Britannica, Encyclopedia.com, Biblical Archaeology Society, and the Oxford Academic chapter).
  - Malatya/Melid partly annexed (Kammanu, Til-garimmu): **712 BCE**.
  - Gurgum (province of Marqas): **711 BCE**.
  - Kummuh, the last Neo-Hittite kingdom incorporated, "effectively marking the end of the history of the Neo-Hittite kingdoms": **708 BCE**.
- **Dating basis:** Textual — Assyrian royal inscriptions, the Assyrian Eponym Chronicle, Babylonian Chronicle, Urartian inscriptions, Luwian hieroglyphic inscriptions, and biblical cross-references (2 Kings). No radiocarbon or dendrochronological dating is used for these political/military events.
- **Significance:** These Luwian- and Aramaic-speaking successor states (Carchemish, Malatya, Tabal, Gurgum, Kummuh, Sam'al, Que/Adanawa, etc.) preserved Hittite hieroglyphic and cultural traditions for four centuries after the imperial collapse, until final Assyrian absorption.
- **Sources:** [Oxford Academic, Trevor Bryce, "Absorption by Assyria (8th century)"](https://academic.oup.com/book/9649/chapter/156739759) (fetched — full chapter with itemized dates above); [Britannica, "Anatolia — Neo-Hittite, 1180–700 BCE"](https://www.britannica.com/place/Anatolia/The-neo-Hittite-states-from-c-1180-to-700-bce); [Ayıntap, "Carchemish Ancient City"](https://ayintap.com/en/culture/ancient-cities/carchemish-en/); [Biblical Archaeology Society, "Carchemish in the Bible and History"](https://www.biblicalarchaeology.org/daily/biblical-sites-places/biblical-archaeology-sites/carchemish-bible-history/).
- **Flags:** None beyond noting the entirely textual (Assyrian-source-driven) dating basis — no independent scientific dating exists for these specific conquest years.

### 2. MITANNI, URARTU, PHRYGIA/MIDAS, LYDIA/COINAGE, LUWIANS

#### 2.1 Mitanni
- **Dates:** Genuinely disputed range across sources — no single consensus figure:
  - c. 1600–1260 BCE (Wikipedia, broad range)
  - c. 1500–1360 BCE (Britannica)
  - c. 1475–1275 BCE (World History Encyclopedia)
  - c. 1650–1275 BCE, "estimate," per a Leiden University student thesis
  - Formation "one or two generations before the sack of Babylon [by Mursili I]... predated the sack... by at least two generations," per a fetched Heidelberg University academic paper — i.e., formation is anchored relative to the (chronology-dependent) sack-of-Babylon date, not to an independent absolute year.
- **Key events with dates:** Washukanni (capital) sacked by Suppiluliuma I early in his reign (Suppiluliuma I r. c. 1344–1322 BCE, per multiple sources); sacked again by Adad-nirari I c. 1290 BCE; kingdom finally absorbed into Assyria under Shalmaneser I, with a specific defeat of Hittite-backed forces at Nihriya by Tukulti-Ninurta I dated to **1245 BCE** (World History Encyclopedia).
- **Dating basis:** Textual/king-list synchronism (Hittite, Assyrian, and Egyptian [Amarna Letters] royal correspondence and treaties); explicitly tied to the unresolved Mesopotamian absolute-chronology problem (see 1.3) per the fetched Heidelberg paper, which states Mitanni's formation "must have taken place during the latest phase of the Old Babylonian period."
- **Significance:** A Hurrian-speaking, Indo-Aryan-influenced kingdom in northern Syria/southeastern Anatolia; a major Hittite and Egyptian rival/ally via royal marriage diplomacy (Amarna Letters); produced the world's oldest known horse-training manual (Kikkuli text).
- **Sources:** [Heidelberg University, Novak, "Mittani Empire and the Question of Absolute Chronology"](https://archiv.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/propylaeumdok/1295/1/Novak_Mittani_Empire_2007.pdf) (fetched); [EBSCO Research Starters, "Mitanni"](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/political-science/mitanni); [Leiden University student thesis, "Was the Mitanni a Suzerain or in a Coalition of States?"](https://studenttheses.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item:2666425/view); [World History Encyclopedia, "Mitanni"](https://www.worldhistory.org/Mitanni/); [Britannica, "Mitanni"](https://www.britannica.com/place/Mitanni).
- **Flags:** ⚑ Present spread of dates (1650–1260 BCE across sources) as a genuine unresolved range tied to the Mesopotamian chronology problem — do NOT average to a single midpoint date.

#### 2.2 Urartu
- **Founding/dynasty start:** Sarduri I's reign is dated variously as 844–828 BCE, 840–830 BCE, 840/832–830/825 BCE, 835–825 BCE, and 834–828 BCE across sources — a real spread of roughly 15 years on both ends. Britannica states "for the reign of Sarduri I (c. 840–830 BCE), there remain only the inscriptions at Van." The Met Museum (institutional source) gives "Sarduri (r. ca. 840–830 BC)."
- **Capital move to Tushpa:** Attributed to Sarduri I, within his reign window above; one source specifies "832 BCE" for the move.
- **Dating basis:** Textual — Urartian royal inscriptions (Corpus Inscriptionum Urartaicarum) cross-synchronized with Assyrian royal annals (contemporary with Shalmaneser III, r. 858–824 BCE). No radiocarbon or dendrochronological dating is used for Urartian royal dates.
- **Significance:** Rival Iron Age kingdom to Assyria centered on Lake Van in the Armenian highlands; the first Urartian king to leave his own inscriptions.
- **Sources:** [Met Museum, "Urartu"](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/urartu) (institutional, high-trust); [Britannica, "Urartu"](https://www.britannica.com/place/Urartu); [EBSCO Research Starters, "Sarduri I"](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/sarduri-i); [UCL, Radner, Oxford Handbook chapter PDF](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/sargon/downloads/radner_ohaa_2011.pdf).
- **Flags:** The ±10–15-year spread across otherwise reputable sources (Britannica, Met, EBSCO, academic UCL PDF) for Sarduri I's reign reflects genuine uncertainty in reconstructing exact regnal years from fragmentary Urartian/Assyrian synchronisms — report as a range, not a fixed year. Collapse date (variously reported elsewhere as 590 BCE or a 640–590 BCE range) was NOT independently confirmed via a fetched source in this pass — mark end date **n.a.**

#### 2.3 Phrygia / King Midas
- **Gordion periodization:** Early Phrygian period: 900–800 BCE; Middle Phrygian period: 800–540 BCE (per Wikipedia/academic OUP chapter, search-located).
- **Midas — Assyrian textual attestations:** As "Mita of Mushki" in Sargon II's annals, dated **717, 709, and c. 670s BCE**, per the Met Museum. Penn Museum's Digital Gordion project confirms the most reliable information on Midas derives from Sargon's records of **717–709 BCE**, and notes some texts imply Midas's reign began before Sargon's own accession in 721 BCE.
- **Dating basis:** Textual (Assyrian royal annals) for Midas's dates; archaeological-typological plus the Gordion dendrochronological sequence (see Section 4) for the Gordion site periods.
- **Significance:** Midas (Mita) was a real, Assyrian-attested Phrygian king, not merely a mythological figure; Gordion was the Phrygian capital.
- **Sources:** [Penn Museum, Digital Gordion, "Myth and Religion"](https://www.penn.museum/sites/gordion/articles/myth-religion/320-2/) (fetched); [Met Museum, "Phrygia, Gordion, and King Midas in the Late Eighth Century B.C."](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/phrygia-gordion-and-king-midas-in-the-late-eighth-century-b-c).
- **Flags:** ⚑ COMMON MISCONCEPTION — the famous "Midas Mound Tumulus" at Gordion is **not** Midas's own tomb. Per BBC Travel, quoting Penn archaeologist Brian Rose: dendrochronological dating of the tomb's wood places it at approximately 740 BCE, but Assyrian records confirm Midas was still alive in 709 BCE — 31 years later — making the attribution impossible ("this tomb cannot belong to Midas"). Gordion was sacked by Cimmerian invaders c. 696 BCE (search-located; not independently fetched — treat as approximate). See [BBC Travel, "Gordion: A lost city of legends in central Turkey"](https://www.bbc.com/travel/article/20240320-gordion-a-lost-city-of-legends-in-central-turkey).

#### 2.4 Lydia and the Invention of Coinage
- **Dates:** Earliest known electrum coins from Lydia dated to **"about 650 BCE"** per the British Museum. Reign dates (search-located, not independently fetched): Alyattes r. c. 618–561 BCE; Croesus r. c. 560–546 BCE.
- **Dating basis:** Archaeological-typological/numismatic — hoard finds (notably the foundation deposit under the Temple of Artemis at Ephesus) combined with stylistic/die sequencing of coin types. This is explicitly NOT a radiocarbon or dendrochronological date.
- **Significance:** Lydia is the conventionally credited originator of metal-struck coinage in the Mediterranean/Near Eastern world, a major economic-technology innovation.
- **Sources:** [British Museum, Money Gallery learning PDF](https://www.britishmuseum.org/sites/default/files/2021-05/Money_Gallery_LPG_2020_Room_68.pdf) (institutional, search-located); [Austrian Academy of Sciences (OeAW), "Early Lydian Coinage and Chronology"](https://www.oeaw.ac.at/en/oeai/research/classical-studies/numismatics/early-lydian-coinage-and-chronology) (academic, search-located).
- **Flags:** ⚑ COMMON MISCONCEPTION — popular attribution of "inventing coinage" to Croesus is incorrect. Electrum coinage began under his father Alyattes (or possibly earlier Mermnad rulers) c. 650–630 BCE; Croesus's specific innovation (mid-6th century BCE) was the introduction of a bimetallic pure-gold/pure-silver coinage standard (the "Croeseid"), not coinage itself.

#### 2.5 Luwians
- **Dates:** Attested in Old Assyrian trading-colony texts from Kültepe/Kanesh, dated **1950–1700 BCE** under the Middle Chronology (Wikipedia — **locator only**, cross-referencing needed but not independently fetched in this pass; treat as approximate). Luwian became a dominant spoken/written language within the Hittite New Kingdom. Neo-Hittite Luwian hieroglyphic inscriptions (Carchemish, Karatepe, etc.) are attested into the 8th century BCE, ending with the Assyrian absorptions documented in Section 1.7.
- **Dating basis:** Textual/epigraphic (cuneiform tablets, hieroglyphic Luwian monumental inscriptions), cross-dated via the Neo-Hittite state chronology in Section 1.7 (itself textual/Assyrian-synchronism based).
- **Significance:** Luwian-speaking populations and elites were central to both the Hittite Empire and its Iron Age Neo-Hittite/Syro-Hittite successor states in southeastern Anatolia and northern Syria.
- **Sources:** [Oxford Academic, Bryce chapter](https://academic.oup.com/book/9649/chapter/156739759) (fetched, for the end-date evidence via Neo-Hittite state absorption).
- **Flags:** The 1950–1700 BCE Kültepe attestation date rests on a Wikipedia locator only in this research pass and was not independently re-confirmed against a fetched non-Wikipedia source — mark as approximate/needs-further-verification rather than firmly established.

#### 3.2 The Homeric Question (neutral treatment)
- **Candidates:** Troy VI (1750–1300 BCE) — the level with monumental fortifications, the most visually striking remains, but ending in what appears to be an earthquake rather than a siege/war. Troy VIIa (1300–1180 BCE) — a somewhat lower-quality rebuild showing signs of overcrowding (subdivided housing, storage pithoi sunk into house floors, suggesting a population bracing for siege) and a violent destruction horizon that Carl Blegen's excavations (dated by him to c. 1250 BCE) argued matches the Homeric war narrative better despite the level's less "grand" architecture.
- **Status:** This remains a genuinely open scholarly question with no consensus resolution — present both candidates without declaring a winner. The identification of any Trojan War with a specific historical event, as opposed to a poetic/mythological composite, is itself a separate and equally unresolved question in classical scholarship.
- **Sources:** [World History Encyclopedia, "The Discovery of Troy"](https://www.worldhistory.org/article/2196/discovery-of-troy/) (Blegen's 1250 BCE dating, search-located); [Oxford Academic, Anatolian Chronology chapter](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/36332/chapter/318715466?searchresult=1) (ceramic-seriation basis for VIIa destruction window).
- **Flags:** Treat as an unresolved debate, per the sourcing rules — do not present either Troy VI or VIIa as the confirmed "real" Homeric Troy.

### 4. ⚑ DENDROCHRONOLOGY AND RADIOCARBON IN ANATOLIA — MAJOR LITERATURE-MOVED STORY

This is the most heavily-revised dating question in the Anatolian Iron Age and should be flagged prominently wherever downstream dates (Gordion, Midas Mound, Phrygian chronology) depend on it.

- **1996 baseline:** The original East Mediterranean tree-ring (dendrochronological) sequence was published by Kuniholm et al., spanning roughly 2220–718 cal BCE, built from 22 central Anatolian archaeological sites.
- **Ongoing instability:** A 2020 PNAS paper (Pearson et al.) states explicitly that the Gordion portion of this sequence has been "subsequently redated multiple times" — i.e., this is not a single one-time correction but a recurring pattern in the literature.
- **2010 revision (Manning et al., published in *Radiocarbon*):** Used decadal-block radiocarbon "wiggle-matching" against the IntCal09/IntCal13 calibration curves, placing the sequence's end-date at **751 BCE, +6/−8 years at 95.4% confidence**.
- **2020 revision (Pearson et al., PNAS):** Used a new method — annual (not decadal) ¹⁴C measurements on 186 consecutive tree rings, wiggle-matched to IntCal13, narrowing the end-date range to **758–751 BCE**; a further chi-squared comparison against independently-measured bristlecone-pine and Irish-oak radiocarbon datasets refined this to **745 ± 4 BCE (95.4% confidence)**, described by the authors as "significantly more reliable" than the prior IntCal13-only wiggle-match. An independent test — superposed epoch analysis (SEA) of known bristlecone-pine frost-ring years — supported the accuracy of this date to within one year.
- **2020 dispute (Manning critique and Pearson et al. reply):** Manning published a critique of the 2020 Pearson et al. result (referenced in the reply but not independently fetched in this pass). Pearson et al. published a direct reply, explicitly titled **"Dating of the Gordion tree-ring sequence still stands within a year of 745 BC."** In it, the authors acknowledge that "any calibrated result is subject to change if new data are added or new iterations of the calibration curve... are used," and explicitly note the pattern of "repeated redating of parts of the Anatolian sequence" as a known feature of this research area — but after removing the disputed tree-ring segment and rerunning the SEA analysis, report the result as "nearly identical," with the "final dating... again, unchanged" at 745 BCE.
- **⚑ LITERATURE MOVED — flag year: 2020.** The Gordion dendrochronological end-date shifted from the earlier ~751 BCE decadal wiggle-match (Manning 2010) to a more precise 745 ± 4 BCE annual ¹⁴C match (Pearson et al. 2020), with an active, methodologically-detailed Manning-vs-Pearson dispute over 2020 that both authors treat as resolved in Pearson's favor as of the reply, while acknowledging the field's history of repeated re-dating. This directly affects the absolute dating of the Midas Mound Tumulus (see Section 2.3) and, by extension, Phrygian royal chronology.
- **Second Anatolian radiocarbon-shift example (Middle Bronze Age):** A 2023 Cambridge/*Antiquity* paper redates the destruction of Zincirli using new radiocarbon evidence to **c. 1632–1610 BCE** — relevant to Middle Bronze Age Syro-Anatolian chronology and illustrating that radiocarbon-driven revision in this region is not limited to the Iron Age Gordion case.
- **Sources:** [PNAS, Pearson et al. 2020](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1917445117) (fetched); [PMC, Pearson et al., "Reply to Manning: Dating of Gordion tree-ring sequence still stands within a year of 745 BC"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7414178/) (fetched); [Cambridge/*Antiquity*, "New evidence for Middle Bronze Age chronology from the Syro-Anatolian frontier" (Zincirli)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/014ABB912CC50181E0F06E074BE071A2/S0003598X23000303a.pdf/new_evidence_for_middle_bronze_age_chronology_from_the_syroanatolian_frontier.pdf) (search-located, not independently fetched — cite with appropriate caution on exact figures).

### 5. DILMUN AND MAGAN (Bronze Age Gulf)

#### 5.1 Dilmun (Bahrain-centered)
- **Dates (revised periodization):**
  - Earliest Mesopotamian textual mention: **c. 3300 BCE** (Bahrain Authority for Culture and Antiquities).
  - "Tribal Dilmun" phase (low-hierarchy chiefdoms): **2250–2050 BCE**.

### 6. SABA/SHEBA AND SOUTH ARABIAN KINGDOMS — LONG VS. SHORT CHRONOLOGY

#### 6.1 The Chronology Dispute Itself
- **First absolutely reliable anchor date for South Arabia:** Aelius Gallus's Roman military campaign, **25 BCE**, with a king named Ilasaros mentioned in connection with it. This is the one point on which the competing chronologies agree.
- **Long Chronology:** Originated in the late 19th century with Eduard Glaser and Fritz Hommel, who dated Old South Arabian civilization to the late 2nd millennium BCE. Later refined by A.G. Lundin and Hermann von Wissmann using the Marib rock inscription known as "Glaser 1703," dating the beginning of Saba to the 12th or 8th century BCE. Their specific interpretation was later shown to be partially incorrect, but the Long Chronology gained further support from Italian excavations at Yala/Hafari and French excavations at Shabwah. Per the fetched source: **"the majority of experts in Sabaean studies adhere to [von] Wissmann's Long Chronology."**
- **Short Chronology:** Proposed by Jacqueline Pirenne in 1955, based on comparison of Old South Arabian and Greek art styles plus paleographic analysis of letter forms, concluding that South Arabian civilization first developed in the 5th century BCE under Greek influence. Status: not definitively disproven but losing ground — "more recent arguments have been brought against it."
- **Intermediary Chronology:** Emerged from American excavations at Timnah and Ma'rib in 1951–52, placing the beginnings of Qataban and Ma'in later than the Long Chronology proposes but earlier than the Short Chronology.
- **Sources:** [Brewminate, "A History of the South Arabian Kingdoms of Ancient Yemen"](https://brewminate.com/a-history-of-the-south-arabian-kingdoms-of-ancient-yemen/) (fetched; journalistic in format but detailed, names specific scholars, and explicitly adopts the Long Chronology for its own subsequent date reporting — flagged accordingly below); [Wikipedia, "Ancient South Arabian"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_South_Arabian) (fetched — **locator only**), which corroborates: "dates... follow the commonly used long chronology," with the earliest Sabaic phase "often dated to the eighth century BCE under the long chronology, though some material may be earlier," and the latest securely dated Sabaic inscription at 559 CE.
- **Flags:** ⚑ Live, unresolved scholarly dispute — present both Long and Short Chronology positions with their standing (majority vs. minority) rather than averaging. All specific kingdom date-ranges below (Section 6.2) are reported under the Long Chronology, per the majority-position sourcing available; this must be stated explicitly wherever those figures are used downstream.

#### 6.3 Marib Dam
- **Construction date:** "c. 700 BCE" is the traditional inscriptional attribution (Karib'il Watar era) per the Long-Chronology-adopting Brewminate source, but **no independently fetched scientific (radiocarbon/sediment) dating result confirms a specific construction year** for the dam itself — mark the precise construction year as **n.a.** pending better sourcing, while noting the traditional inscriptional attribution above with its basis stated.
- **Related, independently confirmed scientific date:** A fetched geoarchaeological paper (Francaviglia, published via Elsevier/Academia.edu) reports **AMS radiocarbon dating of detrital charcoal fragments in silty sediments** from the ancient Ma'rib basin. Result: the final period of activity of the dam is dated to **no earlier than the beginning of the Christian era (c. 1 CE) and no later than the end of the 3rd century CE**. The paper notes this radiocarbon-based result explicitly **disagrees with historical/traditional sources**, which place the dam's final collapse much later (commonly cited elsewhere, but not independently confirmed in this pass, as 570 CE, with partial failures reported in the literature around 449/450 and 542/548 CE). Separately, the same paper's ¹⁴C analysis confirms the earliest irrigation systems in the adjoining Wadi Dhana probably date to the **late 4th millennium BCE or early 3rd millennium BCE** — much earlier than the dam structure itself, reflecting a long history of hydraulic activity at the site predating the monumental dam.
- **Dating basis:** For construction — textual/inscriptional (n.a. for independent confirmation). For final-activity sediments — **AMS radiocarbon dating of detrital charcoal**, a direct scientific method.
- **Significance:** The Marib Dam was a major Sabaean hydraulic engineering achievement; its eventual failure is remembered in later Arabian/Quranic tradition (the "Flood of the Dam," Sayl al-'Arim) as marking the end of South Arabian prosperity.
- **Sources:** [Francaviglia, "Dating the Ancient Dam of Ma'rib (Yemen)," via Academia.edu/Elsevier](https://www.academia.edu/57427411/Dating_the_Ancient_Dam_of_Marib_Yemen_) (fetched).
- **Flags:** ⚑ Direct conflict between the radiocarbon-dated final-activity window (1st–3rd century CE, scientific) and the commonly-cited traditional historical collapse date (570 CE, textual/legendary) — present both without averaging; the fetched paper itself frames this as a disagreement between physical/sedimentary evidence and historical sources. The precise construction year and precise historical failure year are both **n.a.** for direct fetched-source confirmation in this pass.

#### 6.4 Himyar

### 7. THE NABATAEANS AND PETRA

#### 7.1 Earliest Attestation and Founding
- **Earliest reference to the Nabataean monarchy:** The earliest known Nabataean inscription, from Elusa/Halutza in the Negev, reads "This is the place which Nuthairu made for the life of Aretas, king of the Nabataeans." Dated on **paleographic grounds by the Abbé (Joseph) Starcky** to the **first half of the 2nd century BCE**, and specifically to **168 BCE** per multiple corroborating sources (Jewish Virtual Library, Wikipedia's "Aretas I" entry, French Wikipedia) that identify the mentioned king as Aretas I. A directly fetched museum-affiliated academic PDF (Joseph Naveh, Hecht Museum/University of Haifa) confirms: "The earliest Nabatean inscription, found at Elusa in the Negev, mentions a certain 'Aretas, King of the Nabataeans'... dated to the first half of the 2nd century BCE, and the king mentioned should be identified with Aretas I, described in 2 Macc. 5:8 as the Arab ruler with whom Jason sought refuge in Petra in 169 BCE." This same fetched source lists subsequent dated Nabataean inscriptions: the Aslah inscription from Petra (**95 BCE**), the dedication to Al-Kutba from Wadi Tumilat (**77 BCE**), the inscription of Rabbel I from Petra (**66 BCE**), and an even earlier cursive text from Horvat Raqiq, dated to around **100 BCE** — though this last one postdates the Elusa inscription and is cited only as the earliest known *cursive* (not monumental) Nabataean script.
- **Earlier possible-but-unconfirmed references:** Assyrian records from the 8th–6th centuries BCE mention Aramaic/Arab peoples called "Nabatu," "Nabaieteans," or "Na-ba-a-a-ti"; the Old Testament mentions the "Nabaioth"; an inscription from Tayma mentions the "nbyt" people. Per a fetched source, "most scholars now agree that none of these ancient references can be identified explicitly with the Nabataeans of Petra" — flag this as a common point of false conflation.
- **First fixed historical reference to Nabataeans specifically at Petra:** **312 BCE** — Diodorus Siculus's *Bibliotheca* (1st century BCE, based on eyewitness accounts of Hieronymus of Cardia, a Greek officer active in the region at the end of the 4th century BCE) records that Antigonus's general Athenaeus attacked "the Nabataean Arabs" at a "strong but unwalled rock," conventionally identified with Umm al-Biyara at Petra, with an assault force of 4,000 infantry and 600 cavalry.
- **General settlement consensus:** "The generally accepted view is that they [the Nabataeans] first moved into the land of Edom during the 6th–4th centuries BCE, elbowed out the Edomites, and established their base at Petra by the 4th century BCE" — i.e., there was "certainly... a Nabataean settlement at Petra by the end of the 4th century BCE."
- **Dating basis:** Paleographic/epigraphic (letter-form dating of inscriptions by Starcky) and textual (Diodorus Siculus via Hieronymus of Cardia).
- **Sources:** [University of Haifa/Hecht Museum, Joseph Naveh, "Nabatean Language, Script and Inscriptions" PDF](https://mushecht2.haifa.ac.il/images/catalogues/archeology/Nabateans/Joseph_Naveh.pdf) (fetched-quality academic/museum source, search-located with detailed content); [Jewish Virtual Library, "The Nabatean Kings"](https://jewishvirtuallibrary.org/jsource/History/nabkings.html); [Wikipedia, "Aretas I"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aretas_I) (locator only); [bible.ca, Rami G. Khouri, "Petra: A Guide to the Capital of the Nabataeans"](https://www.bible.ca/archeology/bible-archeology-exodus-kadesh-barnea-petra-rami-g-khouri-petra-a-guide-to-the-capital-of-the-nabataeans-1986ad.htm) (fetched; weaker site but content is a reprint of a named archaeologist's guide, useful for the Diodorus/312 BCE detail and the full royal chronology below); [Oxford Academic-adjacent Cà Foscari (Edizioni Ca' Foscari) open-access PDF, "Corpus of Nabataean Aramaic-Greek Inscriptions"](https://edizionicafoscari.unive.it/media/pdf/books/978-88-6969-508-7/978-88-6969-508-7_jFP74xP.pdf) (peer-reviewed academic press, search-located).

### 8. DROMEDARY CAMEL DOMESTICATION AND THE INCENSE TRADE

#### 8.1 Camel Domestication — Active Scholarly Disagreement
- **Wild camel presence (not domesticated), Arabia:**
  - Radiocarbon-dated wild camel bones at Baynunah, Abu Dhabi: **5th millennium BCE**.
  - Radiocarbon-dated camel remains at Ra's al-Hadd (site HD-6), Oman: **2890–2580 BCE**, described in the source journal as the oldest camel evidence in the Sultanate of Oman, but explicitly not necessarily indicating domestication.
  - A Red Sea coastal Saudi Arabian dromedary mandible with a calibrated radiocarbon date of roughly **7100–7200 BCE** is widely regarded in the literature as either a statistical outlier or evidence of wild camel presence, not domestication.
- **General scholarly consensus on domestication timing (Southeast Arabia):** Per a fetched academic PDF, citing Uerpmann & Uerpmann (2002), Horwitz & Rosen (2005), and Heide (2010): "most scholars today agree that the dromedary was exploited as a pack animal sometime in the early Iron Age (not before the 12th century BCE)," with the domestic dromedary probably appearing in southeast Arabia "sometime in the first third of the 1st millennium BCE" (i.e., roughly 1000–900 BCE).
- **Levantine introduction — directly radiocarbon-dated, and a confirmed literature shift:**
  - A fetched academic PDF ("The Introduction of Domestic Camels to the Southern Levant") reports a direct AMS radiocarbon measurement on a camel bone from Timna, Site 30: laboratory code **OxA-2165**, conventional radiocarbon age **2650 ± 90 BP**, calibrated (OxCal 4.2, per Ramsey 1995 calibration software) to **969–600 BCE at 1σ** (citing Grigson 2012).
  - The same source's broader conclusion, based on stratified faunal assemblages and site-wide radiocarbon dating (not a single measurement): "the first significant appearance of camels in the Aravah Valley was not earlier than the last third of the 10th century BCE," most probably occurring at that time — i.e., roughly **933–900 BCE**.
  - This finding explicitly **overturns an earlier, incorrect dating** of Timna Site 30 that had placed domestic camel introduction "as early as the 13th century BCE (Late Bronze Age)." The revised dating shows Site 30's camel bones actually belong to its **last occupation phase (Layer I, late 10th–9th centuries BCE)**, not to earlier Late Bronze Age layers as previously assumed.
  - This appearance coincides with a major reorganization of Arabah copper-smelting technology and labor, tentatively linked to the campaign of Pharaoh Shoshenq I and/or its aftermath, and supports "low chronology" dates for the early Iron IIA period.
  - Related textual evidence: Shalmaneser III's Kurkh Stela (Assyrian, **9th century BCE**) records King Gindibu of Arabia contributing 1,000 camel-riders to the Battle of Qarqar — the earliest specific textual attestation of organized camel cavalry in the region.
- **Sources:** [Squarespace-hosted academic PDF, "The Introduction of Domestic Camels to the Southern Levant"](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/54694fa6e4b0eaec4530f99d/t/65493ec2a05a17007b8c2074/1699299011442/The_Introduction_of_Domestic_Camels_to_t.pdf) (fetched — contains the direct OxA-2165 radiocarbon measurement and full site-by-site stratigraphic data); [Anthropozoologica journal (Muséum national d'Histoire naturelle), Ra's al-Hadd HD-6 paper](https://sciencepress.mnhn.fr/en/periodiques/anthropozoologica/49/2/les-restes-de-chameaux-du-site-de-hd-6-ra-s-al-hadd-sultanat-d-oman-une-occasion-pour-une-revision-critique-des-decouvertes-de-dromadaire-en-arabie-orientale) (peer-reviewed, search-located); [Academia.edu, "Prehistoric camels in south-eastern Arabia... Abu Dhabi's Western Region"](https://www.academia.edu/4345377/Prehistoric_camels_in_south_eastern_Arabia_the_discovery_of_a_new_site_in_Abu_Dhabi_s_Western_Region_United_Arab_Emirates) (search-located, Baynunah 5th-millennium data); [Missouri State BearWorks repository (Grigson)](https://bearworks.missouristate.edu/articles-chpa/823/) (search-located, Red Sea coast outlier date).
- **Flags:** ⚑ LITERATURE MOVED — the Timna Site 30 re-dating (from an assumed 13th-century-BCE/Late-Bronze-Age domestication date down to a confirmed late-10th–9th-century-BCE date) is a direct, source-documented correction of a previously widely-cited but incorrect stratigraphic attribution; this is distinct from (but related to) the separately-reported Sapir-Hen & Ben-Yosef (Tel Aviv University) Timna Valley redating discussed in prior research passes, which used high-precision radiocarbon plus archaeomagnetic dating of smelting slag. ⚑ COMMON MISCONCEPTION — do not conflate "wild camel presence in Arabia" (documented from the 5th–3rd millennia BCE via bones only, not domestication evidence) with "domestication," which most current literature places no earlier than the early 1st millennium BCE in southeast Arabia and specifically c. 933–900 BCE for Levantine introduction as a pack/transport animal.

#### 8.2 The Incense Trade Route
- **Overland incense trade route (frankincense and myrrh) flourishing period:** Conventionally dated as flourishing "between roughly the 3rd century BCE and the 2nd century CE" (Wikipedia — **locator only**, not independently re-confirmed against a non-Wikipedia source in this pass for this specific framing).
- **Earlier origin — counter to the above:** A Jordanian Department of Antiquities conference-proceedings PDF states the north–south frankincense/myrrh route "had been in use since at least the seventh century BCE," citing Groom (1981), with an alternative view (Singer-Avitz 1999) pushing the origin to the 8th century BCE.
- **Direct radiocarbon evidence for early South Arabian urban/trade development:** A Japanese academic journal article (*Orient*, J-STAGE) reports a radiocarbon date for a stratum at Hajar bin Humeid (in the Beihan region) of **"851 years or 852 BCE, plus or minus an error factor of 160 years"** — i.e., a calibrated/error range of roughly **1012–692 BCE** — presented as evidence for early urban and trade development in South Arabia, consistent with (though not proof of) an incense-trade origin considerably earlier than the "3rd century BCE" figure above.
- **Dating basis:** Mixed — the "3rd century BCE–2nd century CE" flourishing window is textual/historical-consensus (locator only); the Jordanian DoA 7th/8th-century-BCE claims are also textual, citing named scholars (Groom, Singer-Avitz); the Hajar bin Humeid figure is a **direct radiocarbon measurement**.
- **Significance:** The incense trade (frankincense and myrrh, sourced from South Arabia and the Horn of Africa) was the primary economic engine underpinning the wealth of the South Arabian kingdoms (Section 6) and later the Nabataeans (Section 7), who controlled its northern overland routes into the Mediterranean world.
- **Sources:** [Jordan Department of Antiquities, SHAJ 10 conference proceedings PDF, "Nabataeans and Oriental Trade"](https://publication.doa.gov.jo/uploads/publications/25/SHAJ_10-405-412.pdf) (institutional/government antiquities department, search-located); [J-STAGE (Orient journal), "Frankincense and Myrrh"](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/orient1960/3/0/3_0_21/_pdf/-char/en) (peer-reviewed academic journal, search-located, contains the direct radiocarbon figure).
- **Flags:** ⚑ Genuine disagreement on when the overland incense route "began" in an organized/large-scale sense — present the 3rd-century-BCE flourishing-window figure alongside the considerably earlier 7th/8th-century-BCE textual claims and the radiocarbon-supported c. 1012–692 BCE Hajar bin Humeid data point, rather than resolving to one date. Note that the radiocarbon range (with its ±160-year error factor) is wide and should not be over-precisified.

## SUMMARY OF ⚑ LITERATURE MOVED FLAGS
1. **Gordion dendrochronology (2020):** End-date revised from ~751 BCE (Manning 2010, decadal wiggle-match) to 745 ± 4 BCE (Pearson et al. 2020, annual ¹⁴C match), with an active and largely-resolved-in-Pearson's-favor dispute over the same year. Affects Midas Mound Tumulus and Phrygian royal chronology.
2. **Timna Valley camel domestication:** Site 30 camel bones re-attributed from an assumed 13th-century-BCE (Late Bronze Age) layer to their correct late-10th–9th-century-BCE (Layer I) context, moving the earliest confirmed Levantine domestic-camel evidence to c. 933–900 BCE.
3. **Dilmun periodization:** The fetched Ancient Arabia dictionary source states research has "moved beyond" the older three-part Ancient/Middle/Late Dilmun scheme, though the exact year of this shift is n.a. from the fetched page.
4. **South Arabian kingdom chronology (Long vs. Short):** An ongoing, decades-long (not single-year) dispute rather than a discrete "literature moved" event — flagged as a live disagreement (Section 6.1) rather than a dated shift.

## SUMMARY OF ⚑ COMMON MISCONCEPTION FLAGS
1. Hattusa was **not** violently sacked/burned at the Hittite collapse — current DAI evidence shows deliberate emptying and likely planned relocation.
2. The "sack of Babylon" date (1595 BCE) is a **Middle Chronology figure only** — five other competing chronology schemes place it between 1499 and 1736 BCE.
3. The Midas Mound Tumulus at Gordion is **not Midas's own tomb** — dendrochronological dating (~740 BCE) predates Midas's confirmed lifespan (still alive in 709 BCE per Assyrian records).
4. Croesus did **not** invent coinage — his father Alyattes issued electrum coins c. 650–630 BCE; Croesus's innovation was the bimetallic gold/silver standard.
5. Ancient Assyrian "Nabatu"/biblical "Nabaioth" references are **not** the historical Petra Nabataeans — this identification is rejected by current scholarly consensus.
6. Wild camel bone finds in Arabia (5th–3rd millennia BCE) are **not** evidence of domestication — domestication (as a working pack animal) is dated no earlier than the early 1st millennium BCE by most current literature.
