# Central Asian and Steppe Polities — Research Notes

Companion research for entries missing from the existing history dataset (which already covers Scythians, Xiongnu, Kushan, First Turkic Khaganate, Samanid, Seljuk, Mongol Empire, Timurid, Chagatai Khanate, Golden Horde, Ilkhanate). Each entry below gives dates with justifying events, native/contemporary name and script where verifiable, capital, ruling people, territory, sourcing, and — where relevant — an explicit note on disputed identity, unity, or chronology, and on exonyms.

---

## 1. SAKA

**This is primarily a naming/identity question, not a clean dating one.**

### Identity: are the Saka "the Scythians," a branch of them, or a Persian exonym?

- **"Saka" is not an ethnonym the group necessarily used for itself in a unified sense — it is best understood as a Persian umbrella term.** Old Persian royal inscriptions (Achaemenid) used *Sakā* as a blanket label for Iranian-speaking steppe nomads generally, subdividing them by epithet rather than by self-name: *Sakā tigraxaudā* ("pointed-cap Saka"), *Sakā haumavargā* ("haoma-drinking Saka"), and *Sakā paradraya* ("Saka beyond the sea/Black Sea" — the group Greeks called Scythians) ([Wikipedia, "Saka"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saka); [Achaemenica, "The Saka (Scythians)"](https://achaemenica.org/articles/the-saka-scythians)).
- **"Scythian" is the Greek exonym for the same broad family of related Iranian nomadic groups**, but in modern scholarly convention has narrowed to mean specifically the western/Pontic-steppe branch (Ukraine/southern Russia, 9th–3rd century BCE), while "Saka" is reserved in modern usage for the eastern branch (Central Asian steppe, Tarim Basin, roughly 9th century BCE–5th century CE) ([Wikipedia, "Saka"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saka); [Wikipedia, "Scythians"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scythians)).
- **Ancient sources did not draw this east/west line themselves** — Herodotus explicitly states the Persian name "Sacae" was applied by Persians to what Greeks called "Scythians" generally, including some groups Greeks would call "Amyrgian Scythians" ([Achaemenica](https://achaemenica.org/articles/the-saka-scythians)). The 1911 *Encyclopædia Britannica* article on "Saka" takes a more skeptical position, arguing the Indian-invading Saka "cannot be the same as the Scythians of Europe, though the name and original nomadic life are points in common," and leaves open whether the Saka were "Turanians" or "Iranians" ([1911 Britannica via Wikisource, "Saka"](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/Saka)).
- **Whether "Saka" was ever a self-designation is explicitly unresolved** — a recent secondary account states plainly: "We do not know if it was a self-designation or a name given to them by neighboring peoples" ([Astana Times, "Ancient Sakas: All You Need to Know"](https://astanatimes.com/2024/07/ancient-sakas-all-you-need-to-know/)).
- Modern scholarly consensus (reflected in Wikipedia's synthesis of the field) treats Saka, Scythians, and Cimmerians as **"closely related nomadic Iranic peoples"** — parts of a broader "Scythian culture" continuum rather than three cleanly separate peoples, with Cimmerian/Saka/Scythian representing Babylonian/Persian/Greek naming choices respectively for overlapping or adjacent populations ([Wikipedia, "Saka"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saka)).

**Bottom line for the dataset:** treat "Saka" as **the Persian exonym for the eastern Iranian steppe nomads**, cognate in function to the Greek exonym "Scythian" used for the western branch — not evidence of a separate, unified "Saka nation." The two names cover overlapping, related Iranian-speaking nomadic populations; whether they were ethnically identical, closely related branches, or a loose family of tribal groups lumped together by outside empires is not settled, and this should be flagged as a naming/identity dispute rather than resolved.

### Dates
- Conventionally dated **9th century BCE to 5th century CE** as an eastern Iranian nomadic milieu, per modern synthesis ([Wikipedia, "Saka"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saka)). These are not "reign" dates for a state — the Saka were never a single polity (see territory/capital note below).
- Two major Saka tribal confederations are attested from the 6th–5th centuries BCE: Saka Tigrahauda ("pointed-cap," per Persian sources) and Saka Haumavarga ("haoma-drinking") ([Open.kg, "Saks in World History"](https://open.kg/en/about-kyrgyzstan/history/kyrgyz-in-antiquity/print:page,1,418-saki-v-mirovoy-istorii-borba-za-nezavisimost.html)).
- Saka rule over parts of Central Asia is described as ending roughly in the mid-2nd century BCE when Yuezhi/Wusun migrations displaced them, driving groups south into Bactria, Sistan ("Sakastan," a toponym derived from the name), Gandhara, and northwest India, culminating in the Indo-Scythian kingdoms (from c. 150–130 BCE) ([SCIRP journal, "Nomadic Governments of Central Asia"](https://www.scirp.org/journal/paperinformation?paperid=138636); [Wikipedia, "Indo-Scythian Kingdom"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Scythian_Kingdom)).

### Capital, ruling people, territory
- **No single capital or unified state** — the Saka were organized as multiple nomadic/semi-nomadic tribal confederations, not a centralized kingdom ([Wikipedia, "Saka"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saka)).
- Ruling people: Iranian-speaking pastoral nomadic tribes (Eastern Iranic).
- Territory: the Eurasian steppe and Tarim Basin, spanning parts of modern Kazakhstan (Jetysu/Semirechye), Kyrgyzstan (Tian Shan), Uzbekistan, Tajikistan (Pamir, Fergana), Turkmenistan (Khorezm), Xinjiang (Tarim Basin/Kashgar area), and later Afghanistan, Sistan, and northwest India/Pakistan as groups migrated south ([Astana Times](https://astanatimes.com/2024/07/ancient-sakas-all-you-need-to-know/); [Wikipedia, "Saka"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saka)).

## 2. SOGDIA / SOGDIANA

### Was it ever a unified independent kingdom? — No.

Multiple independent academic and encyclopedic sources agree explicitly that **Sogdiana was never a unified political state**:

- "Sogdiana was never unified until it was governed by the Samanid dynasty in the late 9th century" ([Oxford Research Encyclopedia of Asian History, "The Sogdian Merchant Network"](https://oxfordre.com/asianhistory/display/10.1093/acrefore/9780190277727.001.0001/acrefore-9780190277727-e-491)).
- "There was never a unified Sogdian empire or kingdom" ([Oxford Academic / edited volume, "Sogdians in Their Homeland"](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61799/chapter/546293088)).
- "The Sogdian city-states, although never politically united, were centred on the city of Samarkand" ([Wikipedia, "Sogdia"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sogdia)).
- The Smithsonian's dedicated Sogdian exhibition project states: "They had no empire, and their political organization at home in Sogdiana was a series of small principalities, each with its own leader" ([Smithsonian Institution, "Who Were the Sogdians"](https://sogdians.si.edu/introduction/)).

**Represented honestly here as a network of city-states, not a kingdom.**

### Political structure
- Sogdiana consisted of a shifting set of small oasis principalities, each with its own local ruler (title *ikhshid*), the leading ones being **Samarkand** and **Bukhara**, with other centers at Kesh (Shahrisabz), Khujand, and Panjikent ([EBSCO Research Starters, "Sogdiana"](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/sogdiana); [Oxford Research Encyclopedia](https://oxfordre.com/asianhistory/display/10.1093/acrefore/9780190277727.001.0001/acrefore-9780190277727-e-491)).
- Sogdiana was, for most of its history, under the suzerainty of larger surrounding empires — Achaemenid Persia (18th satrapy per the Behistun inscription), then Alexander's empire and Hellenistic successors, Kushans, Hephthalites, First/Western Turkic Khaganate, before falling to the Arab conquest of Transoxiana in the early 8th century and eventual absorption into the Samanid state by the late 9th century ([New World Encyclopedia, "Sogdiana"](https://www.newworldencyclopedia.org/entry/Sogdiana); [Wikipedia, "Muslim conquest of Transoxiana"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_conquest_of_Transoxiana)).
- Native/contemporary name: **Sughd** (Old Persian *Sughuda*; Sogdian: *Suγd*; Persian سغد; Chinese 粟特 *Sùtè*; Tajik Суғд) — this is a native-derived regional name, not an exonym ([New World Encyclopedia, "Sogdiana"](https://www.newworldencyclopedia.org/entry/Sogdiana)). Sogdian was written in the Sogdian script, an Aramaic-derived alphasyllabary.

### Approximate dates as a documented civilization
- First attested as an Achaemenid province from the 6th century BCE (Behistun Inscription of Darius I) through roughly the 11th century CE, when Sogdian distinctiveness faded following Turkicization and Islamization of the region ([New World Encyclopedia, "Sogdiana"](https://www.newworldencyclopedia.org/entry/Sogdiana)).
- Peak of the Sogdian merchant network and greatest political/cultural prominence: roughly **5th–8th centuries CE**, ending with the Arab conquest of Transoxiana (completed by the mid-8th century) and disruption of the eastern trade network by the An Lushan Rebellion (755 CE) in Tang China ([Oxford Research Encyclopedia](https://oxfordre.com/asianhistory/display/10.1093/acrefore/9780190277727.001.0001/acrefore-9780190277727-e-491); [Facts and Details, "Sogdians: Merchants of the Silk Road"](https://factsanddetails.com/central-asia/Central_Asian_Topics/sub8_8a/entry-4498.html)).

### Territory
Between the Amu Darya and Syr Darya rivers, centered on the Zarafshan valley: modern Uzbekistan (Samarkand, Bukhara regions) and Tajikistan's Sughd province, with the merchant network extending trade colonies as far as Byzantium, India, and China ([Wikipedia, "Sogdia"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sogdia); [Smithsonian, "Who Were the Sogdians"](https://sogdians.si.edu/introduction/)).

### Ruling people
Iranian-speaking Sogdians; individual city-states ruled by local kings/lords (e.g., the Bukhar Khudas of Bukhara, a local Sogdian Zoroastrian dynasty, 681–890 CE) ([Wikipedia, "List of Sogdian states"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sogdian_city-states)).

## 3. HEPHTHALITES (White Huns)

### Dates and justifying events
- **c. 440s CE** — Hephthalites appear as a distinct, powerful force in Bactria; conventional start of the "Hephthalite Empire" phase. (Some sources place the group's first appearance as early as the mid-4th century as part of a "second wave" of Hunnic invasion following the Chionites; the *imperial* phase is dated later, c. 440s.) ([Wikipedia, "Hephthalites"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hephthalites); [Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Hephthalites"](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hephthalites/)).
- **484 CE** — Hephthalites kill the Sasanian king Peroz I in battle, the high-water mark of their power over Sasanian Iran ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Hephthalites"](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hephthalites/)).
- **c. 510 CE** — Hephthalite ruler Toramana establishes power over much of northern and western India ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Hephthalites"](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hephthalites/)).
- **558–561 CE** — A Sasanian–Türk alliance (Khosrow I with the newly arrived First Turkic Khaganate under Khagan Sinjibu/Istämi) crushes the Hephthalite empire near Bukhara; their territory is partitioned along the Oxus/Amu Darya between the Sasanians and the Turks. This is the conventional **end date, c. 560**, though sources vary between 557–567 for the final defeat ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Hephthalites"](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hephthalites/); [Wikipedia, "Hephthalites"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hephthalites)).
- Remnant Hephthalite principalities persisted as local rulers in Tokharistan and the Hindu Kush under Western Turk/Tokhara Yabghu suzerainty until roughly **710 CE**, and possibly-related groups (e.g., the Khalaj) are attested even later, though Iranica stresses this later connection is a claim, not an established fact ([Wikipedia, "Hephthalites"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hephthalites); [Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Hephthalites"](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hephthalites/)).

### Dispute: one people or a confederation? Origins?
This is explicitly and extensively disputed in the literature — **do not resolve it**:

- **Encyclopaedia Iranica states plainly that the Hephthalites' precise ethnic origin is not established.** Chinese sources disagree among themselves: some say they descended from Ch'e-shih (Turfan), others from "descendants of K'ang-chü" in southern Kazakhstan, still others from the Great Yuezhi ([UNESCO, "The Hephthalite Empire"](https://unesdoc.unesco.org/ark:/48223/pf0000119001); [Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Hephthalites"](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hephthalites/)).
- **Iranica explicitly separates the Hephthalites from the earlier "Kidarite" Hunnic wave**, noting Procopius called the Hephthalites "entirely distinct from the other Huns," while also noting that Indian sources do **not** clearly distinguish Kidarites from Hephthalites, lumping both under "Huna" ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Hephthalites"](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hephthalites/)).
- **Whether "Hephthalite" names a single ethnic people or a political/military confederation of multiple groups is unresolved.** Iranica notes the related term "Kidarite" was "evidently a political rather than an ethnic grouping" and does not commit to a single verdict for "Hephthalite" either; other sources call the Hephthalites "a nomadic confederation" or "multi-ethnic" ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Hephthalites"](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hephthalites/); [HistoryData, "Hephthalites"](https://historydata.com/empires/hephthalites)).
- **Linguistic/ethnic affiliation (Iranian vs. Turkic vs. Tocharian/other) is actively disputed.** Iranica states the Bactrian language (Middle Iranian, Greek script) used in Hephthalite administrative documents was "not the native idiom of the Hephthalites" but rather an inherited regional administrative language, while noting "some evidence" of Turkish royal titles among the Hephthalites — without resolving which (if either) reflects their native tongue ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Hephthalites"](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hephthalites/)). German Wikipedia's academic synthesis states the dominant current view links Hephthalites most closely to Tocharians and/or Iranians, but calls their ethnic and linguistic origin "not sufficiently researched" ([German Wikipedia, "Hephthaliten"](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hephthaliten)). Other scholarship (e.g., Enoki, cited in a Freie Universität Berlin dissertation) argues for descent from a Rouran tribe called "Hua" from the Turfan (Qeshi) region ([Refubium/FU Berlin, "The Hephthalites"](https://refubium.fu-berlin.de/bitstream/handle/fub188/8366/01_Text.pdf)).
- **"Hephthalite" itself is likely not a self-designation preserved intact** — Bactrian documents render it as *hbodalo* / *ηβοδαλο* (romanized *Ebodalo*), and the term "White Huns" is explicitly a Greek/Byzantine exonym coined by the historian Procopius, not the group's own name ([Wikipedia, "Hephthalites"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hephthalites); [Refubium/FU Berlin](https://refubium.fu-berlin.de/bitstream/handle/fub188/8366/01_Text.pdf): "the name they used themselves is unknown"). Sanskrit/Prakrit sources call them *Sveta-huna* ("White Huna"), an Indian rendering of the same "white" epithet, and Arabic-Persian sources call them *Hayāṭela* ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Hunnic Coinage"](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hunnic-coinage)).

### Capital
No single, universally agreed capital. Likely **Kunduz** (also called Walwalij; known to 11th-century scholar al-Biruni as War-Walīz) in Tokharistan for the imperial phase; Balkh is also cited by some sources (Xuanzang described it as their capital); Iranica notes competing candidates for a later Afghan-based Hephthalite center including Kabul, Bamiyan, Gardez, and "most probably" Ghazni ([Wikipedia, "Hephthalites"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hephthalites); [Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Hephthalites"](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hephthalites/); [War History, "Hephthalite Huns"](https://warhistory.org/@msw/article/hephthalite-huns)).

### Territory
At their height (c. 500s CE): Tokharistan (northern Afghanistan/southern Uzbekistan) as core stronghold, extending into Sogdiana/Transoxiana, parts of the Tarim Basin, eastern Khorasan, and much of northern/western India ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Hephthalites"](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hephthalites/); [HistoryData, "Hephthalites"](https://historydata.com/empires/hephthalites)).

### Ruling people
Uncertain/disputed (see above) — administratively used Bactrian (Middle Iranian in Greek script), with evidence of Turkish royal titles also present.

## 4. FIRST TURKIC KHAGANATE'S SPLIT (581/603) and SECOND TURKIC KHAGANATE (682–744)

**Note for the dataset:** the existing entry "First Turkic Khaganate 552–744" conflates three distinct polities recognized separately in the literature: the unified First Turkic Khaganate (552–581/603), the separate Eastern and Western Turkic Khaganates (581/603–630/657, under varying degrees of Tang overlordship after their respective conquests), and the Second Turkic Khaganate (682–744), a distinct re-founding after a Tang-ruled interregnum.

### The split (581–603)
- The unified First Turkic Khaganate (founded 552 by Bumin Qaghan of the Ashina clan) began fracturing after the death of Taspar Qaghan in **581**, amid succession disputes and civil war (the "Göktürk civil war") ([Wikipedia, "Eastern Turkic Khaganate"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Turkic_Khaganate); [Wikipedia, "Göktürk civil war"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%B6kt%C3%BCrk_civil_war)).
- Sources place the **definitive, final separation into Eastern and Western Turkic Khaganates in 603**, after a brief reunification under Tardu around 599–603 collapsed ([Wikipedia, "Eastern Turkic Khaganate"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Turkic_Khaganate); [Wikipedia, "Göktürk civil war"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%B6kt%C3%BCrk_civil_war); [chinaknowledge.de, "Tujue 突厥, Gök Türks"](http://chinaknowledge.de/History/Altera/turks.html)). Some sources cite 581 as the split date and 603 as its confirmation — **the exact year (581 vs. 603) is treated inconsistently across sources** and should be flagged as approximate/contested rather than a single hard date.
- **Eastern Turkic Khaganate** (Chinese: 東突厥, *Dōng Tūjué*): centered in Mongolia, capital at Ötüken; conquered by the Tang dynasty in **630** ([Wikipedia, "Eastern Turkic Khaganate"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Turkic_Khaganate)).
- **Western Turkic Khaganate** ("Ten Arrow people," Old Turkic self-reference): centered in Central Asia/Zhetysu, capital traditionally Suyab; conquered by the Tang in **657** ([Wikipedia, "Western Turkic Khaganate"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Turkic_Khaganate)).

### Second Turkic Khaganate (682–744)
- **Start: 682** — Ashina Qutlugh (posthumously titled Ilterish Qaghan) leads a successful revolt against Tang overlordship, re-establishing an independent Turkic khaganate ("Kutlug's state"), initially based at Ötüken/the Ötüken forest region and Chugay mountains ([Wikipedia, "Second Turkic Khaganate"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Turkic_Khaganate); [Wikipedia, "Ilterish Qaghan"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ilterish_Qaghan)).
- **End: 744** — the Second Turkic Khaganate is overthrown by a coalition of the Uyghurs, Karluks, and Basmyls, who install Uyghur supremacy over the Mongolian steppe (leading directly to the Uyghur Khaganate, entry 5 below) ([Wikipedia, "Second Turkic Khaganate"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Turkic_Khaganate); [Alchetron, "Turkic Khaganate"](https://alchetron.com/Turkic-Khaganate)).
- Native/contemporary name and script: the polity is attested in its own **Old Turkic language, written in the Orkhon (Old Turkic) runic script**, most famously in the Orkhon inscriptions raised for Bilge Khagan, Kul Tigin, and the statesman Tonyukuk — a rare case among these entries where a genuinely native-language, native-script primary source survives ([Wikipedia, "Old Turkic script"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Turkic_script); the Tonyukuk inscription renders the state's name as 𐰃𐰠 (*el*), "the [Turkic] realm/state," per [French Wikipedia, "Second Khaganat turc"](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Khaganat_turc)).
- Capital: **Ötüken** (Orkhon valley, Mongolia) ([Wikipedia, "Second Turkic Khaganate"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Turkic_Khaganate)).
- Ruling people: Göktürks (Ashina clan of Turkic peoples); territorially organized into a western Tardush wing and eastern Tölis wing along tribal lines ([Russian Wikipedia, "Второй Восточно-тюркский каганат"](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/%D0%92%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%BE%D0%B9_%D0%92%D0%BE%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%87%D0%BD%D0%BE-%D1%82%D1%8E%D1%80%D0%BA%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B8%D0%B9_%D0%BA%D0%B0%D0%B3%D0%B0%D0%BD%D0%B0%D1%82%D0%B0%D1%82)).
- Territory: Mongolian plateau and adjacent steppe, roughly corresponding to the eastern part of the former First Turkic Khaganate.

## 5. UYGHUR KHAGANATE (744–840)

### Dates and justifying events
- **Start: 744** — the Uyghurs, allied with the Karluk and Basmyl tribes, defeat and overthrow the last ruler of the Second Turkic Khaganate; Uyghur leader Qutlugh Bilge Köl (Kutlug Bilge Kagan) establishes supremacy over the Mongolian steppe ([Simple Wikipedia, "Uyghur Khaganate"](https://simple.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uyghur_Khaganate); [Human Saga, "Uyghur Khaganate"](https://humansaga.net/empire/uyghur-khaganate)).
- **End: 840** — the Yenisei Kyrgyz, having been Uyghur vassals since 758 but in frequent rebellion, invade with a large mounted force (cited as roughly 80,000 horsemen in some secondary accounts), kill the reigning Uyghur khagan, and sack the capital Ordu-Baliq, driving the Uyghurs out of Mongolia entirely ([Wikipedia, "Yenisei Kyrgyz"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yenisei_Kyrgyz); [Wikipedia, "Ordu-Baliq"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordu-Baliq); [Cartographer/Substack, "The Collapse of the Uyghur Khaganate"](https://cartographer.substack.com/p/the-collapse-of-the-uyghur-khaganate)).

### Native/contemporary name
- **Self-designation: Toquz Oɣuz Budun ("People of the Nine Tribes/Clans")**, attested in Old Turkic script (𐱃𐰆𐰴𐰕:𐰆𐰍𐰕:𐰉𐰆𐰑𐰣). The Tang Chinese referred to them by a separate name, **Jiuxing** (回鶻/回紇, also transliterated Huihu/Huige) — an example of a distinct outside (Chinese) label existing alongside self-designation ([Wikipedia, "Uyghur Khaganate"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uyghur_Khaganate)). The Uyghurs also developed their own **Old Uyghur alphabet**, adapted from the Sogdian script, which later influenced Mongolian and Manchu writing systems ([Simple Wikipedia, "Uyghur Khaganate"](https://simple.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uyghur_Khaganate)).

### Capital
Initially **Ötüken**; the primary imperial capital built by the Uyghurs was **Ordu-Baliq** (also called Karabalghasun/Khar Balgas, "city of the court/army") in the Orkhon Valley, established c. 744–745 by Bayanchur Khan ([Wikipedia, "Ordu-Baliq"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordu-Baliq); [German Archaeological Institute, "The ancient Uyghur capital Karabalgasun"](https://www.dainst.org/en/research/projects/noslug/5297)).

### Ruling people
Uyghurs, a Turkic-speaking people.

### Territory
At its height (c. 800 CE), roughly 3.1 million km², covering much of modern Mongolia and extending into parts of northern China and southern Siberia ([Wikipedia, "Uyghur Khaganate"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uyghur_Khaganate)).

## 6. GHAZNAVID EMPIRE (977–1186)

### Dates and justifying events
- **Start: 977** — Sabuktigin (a Turkic ghulam/former military slave of the Samanids, son-in-law and successor of the Samanid governor Alp Tigin) consolidates control of the city of Ghazna (Ghazni, Afghanistan), founding an autonomous dynasty nominally still under Samanid overlordship ([Britannica, "Ghaznavid dynasty"](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Ghaznavid-dynasty); [Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Ghaznavids"](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/ghaznavids/)).
- **End: 1186** — the last Ghaznavid ruler is besieged in his capital (by this point Lahore) by the rising Ghurid dynasty, forced to surrender, and executed ([Archnet, "Ghaznavid {977-1186}"](https://www.archnet.org/collections/2360)).

### Capital
**Ghazni** (Afghanistan), 977–1163; capital shifted to **Lahore** (now Pakistan) 1163–1186 as Ghurid pressure mounted in the west ([Wikipedia, "Ghaznavid Empire"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghaznavid_Empire); [Archnet](https://www.archnet.org/collections/2360)).

### Ruling people
A Persianate Sunni Muslim dynasty of **Turkic origin**, founded by a former military slave (ghulam/mamluk) of the Samanids; ruled a Persian-speaking administrative culture ([Britannica, "Ghaznavid dynasty"](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Ghaznavid-dynasty); [Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Ghaznavids"](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/ghaznavids/)).

### Territory
At its height under Mahmud of Ghazni (r. 998–1030): eastern Iranian lands (Khorasan), Afghanistan, briefly as far west as Ray and Jebal in Iran, parts of Transoxiana including Khwarazm, Baluchistan, and northwestern India/Pakistan following Mahmud's roughly seventeen campaigns into the subcontinent ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Ghaznavids"](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/ghaznavids/); [Human Saga, "Ghaznavid Empire"](https://humansaga.net/empire/ghaznavid-empire)).

## 7. KARA-KHANID KHANATE (c. 840–1212)

### Dates and justifying events — and a key internal dispute
- **Start: c. 840** (some sources say c. 955, when Satuq Bughra Khan converted to Islam, marking the start of the Muslim Turkic Karakhanid polity proper) — founded by the confederation of Karluk, Chigil, Yaghma, and Tukhsi Turkic tribes following the collapse of the Uyghur Khaganate and the reorganization of Karluk power in Semirechye ([Wikipedia, "Kara-Khanid Khanate"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kara-Khanid_Khanate); [Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Ilak-khanids" (Qarakhanids)](https://iranicaonline.org/articles/ilak-khanids); a Hebrew University mongol-studies source dates the khanate "c. 955–1213" — [mongol.huji.ac.il, "Karakhanid Khanate"](http://mongol.huji.ac.il/sites/default/files/wbeoe156%20qarakhanids%20eoe%2016.pdf)). **The exact founding date is treated inconsistently across sources (840 vs. c. 955) and should be flagged as approximate.**
- **c. 1040–1043** — internal succession conflict (between the "Alid" senior line and "Hasanid" junior line) splits the khanate into an **Eastern Khanate** (capital Balasagun, later Kashgar) and a **Western Khanate** (capital Uzgen, later Samarkand) ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Ilak-khanids"](https://iranicaonline.org/articles/ilak-khanids); [UNESCO, "The Karakhanids"](https://unesdoc.unesco.org/ark:/48223/pf0000112856)).
- Both khanates fell under **Seljuk suzerainty** (from c. 1055–1089) and then under **Qara Khitai (Western Liao) suzerainty** after the Qara Khitai's decisive victory over the Seljuk-Karakhanid coalition at the **Battle of Qatwan (1141)** ([mongol.huji.ac.il](http://mongol.huji.ac.il/sites/default/files/wbeoe156%20qarakhanids%20eoe%2016.pdf); [e-history.kz, "Division of the Karakhanids into two Kaganates"](https://e-history.kz/en/history-of-kazakhstan/show/9457)).
- **End: 1211/1212 — a dated split similar in structure to the Qara Khitai dating dispute (see entry 10).** The **Eastern Khanate** ends in **1211** (some sources cite 1204 or 1210 for when it was effectively "rendered toothless" by internal rebellion against Qara Khitai overlordship, with formal end following); the **Western Khanate** is extinguished in **1212**, when the Khwarazmshah Muhammad II has the last Karakhanid ruler (his own son-in-law, Osman/Uthman) executed at Samarkand ([Wikipedia, "Kara-Khanid Khanate"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kara-Khanid_Khanate); [mongol.huji.ac.il](http://mongol.huji.ac.il/sites/default/files/wbeoe156%20qarakhanids%20eoe%2016.pdf); [Humanities Institute, "Qarakhanid Government"](https://humanitiesinstitute.org/__static/3729208acac742936c0d529b666dc03e/qarakhanid-government(3).pdf?dl=1)).

### Native/contemporary name
Sometimes called the **Ilek-Khanids/Ilak-Khanids** in scholarship (from the title *Ilek/Ilig Khan*) as well as "Kara-Khanid" ("Black Khan") — both are scholarly/dynastic-title-derived names rather than a single fixed self-designation preserved in a native document; titles used by rulers included **Arslan Kara Khaqan** ("Lion," associated with the Chigil tribe, senior/eastern) and **Bughra Kara Khaqan** ("Camel," associated with the Yaghma tribe, western) ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Ilak-khanids"](https://iranicaonline.org/articles/ilak-khanids); [Mughal Library, "The Karakhanid State"](https://www.mughallibrary.com/research-paper/166/)).

### Capitals
**Balasagun** (942–1130, Chu valley, near modern Burana, Kyrgyzstan) and **Kashgar** (1130–1211) for the Eastern Khanate; **Samarkand** (from 1040/after Uzgen) for the Western Khanate ([Wikipedia, "Kara-Khanid Khanate"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kara-Khanid_Khanate)).

### Ruling people
Turkic tribal confederation, primarily **Karluk, Chigil, and Yaghma** tribes ([chinaknowledge.de, "The Qaraqan Empires"](http://www.chinaknowledge.de/History/Altera/qaraqans.html); [Wikipedia, "Karluks"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karluks)). Historically significant as the first Turkic Muslim dynasty in Central Asia, marking a shift from Iranic to Turkic political predominance in Transoxiana ([sholu.app, "Eastern Kara-Khanids"](https://sholu.app/entity/eastern-kara-khanids)).

### Territory
At its height: from the Tarim Basin (Kashgar) in the east to the Amu Darya/Oxus in the west, covering Semirechye (Zhetysu), Ferghana, Shash (Tashkent), and Transoxiana (Bukhara, Samarkand) ([alongthesilkroad.com, "Political History of the Kara-Khanid Khanate"](https://alongthesilkroad.com/2025/04/28/political-history-of-the-kara-khanid-khanate-from-its-origins-to-the-division-of-the-empire/)).

## 8. KHWARAZMIAN EMPIRE (c. 1077–1231)

### Dates and justifying events
- **Start: c. 1077** — Anushtegin Gharchai, a Turkic *ghulam* (military slave) and *tashtdar* (keeper of the royal washing bowls) of the Seljuk sultan Malik-Shah I, is appointed governor (Khwarazmshah) of Khwarazm around 470 AH/1077 CE, founding the Anushteginid line (initially as Seljuk vassals) ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Khwarazmshahs I"](https://iranicaonline.org/articles/khwarazmshahs-i); [Britannica, "Khwarezm-Shah dynasty"](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Khwarezm-Shah-dynasty)). Iranica notes the dynasty's "most splendid" and lasting line properly begins with Anushtegin's son Qutb al-Din Muhammad in 1097, so **some sources treat 1097 rather than 1077 as the more meaningful institutional start** — this is a minor dating variance worth flagging.
- The Khwarazmshahs were vassals of the Seljuks and then the Qara Khitai before becoming fully independent rulers from roughly **1190** ([Wikipedia, "Khwarazmian Empire"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khwarazmian_Empire)).
- **1219–1221** — Genghis Khan invades in response to the Khwarazmian governor of Otrar's massacre of a Mongol trade/diplomatic caravan; the Mongols conquer the empire's core territories (Bukhara, Samarkand, Gurganj) within about two years ([Wikipedia, "Mongol invasion of the Khwarazmian Empire"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_invasion_of_the_Khwarazmian_Empire)).
- **End: 1231** — the last Khwarazmshah, Jalal al-Din Mangburni, having continued resistance from Iran, the Caucasus, and India after his father Ala al-Din Muhammad II's death in 1220, is killed (reportedly by Kurdish assailants, possibly employed by the Seljuks of Rum) near Mayyafariqin, marking the definitive end of the dynasty and organized Khwarazmian resistance ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Jalal-al-Din Khvarazmsah(i) Mengubirni"](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/jalal-al-din-kvarazmsahi-mengbirni/); [Britannica, "Jalal al-Din Mingburnu"](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Jalal-al-Din-Mingburnu)).

### Capital
**Gurganj** (Konye-Urgench, in modern Turkmenistan), 1077–1212; shifted to **Samarkand**, 1212–1220, by Muhammad II; then briefly **Ghazni**, 1220–1221; and finally **Tabriz**, 1225–1231, under the fugitive Jalal al-Din ([Wikipedia, "Khwarazmian Empire"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khwarazmian_Empire)).

### Ruling people
Turkic-origin (Anushteginid) dynasty ruling over a largely Persian-speaking administrative and urban population — commonly described as a **Turko-Persian** state ([Simple Wikipedia, "Khwarazmian dynasty"](https://simple.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khwarazmian_dynasty)).

### Territory
At its height under Muhammad II (r. 1200–1220): most of present-day Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan, Iran, Afghanistan, and parts of Pakistan, Azerbaijan, and Kyrgyzstan — one source estimates roughly 3.6 million km² ([Croatian Wikipedia, "Horezmijsko Carstvo"](https://hr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horezmijsko_Carstvo); [Wikipedia, "Khwarazmian Empire"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khwarazmian_Empire)).

### Sources

## 9. THE THREE UZBEK KHANATES AND THE RUSSIAN CONQUEST OF CENTRAL ASIA

### 9a. Khanate of Bukhara (1501–1785) → Emirate of Bukhara (1785–1920)

**Note on dating: sources differ on how to periodize "1501–1920" as requested in the brief** — the polity's *dynasty and formal title* changed partway through, and this should be represented transparently rather than smoothed over.

- **Start: 1500/1501** — Muhammad Shaybani Khan, leading the Uzbek Shaybanid dynasty (descended from Genghisid/Jochid lines via Abu'l-Khayr Khan), defeats the Timurids and takes Samarkand and Bukhara, founding the **Khanate of Bukhara** ([Wikipedia, "Khanate of Bukhara"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khanate_of_Bukhara); [Britannica, "Bukhara"](https://www.britannica.com/place/Bukhara)).
- Bukhara was formalized as the permanent capital under Abdullah Khan II by 1562 (some sources say 1533 or 1539 for related capital-related milestones) ([Wikipedia, "Khanate of Bukhara"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khanate_of_Bukhara); [Leiden Iran-Turan project, "Bukhara"](https://iranturan.leiden.edu/cities/bukhara/)).
- The **Shaybanid line** (1500–1598) was succeeded by the **Ashtarkhanid/Janid (Toqay-Timurid) dynasty** (1599–1747×1785, dates vary by source) ([History Atlas, "Bukhara, Emirate of"](https://www.historyatlas.com/groups/bukhara-emirate-of/); [Encyclopedia.com, "Bukhara, Khanate and Emirate of"](https://www.encyclopedia.com/religion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/bukhara-khanate-and-emirate)).
- **1747–1785** — following the death of Nader Shah of Persia, the Manghit tribal chief Muhammad Rahim Biy consolidates power as *ataliq* (chief minister) behind puppet Chinggisid khans; his relative **Shah Murad formalizes outright Manghit dynastic rule in 1785**, abolishing the khanate title in favor of the Islamic title **Emir**, since the Manghits (though of Mongol/Turco-Mongol origin) were not descendants of Genghis Khan and so could not legitimately claim the title "Khan" under prevailing Central Asian political custom. From this point the state is properly the **Emirate of Bukhara**, though Russian-language sources often use "Khanate of Bukhara" and "Emirate of Bukhara" interchangeably ([Wikipedia, "Khanate of Bukhara"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khanate_of_Bukhara); [Wikipedia, "Emirate of Bukhara"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emirate_of_Bukhara); [Oxford Academic, "The Emirate of Bukhara"](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61799/chapter/546317702)).
- **1868** — after military defeat, Bukhara becomes a **Russian protectorate** (retaining internal autonomy) ([Oxford Academic, "The Emirate of Bukhara"](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61799/chapter/546317702)).
- **End: 1920** — the last emir, Sayyid Alim Khan, is overthrown by the Red Army in late August/September 1920; the emirate is formally abolished October 6, 1920, and replaced by the Bukharan People's Soviet Republic ([Wikipedia, "History of Tajikistan"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Tajikistan); [Britannica, "Mangit dynasty"](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Mangit-dynasty)).
- Capital: **Bukhara** (with brief earlier alternation with Samarkand) ([Wikipedia, "Khanate of Bukhara"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khanate_of_Bukhara)).
- Ruling people: **Uzbeks** — first the Shaybanid dynasty (Genghisid/Jochid descent), then Ashtarkhanids (also Genghisid, of Astrakhan-Tatar origin), then the non-Genghisid **Manghit** dynasty from 1785 ([Wikipedia, "Emirate of Bukhara"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emirate_of_Bukhara)).
- Territory: core Transoxiana — parts of modern Uzbekistan, Tajikistan, and Turkmenistan.

### 9b. Khanate of Khiva (1511–1920)

- **Start: 1511** — Uzbek tribes under the Yadigarid/Arabshahid line establish a khanate centered on the historical region of Khwarazm, with an early capital at **Urgench** (Konye-Urgench, in what is today Turkmenistan) ([Encyclopedia.com, "Khiva, Khanate of"](https://www.encyclopedia.com/religion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/khiva-khanate); [Wikipedia, "Khanate of Khiva"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khanate_of_Khiva)). Some sources give 1512 or 1515 as the founding year, reflecting minor sourcing variance ([French Wikipedia, "Khanat de Khiva"](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khanat_de_Khiva); [Arabic Wikipedia/Marefa, "خانية خيوة"](https://www.marefa.org/%D8%AE%D8%A7%D9%86%D9%8A%D8%A9_%D8%AE%D9%8A%D9%88%D8%A9)).
- **1598** — the Amu Darya shifts course, undermining Urgench; the capital is moved to **Khiva**, which remained the seat of government through 1920 ([Wikipedia, "Khanate of Khiva"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khanate_of_Khiva); [inLibrary.uz, "Socio-Economic Life of the Khanate of Khiva"](https://inlibrary.uz/index.php/ijai/article/view/108307)).
- A short Persian occupation occurred under Nader Shah, 1740–1746, per some sources — noted as an interruption rather than an end of the khanate ([Marefa, "خانية خيوة"](https://www.marefa.org/%D8%AE%D8%A7%D9%86%D9%8A%D8%A9_%D8%AE%D9%8A%D9%88%D8%A9)).
- **1873** — defeated by Russian forces and made a Russian protectorate (retaining internal rule) ([Wikipedia, "Russian conquest of Central Asia"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_conquest_of_Central_Asia)).
- **End: 1920** — the khanate is abolished by Soviet forces in 1919–1920 and replaced by the Khorezm People's Soviet Republic ([Britannica, "Khanate of Khiva"](https://www.britannica.com/place/khanate-of-Khiva)).
- Capital: **Konye-Urgench** (1511–1598), then **Khiva** (1599–1920) ([Wikipedia, "Khanate of Khiva"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khanate_of_Khiva)).
- Ruling people: Uzbeks, ruled by the Yadigarid/Arabshahid dynasty, descended from a branch of the Jochid (Golden Horde) line ([Encyclopedia.com, "Khiva, Khanate of"](https://www.encyclopedia.com/religion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/khiva-khanate)).
- Territory: historical Khwarazm — the lower Amu Darya delta south of the Aral Sea, in modern Uzbekistan and Turkmenistan.

### 9c. Khanate of Kokand (1709–1876)

- **Start: 1709** — the Shaybanid-descended Uzbek emir Shahrukh Biy of the Ming tribe declares independence from the Khanate of Bukhara, establishing a separate state in the eastern Ferghana Valley ([Wikipedia, "Khanate of Kokand"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khanate_of_Kokand); [Wikipedia, "Fergana Valley"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fergana_Valley)). Note: one specialist source periodizes the "Khanate of Kokand" narrowly as 1798–1876, treating the earlier period (1709–1798) as a pre-khanate principality under Ming-tribe begs — **this is a periodization variance worth flagging** ([pahar.in, "Turkestan in the Nineteenth Century"](https://pahar.in/pahar/Books%20and%20Articles/Central%20Asia/1959%20Turkestan%20in%20the%20Nineteenth%20Century--brief%20history%20of%20Bukhara%20Kokand%20and%20Khiva%20by%20Holdsworth%20s.pdf)).
- **1740** — Kokand (from a fort built 1732) becomes the formal capital ([Britannica, "Kokand"](https://www.britannica.com/place/Kokand)).
- **1868** — a treaty reduces Kokand to a Russian vassal state after Tashkent and Samarkand fall to Russian forces (1864–1868) ([Alchetron, "Khanate of Kokand"](https://alchetron.com/Khanate-of-Kokand); [Wikipedia, "Russian conquest of Central Asia"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_conquest_of_Central_Asia)).
- **End: February 19, 1876** — following an anti-Russian uprising under Khan Nasruddin (Nasir ad-Din), Russian generals Konstantin von Kaufman and Mikhail Skobelev suppress the rebellion and capture Andijan (January 1876); Tsar Alexander II formally annexes the khanate, which is abolished and incorporated into the Fergana Oblast of Russian Turkestan ([Britannica, "Khanate of Kokand"](https://www.britannica.com/place/khanate-of-Kokand); [Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Central Asia vii. In the 18th–19th Centuries"](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/central-asia-vii/)).
- Capitals: **Koktonliq Ata** (1709–1740), then **Kokand** (1740–1876) ([Wikipedia, "Khanate of Kokand"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khanate_of_Kokand)).
- Ruling people: Uzbeks of the **Ming tribe**.
- Territory: the Ferghana Valley, extending at its height over parts of modern eastern Uzbekistan, Tajikistan, southeastern Kazakhstan, and all of Kyrgyzstan ([Alchetron, "Khanate of Kokand"](https://alchetron.com/Khanate-of-Kokand)).

### The Russian conquest of Central Asia
- Conventionally dated **1717–1895** (Wikipedia's framing, encompassing early skirmishes through final border settlements with Britain over the Pamirs) or more narrowly **1864–1876** for the decisive conquest of the three khanates ([Wikipedia, "Russian conquest of Central Asia"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_conquest_of_Central_Asia)).
- Result: **Khanate of Kokand annexed outright (1876)**; **Khanate of Khiva (1873)** and **Emirate of Bukhara (1868)** reduced to Russian protectorates retaining nominal internal sovereignty until the Bolshevik-era abolitions of 1919–1920; the conquered/annexed territories were organized into the **Governorate-General of Russian Turkestan** (established 1867) ([Wikipedia, "Russian conquest of Central Asia"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_conquest_of_Central_Asia); [Britannica, "Khanate of Khiva"](https://www.britannica.com/place/khanate-of-Khiva)).

## 10. QARA KHITAI / WESTERN LIAO (1124–1218)

### Dates and the 1211 vs. 1218 dispute — do not resolve, report both positions

- **Start: 1124** — Yelü Dashi, a Khitan noble of the House of Yelü fleeing the Jurchen (Jin dynasty) conquest of the Khitan Liao dynasty's homeland in Manchuria/northern China, leads Khitan remnants west and declares himself king (not yet emperor — that title came in 1131/1132) ([Cambridge University Press, excerpt from "The Empire of the Qara Khitai in Eurasian History" by Michal Biran](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805218/42266/excerpt/9780521842266_excerpt.pdf)). He established a new steppe empire based at **Balasagun** in the Chu Valley (modern Kyrgyzstan) ([Britannica, "Karakitai dynasty"](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Karakitai-dynasty)).
- **The dating dispute is explicit and well documented, exactly as flagged in the task brief:**
  - **1211 — the Naiman usurpation.** The Naiman prince Kuchlug, who had taken refuge with the Qara Khitai after fleeing Genghis Khan's rise in Mongolia, deposes his father-in-law, the reigning Gurkhan Yelü Zhilugu, and seizes the throne. **"Traditional Chinese, Persian and Arab sources generally treat this usurpation as the end of the Khitan dynasty"** ([Wikipedia, "Qara Khitai"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qara_Khitai); confirmed independently by [Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Qara Ketay"](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/qara-ketay/), which states the empire "was crushed by the Naiman leader Küčlüg... in 608/1211" and that it "survive[d] some eighty years until its subjugation in 608/1211 by Mongol Naiman conquerors" — treating 1211 as the terminal date). Kuchlug's contemporaries in China and the Muslim world "never regarded him as a legitimate Qara Khitai ruler" even though he continued to use the state's official name, "Great Liao" ([Cambridge, Biran excerpt](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805218/42266/excerpt/9780521842266_excerpt.pdf)).
  - **1218 — the actual fall of the state.** Modern academic historiography (notably Michal Biran's Cambridge history of the Qara Khitai, the standard scholarly reference) explicitly chooses **1218** as the closing date for the empire's *history*, even while noting 1211 (Zhilugu's deposition) and 1213 (Zhilugu's death) as alternative candidates that she deliberately rejects for periodization purposes, "since the polity over which [Kuchlug] ruled was still called the Western Liao" ([Cambridge, Biran excerpt](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805218/42266/excerpt/9780521842266_excerpt.pdf)). In 1218, the Mongol general Jebe defeats Kuchlug's forces at Balasagun; Kuchlug flees to Badakhshan (Afghanistan), is captured, and killed, and the Qara Khitai realm is absorbed into the Mongol Empire ([Wikipedia, "Mongol conquest of the Qara Khitai"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_conquest_of_the_Qara_Khitai); [Wikipedia, "Kuchlug"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuchlug)).
  - **This dataset should record both 1211 (traditional/dynastic-historiographical end, per Chinese, Persian, and Arabic sources) and 1218 (the state's actual/political end, per modern scholarly convention including Biran/Cambridge) rather than picking one.**

### Native/contemporary name
- Officially, the state retained the name **"Great Liao"** (大遼, *Dà Liáo*) — a continuation of the Khitan Liao dynastic name — throughout its existence, including under Kuchlug ([Wikipedia, "Qara Khitai"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qara_Khitai); [French Wikipedia, "Kara-Khitans"](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kara-Khitans)).
- **"Qara Khitai" ("Black Khitans"/"Black Cathay") and "Western Liao" (西遼, *Xī Liáo*) are both retrospective/exonymic-descriptive labels** used respectively in the Islamic/Central Asian tradition and in later Chinese historiography, rather than the state's own official self-designation, which was simply "Liao" ([Wikipedia, "Qara Khitai"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qara_Khitai)).

### Capital
**Balasagun** (Chu Valley, near modern Burana/Tokmok, Kyrgyzstan) throughout the state's existence ([Cambridge University Press journal, "The Kök-Tash underground mausoleum"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-the-royal-asiatic-society/article/koktash-underground-mausoleum-in-northeastern-kyrgyzstan-the-firstever-identified-qara-khitai-elite-tomb/91F251DFB09E9FFFF230BDD774EC26A1)).

### Ruling people
The **Khitan Yelü clan** — a formerly nomadic, partially Sinicized people originating in Manchuria/Mongolia, ruling over a vassal patchwork that included the Eastern and Western Kara-Khanids, the Khwarazmshahs, the Uyghur Idiqut of Turfan, Karluks, Qangli, Qipchaqs, and Naimans ([Cambridge/uok.edu.in, "Khitans and Central Asians"](https://ccas.uok.edu.in/Files/93269b6c-7f53-4439-ae9a-3bdf55a4c649/Journal/7e756ffb-44fb-42a2-8034-90c7ee8f2ab9.pdf)).

### Territory
At its height: from the Tarim Basin/Turfan area (via vassal states) in the east to the environs of the Amu Darya in the west, covering much of modern Kyrgyzstan, southern Kazakhstan, Xinjiang, and Uzbekistan ([Facts and Details, "Khitans and the Liao Dynasty"](https://factsanddetails.com/central-asia/Central_Asian_Topics/sub8_8b/entry-4507.html)).

## What I could not verify

- **A genuinely native, self-produced primary-source name for the Saka** in their own words/script — everything documented for "Saka" comes down to us through Persian, Greek, Chinese, or Indian (Sanskrit/Prakrit) exonymic or quasi-exonymic renderings; no surviving Saka-authored inscription defining group self-identity was found in the sources consulted.
- **A single, universally agreed founding date for the Kara-Khanid Khanate** — sources split between c. 840 and c. 955 without a clear scholarly consensus resolving the gap; I could not identify a primary source that definitively settles which year specialists now prefer.
- **A precise, single capital for the Hephthalites** — Encyclopaedia Iranica itself declines to name one definitive capital across all periods and territories of Hephthalite rule, and I could not find a source that resolves the Kunduz-vs-Balkh-vs-Gorgo/Jorjaniya question.
- **The Hephthalites' language and core ethnic origin** — this remains a live scholarly dispute (Iranian? Turkic? Tocharian? a mixed confederation?) and I found no authoritative source claiming to have resolved it; I have deliberately left it unresolved per the task's instructions.
- **Exact year of the First Turkic Khaganate's split (581 vs. 603)** — sources use both dates inconsistently, sometimes citing 581 as when hostilities/fragmentation began and 603 as when the Eastern/Western division became final and irreversible; I could not find a source that reconciles this into one uncontested date, so both are reported.
- **Whether "1077" or "1097" is the more historiographically meaningful start date for the Khwarazmian/Anushteginid state** — Encyclopaedia Iranica itself flags 1077 as merely the appointment of Anushtegin as a Seljuk provincial governor and treats his son's succession in 1097 as the true start of "the fourth and most splendid" line, without fully reconciling the two as equally valid "start" answers.
- **A confirmed native Khitan-language or Khitan-script primary source for the Qara Khitai's own name for their state** beyond the Chinese-recorded "Great Liao" — the Khitan large and small scripts are only partially deciphered, and I could not verify an independent Khitan-script attestation of self-designation in the sources consulted here.
- **A single precise founding year for the Khanate of Khiva** — sources vary between 1511, 1512, and 1515, and I could not determine which is treated as authoritative in specialist Central Asian historiography versus which are rounding/transcription artifacts.
- **The exact status of "1709 vs. 1798" as the correct start date for the Khanate of Kokand** — at least one specialist secondary source (Holdsworth, cited via pahar.in) treats 1709–1798 as a pre-khanate phase and dates the khanate proper from 1798, while most general sources (Wikipedia, Britannica-adjacent, Alchetron) use 1709. I was not able to access a primary Central Asian chronicle to adjudicate this.
