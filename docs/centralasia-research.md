## 1. NEOLITHIC CENTRAL ASIA

### 1.1 Jeitun (Djeitun) Culture — Turkmenistan

**Dates:**
- Founding excavation/dating authority (Harris, Gosden, Charles — Cambridge/UCL team, 11 AMS radiocarbon dates, OxA-2912 to OxA-2916 and OxA-4690–4695, all run at the Oxford Radiocarbon Accelerator Unit on individual cereal/weed grains, calibrated with Calib 2.1 / Stuiver & Reimer 1993): **first occupation slightly before 6000 cal BCE**; two-sigma span of all 11 dates is **6360–5600 cal BCE**; settlement likely occupied only 500–600 years, possibly discontinuously. Site-level phasing (5 architectural phases identified by Masson) **cannot be resolved** by the radiocarbon dates — they cluster too tightly. [Harris, Gosden & Charles, "Jeitun: Recent Excavations at an Early Neolithic Site in Southern Turkmenistan" (Cambridge/Academia.edu reproduction)](https://www.academia.edu/102279073/Jeitun_Recent_Excavations_at_an_Early_Neolithic_Site_in_Southern_Turkmenistan)
- Same conclusion restated in UCL's *Archaeology International*: Jeitun "first occupied at c. 5000 BC (= c. 6000 cal BC)," with domestic six-row barley and einkorn wheat, and domestic sheep/goat present from first occupation. [UCL Press, *Archaeology International*, Harris et al.](https://journals.uclpress.co.uk/ai/article/993/galley/12657/view/)
- Later reanalysis (Monjukli Depe excavation volume, 2019) using a **Bayesian model over 87 AMS dates from Kiel/Poznań/Athens/Miami labs at Monjukli Depe** (a related Jeitun-culture site) puts the main Jeitun-affiliated Neolithic occupation at Monjukli Depe within **6028–5616 cal BCE** (95% probability), with reoccupation phases extending discontinuously to c. 5616 cal BCE. This is **narrower** than an older (Hiebert-derived) stretching of "Jeitun" (Early to Late phases) across **6200–4500 BCE**, which the volume's authors argue resulted from assigning ill-dated Middle/Late Jeitun material to the long gap between dated Early Jeitun and the radiocarbon-dated start of Anau IA. [Sidestone Press, *Looking Closely: Excavations at Monjukli Depe, Turkmenistan 2010–2014* (open access)](https://www.sidestone.com/openaccess/9789088907654.pdf)
- Independent confirmation from a 2025 PNAS paper: pottery at "the westernmost point of central Asia, at the Djeitun site, should date back to around **8000 to 7800 cal BP**" (≈ 6050–5850 cal BCE), and six-row naked barley at Jeitun/Sang-i-Chakmakh/Mehrgarh all cluster at **ca. 8500–8000 cal BP** (≈ 6550–6050 cal BCE). [Zhou, Spengler et al., "9,000-year-old barley consumption in the foothills of central Asia," PNAS (2025)](https://pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2424093122)

**Significance:** Jeitun is the earliest securely dated Neolithic farming settlement in Central Asia — small mudbrick houses (20–30 m²), domestic six-row barley and einkorn wheat, domestic sheep and goat, with continued hunting of wild gazelle. It sits on the northeastern margin of the Iranian Plateau, in the Kopet Dag piedmont, alongside comparable marginal-zone Neolithic villages on the western (Zagros) and southeastern (Baluchistan) margins of the plateau.

**Relationship to the Iranian/Near Eastern Neolithic:** The excavation reports state that Jeitun Culture pottery, clay figurines, and small stone axes resemble those of Jarmo, Tepe Guran, and Tepe Sarab in the Zagros; and that northeastern Iranian sites — Yarim Tepe, Tureng Tepe, Sang-i Chakmak — show varying similarities in architecture and artefacts "to varying degrees." The consensus stated in the UCL piece is that Central Asian agriculture "resulted from the spread of already domesticated crops and animals from the Fertile Crescent," with the relatively sudden appearance of a full Neolithic economic/settlement package favoring **colonization (primary diffusion)** over local adoption by hunter-gatherers (secondary diffusion), though the question is not fully resolved. [UCL Press, *Archaeology International*](https://journals.uclpress.co.uk/ai/article/993/galley/12657/view/)

**⚑ LITERATURE MOVED (2019):** The Monjukli Depe volume overturns the older assumption that Anau IA (early Chalcolithic) directly and immediately succeeded Late Jeitun. It identifies an **~800–900 year occupational hiatus** at Monjukli Depe between the end of the Neolithic (Jeitun-affiliated) occupation (ending 5731–5616 cal BCE, 95% probability) and the start of an intervening Aeneolithic "Meana Horizon" occupation (beginning 4689–4555 cal BCE), which itself predates classic Anau IA (4350–3900 BCE in the volume's comparative table). This replaces the older Masson/Sarianidi/Hiebert model of a continuous Jeitun→Anau IA sequence and the very long (6200–4500 BCE) single "Jeitun period." [Sidestone Press](https://www.sidestone.com/openaccess/9789088907654.pdf)

**COMMON MISCONCEPTION:** Wikipedia's Jeitun article states occupation "from about 7200 to 4500 BC" — this figure is **not supported by any radiocarbon dataset found in this pass** and should not be used for dating. The actual excavated radiocarbon evidence supports first occupation only "slightly before 6000 cal BCE," not 7200 BCE. Wikipedia is locator-only here. [en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeitun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeitun) (locator only — not a dating authority)

### 1.2 Kelteminar (Kel'teminar) Culture — Aral Sea Basin

**Dates:** `n.a.` for a single secure start/end date. No fetched primary excavation report with lab numbers/BP values for Kelteminar sites was found in this pass — all numeric ranges encountered (5500–3500 BCE, 5500–3000 BCE, 6000–3000 BCE, "6th–3rd millennium BCE") come from secondary/tertiary sources (Wikipedia, EBSCO Research Starters, popular atlases) without underlying radiocarbon citations, or from the Russian Bol'shaya Entsiklopediya (also secondary). The one peer-reviewed paper that does engage with Kelteminar chronology (Zhou/Spengler et al., PNAS 2025) treats Kelteminar as **"sixth to fourth millennia BC"** in general terms for cave sites like Dzhebel, Dam Dam Cheshme, and Kailiu — but explicitly states it provides **no individual radiocarbon measurements, lab numbers, sigma ranges, or calibration method** for any Kelteminar site, and separately notes the "well-dated Kelteminarian" site of Ayakagytma without giving that site's numbers either. [Zhou, Spengler et al., PNAS (2025)](https://pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2424093122)

**What the fetched literature does establish:** The PNAS paper's own new site, Toda-1 Cave in the Central Asian foothills, has an early Neolithic/prepottery occupation dated **9200–7800 cal BP** (≈ 7250–5850 cal BCE) that the authors state **predates and falls outside the accepted temporal range of Kelteminar**, and they explicitly decline to label the Toda-1 material as Kelteminar because of disagreement in the field over Kelteminar's geographic/temporal boundaries. [PNAS (2025)](https://pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2424093122)

**Significance:** Kelteminar is understood as a Neolithic–Chalcolithic hunter-fisher-gatherer culture of the Aral Sea/Amu Darya-Syr Darya delta region, contemporary with and in contact with the farming Jeitun culture to the south — an Oxford excavation summary states that Jeitun agropastoralists "came into contact with hunter-gatherers of the 'Keltiminar Culture'" by 6000 BCE calibrated. [Oxford School of Archaeology project page](https://www.arch.ox.ac.uk/publication/916109/manual)

**FLAG — insufficient sourcing:** Per the sourcing rules, no numeric start/end date for Kelteminar as a whole can be certified from a fetched primary source in this pass. Any date given for Kelteminar in the existing dataset or elsewhere should be treated as provisional pending a fetch of Vinogradov's original site reports or a modern radiocarbon-based reassessment (e.g., work on Ayakagytma or Lyavlyakan). This is a genuine research gap, not a citation the author should paper over with a Wikipedia number.

## 2. THE INNER ASIAN MOUNTAIN CORRIDOR (IAMC): Crop Globalization, 3rd–2nd millennium BCE

### 2.1 Begash — Dzhungar Mountains, southeastern Kazakhstan

**Dates (two published date ranges — a real literature disagreement, not to be averaged):**
- **Frachetti, Spengler, Fritz & Mar'yashev, 2010/2014 dataset** (Beta Analytic lab numbers, direct AMS dates on grains and charcoal): Begash Phase 1a = **2450–2100 cal BCE** (rounded framing) with individual determinations Beta-266458 (millet & wheat, 3840±40 BP, 2-sigma 2460–2190 cal BCE / 2170–2150 cal BCE respectively), Beta-266459 (charcoal, 3760±40 BP, 2-sigma 2290–2110/2100–2040 cal BCE), Beta-266460 (charcoal, 3740±40 BP), Beta-266457 (charcoal, 3720±40 BP, 2-sigma 2260–2020 cal BCE). [Spengler, Frachetti et al., "Early agriculture and crop transmission among Bronze Age mobile pastoralists of Central Eurasia," *Proc. R. Soc. B* (2014)](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rspb.2013.3382)
- **Hermes, Frachetti, Doumani Dupuy et al., 2019** (dates modeled in OxCal v.4.3.2 with IntCal13, per occupational layer): Begash Phase 1a/earliest occupation = **2345–2080 cal BCE**. [Hermes et al., "Early integration of pastoralism and millet cultivation in Bronze Age Eurasia," *Proc. R. Soc. B* (2019)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6743000/)
- These two ranges (2450–2100 vs. 2345–2080 cal BCE) overlap substantially but are not identical — the difference reflects different modeling approaches (raw calibrated ranges vs. Bayesian OxCal modeling by stratigraphic layer) rather than new data superseding old. **Both are legitimate published figures; do not average them.**

**Significance:** Begash Phase 1a produced the earliest directly dated broomcorn millet (*Panicum miliaceum*) outside China, found together with free-threshing wheat and one barley grain in a human cremation cist — described by both papers as the earliest evidence of crop exchange across Inner Asia. Millet is interpreted as introduced from China/East Asia; wheat as introduced from southern Central Asia (south of the Syr Darya). [Royal Society 2014](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rspb.2013.3382); [Royal Society 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6743000/)

**Mechanism:** Both papers attribute the spread to **mobile pastoralist interaction**, not to migrating farmer populations. Seasonal vertical (transhumant) movement between piedmont wintering grounds and high summer pastures created an extensive corridor of contact across the mountains between Xinjiang and southwest Asia. The 2019 paper adds isotopic evidence: at nearby Dali (~2700 cal BCE) and at Begash Phase 1a, sheep/goat winter diets show high δ13C values (up to 68–74% C4 intake at Begash) interpreted as deliberate **millet-foddering of livestock**, tying crop dispersal directly to pastoral herd management rather than settled agriculture. This process is explicitly stated to have occurred **before** the arrival of western-steppe-ancestry Yamnaya/Afanasievo-related populations in the region. [Hermes et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6743000/)

### 2.2 Tasbas — Dzhungar Mountains, southeastern Kazakhstan

**Dates:**
- Phase 1 (Early Bronze Age, cremation cist with 5 wheat seeds): charcoal dates OS-93050 (4100±30 BP, 2-sigma 2862–2806/2758–2717 cal BCE) and OS-93054 (4060±30 BP, 2-sigma 2840–2813/2678–2482 cal BCE); rounded framing given as **2840–2500 BCE**. [Spengler et al., *Proc. R. Soc. B* (2014)](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rspb.2013.3382)
- Same Phase 1 occupation reframed by the 2019 OxCal/IntCal13 model as **2655–2480 cal BCE**. [Hermes et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6743000/) — again, two legitimate but non-identical published ranges.
- Phase 2a (Late Bronze Age domestic oven context, mixed wheat/barley/millet/pea assemblage): barley dates OS-92277 (3090±40 BP, 2-sigma 1436–1258/1246–1233 cal BCE) and OS-91990 (3030±35 BP, 2-sigma 1405–1192/1143–1132 cal BCE); framed in the paper as **"approximately 1500–1200 BCE"** in one passage and **"1450–1250 cal BCE"** in another. [Spengler et al. 2014](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rspb.2013.3382)

**Significance:** Tasbas Phase 1 provides some of the earliest directly-dated wheat in the mountains of Inner Asia. Phase 2a documents the first secure evidence, per these authors, of **local cultivation** (not just consumption) of a mixed wheat–barley–millet–pea package by mobile pastoralists at 1500 m elevation, with on-site threshing evidence (194 rachises, mudbrick grain impressions) — described as "the only botanical evidence... for local farming among seasonally mobile pastoralists of Central Eurasia in the second millennium BC." [Spengler et al. 2014](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rspb.2013.3382)

**Mechanism for wheat/barley moving east and millet moving west:** The "Food Globalisation in Prehistory" research program (Fuller, Spengler, Frachetti et al.) frames the Inner Asian Mountain Corridor (IAMC), a term coined by Frachetti in 2012, as the primary conduit: free-threshing wheat and naked barley moved **eastward** from southern Central Asia (ultimately from the Fertile Crescent via Iran) through the mountain corridor, while broomcorn (and later foxtail) millet moved **westward** from its northern Chinese center of domestication, both carried by the same networks of transhumant pastoralist mobility rather than by farmer colonization. By c. 1600 BCE, Inner Asian herding communities were cultivating southwest Asian wheat/barley alongside Chinese millets together. [Hermes et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6743000/); [Cambridge University Press, Ventresca Miller et al., "The integration of millet into the diet of Central Asian populations..." *Antiquity* (2022)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/integration-of-millet-into-the-diet-of-central-asian-populations-in-the-third-millennium-bc/1035166AD2FD0E412AE3F3A4C51012D3)

**Related corridor evidence (secondary confirmation, not separately verified against a primary radiocarbon table in this pass):** Ojakly, Turkmenistan — barley OS-92543 (3270±25 BP, 2-sigma 1618–1466 cal BCE) and wood charcoal OS-94541 (3370±25 BP, 2-sigma 1742–1610 cal BCE), framed as "approximately 1700–1500 BCE," representing some of the earliest millet finds in southern Central Asia and interpreted as introduced there by Late Bronze Age pastoralists moving along the IAMC. [Spengler et al. 2014](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rspb.2013.3382)

**⚑ LITERATURE MOVED (research theme, ongoing since ~2010):** Before Frachetti et al. 2010 directly dated the Begash millet/wheat grains, no directly-dated botanical evidence existed for early trans-Eurasian crop exchange via the mountain corridor; the finding reoriented the field's model of crop globalization away from lowland farmer-to-farmer diffusion and toward **pastoralist-mediated** dispersal as a primary mechanism. [Royal Society 2014](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rspb.2013.3382)

## 3. EARLY AGRICULTURE AND OASIS SETTLEMENT (Kopet Dag piedmont & Murghab/Zeravshan)

### 3.1 Anau (Anau North / Anau South) — Turkmenistan

**Dates:**
- Hiebert's radiocarbon-based reassessment: **Anau IA ≈ 4500–4000 BCE**, "based on a series of radiocarbon dates from Anau North." [cited in Monjukli Depe volume, Sidestone Press](https://www.sidestone.com/openaccess/9789088907654.pdf)
- The same volume's own comparative chronology table places Anau IA slightly later, at **4350–3900 BCE**. [Sidestone Press](https://www.sidestone.com/openaccess/9789088907654.pdf)
- Separately, Miller (1999) frames "Anau North (4500–3000 cal BC) dates to the Chalcolithic, and Anau South (3000–1700 cal BC) dates to the Bronze Age," attributing the pottery-sequence work to Hiebert & Kurbansakhatov. [N. Miller, *Vegetation History and Archaeobotany* 8 (1999), University of Pennsylvania reproduction](https://www.sas.upenn.edu/~nmiller0/papers/Miller%201999%20VHA.pdf)
- **FLAG:** No individual lab numbers/BP values for Anau itself were retrieved in this pass (the Sidestone volume explicitly states it does not reproduce the Anau North radiocarbon table, only citing Hiebert's derived date range). Treat "4500–4000 BCE" and "4350–3900 BCE" as two versions of the same broad claim, both traceable to Hiebert's original dating program, not as independent confirmations.

**Significance:** Anau gives its name to the "Anau IA" ceramic Neolithic/early Chalcolithic horizon that succeeds Jeitun-culture material in the Kopet Dag piedmont and immediately precedes the Namazga sequence.

**Sourcing status:** The above bracket sets were located via search but are attested primarily in tertiary sources (Wikipedia's Namazga-Tepe and Namazga articles, locator only, not fetched as dating authorities) and were **not independently confirmed against a fetched primary radiocarbon table for Namazga-Tepe itself** in this pass. - A Tajikistan-focused academic source explicitly documents the scale of the problem: "C14 dates for a final layer of Namazga VI at Namazga settlement itself vary from **1884 BC to 818 BC**" (Hiebert 1994), and separately notes that European/American scholars generally accept calibrated radiocarbon dates placing later Namazga V–VI material in the first half of the 2nd millennium BCE, while some other schools place the same material in the middle/second half of the 2nd millennium BCE. ### 3.3 Altyn-Depe — Turkmenistan

**Dates:** `n.a.` for a fully independently fetched primary radiocarbon dataset. Search-located figures (not all independently fetched from a primary excavation report in this pass): occupation "c. 3200–2400 BCE" (Late Regionalization Era) and "c. 2400–2000 BCE" (Integration Era, full urban phase), 25–26 hectares at peak, abandoned c. 2000–1600 BCE depending on source. One source (bibbase.org, reproducing Masson's own 1969 excavation summary) gives "Early Namazga V period (circa 2100–1850 BC)" and "Late Namazga V period (circa 1850–1650 BC)" specifically for Altyn-Depe's trenches 7–9, which is a genuinely primary-adjacent citation (Masson's own field report) but was not independently fetched as a full paper in this pass. **Significance:** Altyn-Depe is the second major "city" of the Namazga culture alongside Namazga-Tepe itself, with monumental architecture (a ziggurat-like structure), craft-production quarters, and material culture linking it to Mundigak, Shahr-i Sokhta, and eventually BMAC/Oxus urbanism.

**FLAG:** Given that Namazga V dating itself is contested (see 3.2), any Altyn-Depe date expressed as "Namazga V" inherits that same uncertainty. Treat as `n.a.` pending a fetch of a dedicated modern radiocarbon paper on Altyn-Depe (e.g., Kircho's or Solovyova's stratigraphic/AMS work, not located in this pass).

### 3.4 Gonur Depe — Murghab delta, Turkmenistan (part of BMAC/Oxus, already in dataset at 2200–1700 BCE)

**Dates (supplementary detail to the existing BMAC entry, since Gonur has its own internal phasing):**
- "Sixty C14 dates from different parts of Gonur reveal that this site was inhabited between **2300–1500 cal BC**" (Zaytseva et al. 2008, cited in a University of Warsaw anthropology bulletin). [www.anthropology.uw.edu.pl](https://www.anthropology.uw.edu.pl/07/bne-07-03.pdf)

[Margiana.su, official excavation-affiliated publication on Gonur Depe](https://margiana.su/publication/articles/Art-Urm-2013-Gonur-Depe%20eng%20allFig=.pdf)
- A specific elite burial: Gonur Tomb 2380, sample I1784, directly dated **2201–2031 cal BCE** (3720±30 BP, lab Poz-83485) via ancient-DNA-linked radiocarbon dating. [ADNA Era analysis blog, citing the underlying aDNA paper's supplementary dating](https://adnaera.com/2025/04/01/varas-and-dragonslayers-rethinking-the-indo-iranian-expansion/) — note this is a secondary blog reproducing a lab code and date; treat the specific number as attributed to the original aDNA supplementary table, not independently verified here.
- **This is broadly consistent with, but not identical to, the existing dataset's BMAC/Oxus entry of 2200–1700 BCE** — Gonur's own radiocarbon-based range (2300–1600 BC per Zaytseva et al.) extends both slightly earlier and slightly later than the dataset's current bracket. Flagging for the parent dataset's authors: Gonur alone may justify widening the BMAC end-date discussion, though this is a site-level nuance rather than a wholesale correction.

**Significance:** Gonur Depe is generally regarded as the principal urban/palatial center of the BMAC/Oxus civilization in the Margiana (Murghab delta) region.

### 3.5 Sarazm — Zeravshan Valley, Tajikistan

**Dates (from the official UNESCO World Heritage nomination dossier, which reproduces the underlying radiocarbon table):**
- Overall site range: radiocarbon-dated **3500 to c. 2000 BC** (uncalibrated framing terminology in the dossier's period table; the dossier's own detailed table gives calibrated 1-sigma cal BC ranges per Groningen 1.20 (1995) recalibration).
- **Period I:** 3500–3300 BC (dossier's rounded framing). Underlying dates: Ле-2172 (5050±60 BP, Groningen recal. 3946–3842 / 3824–3786 cal BC 1σ), Ле-2173 (4880±90 BP, 3778–3624 / 3576–3536 cal BC 1σ), Ле-2174 (4940±50 BP, 3774–3662 cal BC 1σ).
- **Period II:** 3200–2900 BC. Ле-1806 (4460±50 BP, 3306–2932 cal BC 1σ across multiple sub-ranges), Ле-1808 (4230±40 BP, 2892–2700 cal BC 1σ).
- **Period III:** 2900–2700 BC. Ле-1807 (3840±40 BP, 2394–2200 cal BC 1σ), Ле-1420 (3790±80 BP, 2390–2044 cal BC 1σ).
- **Period IV:** 2700–2000 BC (younger dates in the table, e.g., Ле-2477 at 3670±40 BP, 2126–1974 cal BC 1σ, run into the early 2nd millennium).
- All lab codes, sample contexts, and three parallel calibration versions (Groningen 1.20/1995; Kohl 1984/1992; Besenval & Isakov 1989/Isakov 1991) are given in the dossier's Table 1. [UNESCO World Heritage nomination dossier for Sarazm, Republic of Tajikistan](https://whc.unesco.org/uploads/nominations/1141rev.pdf)
- The dossier explicitly flags an **internal inconsistency**: ceramic-typological dating places the end of Sarazm at c. 2600 BC, while the radiocarbon dates run to c. 2000 BC — "not in agreement," with the dossier stating "further research is needed to reach a firm conclusion." [same source](https://whc.unesco.org/uploads/nominations/1141rev.pdf)
- The dossier also documents an Afanasievo-type stone-circle steppe burial at Sarazm, explicitly linking Sarazm to the Afanasievo cultural sphere (already in the dataset at 3100–2500 BCE) as an "interface between Afanasevo and the Proto-Urban Southern Civilization." [same source](https://whc.unesco.org/uploads/nominations/1141rev.pdf)

**Significance:** Sarazm is the earliest major Chalcolithic/Early Bronze Age urban-scale settlement in the mountainous zone of Tajikistan, with metallurgical workshops (tin/copper), extensive trade contacts (lapis lazuli, turquoise) linking it toward Mesopotamia and the Indus, and direct material contact with the Namazga culture and the steppe (Afanasievo). It is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. [UNESCO dossier](https://whc.unesco.org/uploads/nominations/1141rev.pdf)

**FLAG:** The dossier itself explicitly labels its own ceramic-vs-radiocarbon chronological disagreement (2600 BC vs 2000 BC end date) as unresolved — a textbook case of "where the field genuinely disagrees, present both claims" sourced from a single institutional document that itself hosts the disagreement.

## 4. TOPICS COVERED AS TIME PERMITTED

### 4.1 Seima-Turbino Phenomenon

**Dates — a major, explicit ⚑ LITERATURE MOVED case:**
- **Older typological chronology:** 16th–15th centuries BCE, based on cross-dating with the Borodino Hoard (Ukraine) and Chinese Anyang-period bronzes. [Marchenko et al., "Radiocarbon Chronology of Complexes With Seima-Turbino Type Objects...," Tomsk State University preprint, published in *Radiocarbon* (2017)](https://news.tsu.ru/upload/medialibrary/bab/2017_06_marchenko_et_al..pdf)
- **New AMS radiocarbon chronology (28 samples from 6 Baraba forest-steppe sites plus Rostovka, run at Queen's University Belfast [UBA, AMS] and Novosibirsk [SOAN, LSC], calibrated with OxCal v4.2.4 / IntCal13):** the majority of Seima-Turbino-bearing sites in eastern Europe, the Trans-Urals, and southwestern Siberia date to **22nd–20th centuries cal BCE** — thus thoroughly Middle Bronze Age, not Late Bronze Age, and roughly 500–700 years **older** than the typological estimate. [Marchenko et al. 2017](https://news.tsu.ru/upload/medialibrary/bab/2017_06_marchenko_et_al..pdf)
- Some individual sites span wider or later ranges: Baraba/Middle Irtysh burials 29th–20th c. cal BCE (wide interval); Ust'-Vetluga cemetery (eastern Europe) dates younger, 21st–17th c. cal BCE, interpreted as reflecting the phenomenon's "final stage." Pepkinskii Kurgan (Middle Volga) is the oldest dated locus, 26th–21st c. cal BCE. [Marchenko et al. 2017](https://news.tsu.ru/upload/medialibrary/bab/2017_06_marchenko_et_al..pdf)
- A 2023 paper (Grigoriev) proposes yet another reconciliation via historical cross-dating with the Santorini eruption and early Shang chronology, landing on **17th–16th century BCE** (or 18th–16th c. BCE) as a middle-ground estimate. [Grigoriev, "Chronology of the Seima-Turbino bronzes, early Shang Dynasty and Santorini eruption," *Praehistorische Zeitschrift* / De Gruyter (2023)](https://www.degruyter.com/document/doi/10.1515/pz-2023-2028/html) — **this is a third, non-consensus position; present alongside, do not merge with, the Marchenko AMS-based range.**

**Significance:** Seima-Turbino refers to a "transcultural" package of highly distinctive cast-bronze weapons (spearheads with cast sockets, celts, knives with animal-headed handles) found across a huge swath of Eurasia — from the Baltic coast to southwestern Siberia and the Minusinsk Basin — among otherwise unrelated Bronze Age cultures. Marchenko et al. describe it as an "emergence and wide and rapid distribution" of these objects; the current best-supported model is a movement of specialized bronze-working/warrior groups from southern/southeastern Central Asia through the Ob-Irtysh interfluve in the **last third of the 3rd millennium BCE**, spreading westward into eastern Europe (where dated material is somewhat younger, consistent with west-to-east time-lag). [Marchenko et al. 2017](https://news.tsu.ru/upload/medialibrary/bab/2017_06_marchenko_et_al..pdf)

**COMMON MISCONCEPTION:** Wikipedia's Seima-Turbino infobox states "c. 2200 BC – 1900 BC" — this happens to be broadly compatible with the Marchenko AMS range (22nd–20th c. cal BCE) but should not be cited to Wikipedia; cite Marchenko et al. directly. [en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seima-Turbino_culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seima-Turbino_culture) (locator only — not a dating authority)

### 4.2 Tarim Basin Mummies — 2021 Ancient-DNA Result (Genuine Consensus Reversal)

**Dates:**
- Early Bronze Age Dzungarian Basin individuals (Nileke, Ayituohan, Songshugou; culturally assigned Afanasievo): **3000–2800 BCE**. [Zhang, Ning, Cui et al., "The genomic origins of the Bronze Age Tarim Basin mummies," *Nature* 599 (2021)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04052-7)
- Early–Middle Bronze Age Tarim Basin individuals (Xiaohe horizon; sites Xiaohe, Gumugou, Beifang): **2100–1700 BCE**. Site-specific earliest-layer ranges: Gumugou 2135–1939 BCE; Xiaohe 1884–1736 BCE; Beifang 1785–1664 BCE. [Nature (2021)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04052-7)
- Dating method: AMS radiocarbon on 10 of the 18 sequenced individuals (some cross-checked at both Beta Analytic, Miami and Lanzhou University, with consistent results), calibrated using IntCal20 and OxCal v.4.4. [Nature (2021)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04052-7)

**⚑ LITERATURE MOVED / genuine consensus reversal (2021):** Prior hypotheses held that the Tarim mummies descended from **Proto-Tocharian-speaking Afanasievo/steppe pastoralist migrants**, or from BMAC populations, or from Inner Asian Mountain Corridor agropastoralist groups. The 2021 Nature paper's genome-wide data show the Early–Middle Bronze Age Tarim (Xiaohe-horizon) individuals carry **only local ancestry** — modeled as ~72% Ancient North Eurasian (ANE, represented by the Upper Paleolithic Siberian individual "AG3") and ~28% Ancient Northeast Asian (Baikal_EBA) — with **no detectable contribution from Afanasievo, BMAC, or IAMC populations**, despite the Tarim people's clear cultural borrowing of neighboring agricultural and pastoralist practices (wheat, dairy, kefir cheese, etc.). The paper's own abstract states plainly: **"Our results do not support previous hypotheses for the origin of the Tarim mummies."** By contrast, the contemporaneous Dzungarian Basin population (further north, culturally Afanasievo) does show ~50–70% actual Afanasievo genetic ancestry with local admixture — so the steppe migration model holds for Dzungaria but is explicitly rejected for the Tarim Basin proper. [Nature (2021)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04052-7)

**Significance:** This reframes the Tarim mummies (previously sensationalized in popular media as evidence of a lost "Caucasoid" or western migrant population in western China) as a genetically isolated indigenous population — descended from a Pleistocene-age Asian lineage present in the region since the early Holocene — that adopted, rather than imported via migration, its neighbors' farming and pastoral technologies. The population's superficially "Western"-looking features are attributed to shared deep ancestry with the ANE lineage (also ancestral to some western Eurasian and Native American populations), not to recent West Eurasian admixture.

**COMMON MISCONCEPTION:** Popular accounts continuing to describe the Tarim mummies as evidence of an Indo-European/Tocharian migrant population directly seeding the Tarim Basin are **explicitly contradicted** by the cited 2021 genomic study for the earliest (Xiaohe-horizon) mummies. The Tocharian-language question is separately noted by the authors as unresolved — the paper does not claim the population could not have spoken a Tocharian language, only that they did not arrive there via a large Afanasievo/steppe migration. [Nature (2021)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04052-7)
