# East Asian Prehistory, c. 14,000–300 BCE — Sourced Research Dataset

**Purpose:** Subdivide the current single `east-asia.prehistory` entity (14,000–300 BCE) into child entities. This file follows the required mechanical format for each entity: **name → dates (method + explicit calibration frame) → significance → sources (full URLs) → flags.**

**Already in dataset, NOT re-researched here:** Yangtze rice centre, general Neolithic transition, Chinese legendary period, Gojoseon.

**Sourcing discipline applied:** Wikipedia used only as "locator only" (never as a dating authority) and flagged as such wherever it appears. Sci-hub and other pirated-PDF hosts were identified during research and explicitly EXCLUDED as citations (see Jiahu section). Where a number could not be confirmed against a fetched non-Wikipedia, non-exam-prep, non-content-farm source, it is marked `n.a.`

## PART 1 — JAPAN: JŌMON PERIOD

### 1.1 Jōmon period — overall chronology (six-stage division)
- **Dates:** Two independent recalibrated academic schemes were fetched, and they diverge somewhat at the edges. Both are calibrated (cal BC / cal BP), not raw radiocarbon years:
  - **Scheme A** (Habu, based on Taniguchi 2001 recalibration of 158 dates): Incipient 13,680–9250 cal BC (≈15,630–11,200 cal BP); Initial 9250–5300 cal BC; Early 5300–3360/3500 cal BC; Middle 3360/3550–2580/2510 cal BC; Late 2580/2510–1260/1230/1220 cal BC; Final 1260/1230/1220–410(?) cal BC. Source: [Habu, "Debating Jomon Social Complexity," ScholarSpace PDF](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/a23984f3-7be2-4157-8600-9655a965ef1e/content).
  - **Scheme B** (Matsumoto, Habu & Matsui 2017): Incipient 16,000–11,000 cal BP; Initial 11,000–7000 cal BP; Early 7000–5300 cal BP; Middle 5300–4400 cal BP; Late 4400–3300 cal BP; Final 3300–2500/2300 cal BP. Source: [Matsumoto, Habu & Matsui 2017 PDF](https://junkohabu.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/02/matsumoto-habumatsui-2017.pdf).
  - A third comparative table (Jōmon vs. Chulmun sub-periods, "modified from Habu in press") gives: Final 3300–2400 cal BP; Late 4300–3300 cal BP; Middle 5500–4300 cal BP; Early 7000–5500 cal BP; Initial 11,000–7000 cal BP; Incipient 16,000–11,000 cal BP. Source: [studocu course document citing Habu, "Temporal and Regional Variability of Jomon and Chulmun Cultures"](https://www.studocu.com/en-us/document/tulsa-community-college/ancient-and-medieval-western-civilization-h/temporal-and-regional-variability-of-jomon-and-chulmun-cultures-ch22/140129705) — course-document hosting, used only to cross-check figures that also appear in the two academic PDFs above, not as a standalone authority.
- **Significance:** Establishes the internal skeleton for subdividing Jōmon into six child periods within the wider `east-asia.prehistory` entity.
- **Flags:** The two academic schemes disagree by several centuries at Early/Middle/Late boundaries — this reflects genuine ongoing recalibration in the literature, not sourcing error. Present ranges, not a single boundary date, when authoring downstream entities. Any older/popular source giving *uncalibrated* BP dates for these stages should not be merged with these cal BP/cal BC figures.

### 1.2 Ōdai Yamamoto I site — world's-oldest-pottery claim (Japan)
- **Dates:** Uncalibrated AMS dates on charred residue/wood: two younger dates (likely intrusive) 7070±40 and 7710±40 BP; six other samples (5 residue, 1 charcoal) span **12,680–13,780 BP (uncalibrated conventional radiocarbon years)**. Source: [Nakamura, Taniguchi, Tsuji & Oda 2001, *Radiocarbon* — Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/radiocarbon/article/radiocarbon-dating-of-charred-residues-on-the-earliest-pottery-in-japan/D2DE167CFF67D9B361D5D2E3AF198BD8) (does not itself state a calibrated figure on the fetched page).
  - Calibrated equivalents (2σ) from independent papers: individual dates run **cal BP 17,160–15,950 down to 16,680–15,730**; oldest reported calibrated figure **~16,520 cal BP** (Nakamura & Tsuji 1999). Sources: [Keally, *Radiocarbon* PDF, U. Arizona](https://journals.uair.arizona.edu/index.php/radiocarbon/article/viewFile/4273/3698); [Arizona *Radiocarbon* journal PDF](https://journals.uair.arizona.edu/index.php/radiocarbon/article/viewFile/3689/pdf).
  - A Taiwan Ministry of Culture-hosted academic PDF states Japanese pottery appears "by ca. 16,800 cal BP" (citing Kuzmin 2013). Source: [file.moc.gov.tw PDF, "The Advent and Spread of Early Pottery in East Asia"](https://file.moc.gov.tw/001/Upload/OldFiles/AdminUploads/files/201807/423.pdf).
- **Significance:** Currently the best-attested claimant for Japan's oldest pottery, part of the trio of earliest-pottery claims worldwide alongside Xianrendong and Yuchanyan in south China (see Part 4). Marks the "Incipient Jōmon" starting point.
- **Sources:** as above.
- **Flags:** COMMON MISCONCEPTION — popular sources (including Wikipedia, locator only: [Ōdai Yamamoto I Site](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%8Cdai_Yamamoto_I_Site)) state a single date (~14,500 BCE / 16,500 BP) as if uncontested; the underlying AMS dataset is actually a spread across multiple samples/labs (12,680–13,780 BP uncalibrated), and whether Ōdai Yamamoto or the south-China caves hold priority depends on calibration and interpretation choices — see Part 4 flags.

### 1.3 Fukui Cave (Japan)
- **Dates (uncalibrated conventional radiocarbon BP, per lab):** Layer VII (non-ceramic): 13,600±600 BP (Gak-951); Layer IV: 14,000±400 BP (I-945); Layer II/III (linear-relief pottery): 12,400±350 BP (Gak-949). Source: [Keally, "First Radiocarbon Dates for Japan 1951–1970"](http://www.t-net.ne.jp/~keally/Reports/first-dates.html).
- **Significance:** One of the two classic early-Jōmon pottery type-sites (alongside Ōdai Yamamoto), historically central to arguments for very early ceramic use in the Japanese islands.
- **Flags:** These are early-generation (pre-AMS-era, wide-error-margin) dates; treat as uncalibrated BP only. Britannica's framing of "~10,500 BCE" ([Britannica, "Japanese art – Jōmon period"](https://www.britannica.com/art/Japanese-art/Jomon-period)) does not specify calibration status and should not be used as the calibration-labeled figure — flagged `n.a.` for calibrated equivalent pending a dedicated recalibration source.

### 1.4 Sannai Maruyama
- **Dates:** Occupied **c. 5,900–4,300 cal BP** (uncalibrated range c. 5,050–3,900 BP). Independently corroborated by three sources: [Habu, *Antiquity* PDF](https://junkohabu.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/04/habu2008-antiquity.pdf); [Habu, Hall & Ogasawara 2004 PDF](https://junkohabu.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/04/habu-hall-and-ogasawara2004-ocr.pdf); [CiNii Research](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1390001206496450176) (states 5,100–3,900 BP uncal / 5,900–4,300 cal BP, with the Early/Middle Jōmon boundary at 5,400 cal BP); [University of Tokyo, ofgs.aori PDF](http://ofgs.aori.u-tokyo.ac.jp/kawahata/PDF_files/Res_PDF32.pdf) (occupied 5.9 ka to 4.2±0.1 ka cal BP).
- **Significance:** Largest and best-studied Middle Jōmon settlement, key evidence for Jōmon sedentism, large-scale wooden monumental construction, and long-distance exchange despite a foraging (non-agricultural) economic base.
- **Flags:** None — this figure is well corroborated across four independently fetched sources.

### 1.5 Jōmon population decline (Late/Final phases)
- **Dates/data:** Koyama's (1979) demographic model: Middle Jōmon ("Jōmon 3") peak population ~262,600 (density 0.89/km²); Late Jōmon population 160,300; Final Jōmon population 76,800 (nationwide estimates, method = archaeological site-density extrapolation, not radiocarbon). Sources: [J-Stage PDF (Koyama 1979 figures)](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/ase1911/95/3/95_3_391/_pdf); [Minpaku (National Museum of Ethnology) PDF](https://minpaku.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/3481/files/SES02_002.pdf).
- **Independent confirmation via radiocarbon summed-probability method:** PLOS ONE (2016) study using summed probability distributions of dated samples: population trend declines and plateaus through Late Jōmon (n=1,598 dates; **4,420–3,220 cal BP**) and Final Jōmon (n=1,462 dates; **3,220–2,300 cal BP**), with a statistically significant regional trough at **~3,900–3,800 cal BP** in Kantō and **~3,800–3,500 cal BP** in Aomori/Hokkaidō. Source: [PLOS ONE 2016](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0154809).
- **Proposed cause:** Climatic cooling, per Koyama (1978). Source: [J-Stage PDF](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/ase/advpub/0/advpub_110428/_pdf/-char/en).
- **Significance:** Documents a genuine demographic contraction preceding the Jōmon–Yayoi transition, relevant context for interpreting the subsequent spread of wet-rice agriculture.
- **Flags:** Koyama's absolute population figures are archaeological-site-density estimates, not radiocarbon-dated counts — do not present them as directly dated. The PLOS ONE cal BP ranges ARE directly derived from radiocarbon dates (summed probability method) and are the stronger citation for any date-bearing claim about the decline's timing.

### 1.6 ⚑ Jōmon–Yayoi transition and the 2003 NMJH radiocarbon controversy
- **Pre-2003 consensus (uncalibrated BP tradition, "short chronology"):** Yayoi period begins 5th–4th century BCE.
- **⚑ LITERATURE MOVED, 2003:** In May 2003 the National Museum of Japanese History (Harunari, Fujio, Imamura, Sakamoto) announced AMS ¹⁴C dating of charred residue on pottery, arguing the Yayoi start should move back roughly 500 years, potentially to the **10th century BCE**. Sources: [Shoda, SEAA Bulletin PDF, "Yayoi Period Dating Controversy"](https://www.seaa-web.org/sites/default/files/publications/bseaa-1/BSEAA1-Shoda.pdf); [Shoda 2010, *Radiocarbon* 52(2-3), Cambridge Core PDF](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/957FA42A4A7A4F3E8E3DA575246B8367/S0033822200045471a.pdf/radiocarbon-and-archaeology-in-japan-and-korea-what-has-changed-because-of-the-yayoi-dating-controversy.pdf).
- **Where the debate stood as of the fetched sources (2007–2010), presented as competing claims, none averaged:**
  - NMJH position: Yayoi could start as early as **10th century BCE**.
  - Miyamoto (2004): no earlier than **9th century BCE**.
  - Takesue (2004) / Shoda (2005), using independent cross-dating rather than AMS: no earlier than **8th century BCE**.
  - Takakura (2003) / Hashiguchi (2003): rejected the NMJH re-dating outright, retained the traditional **5th century BCE** start.
  - Shoda's own 2010 synthesis (using Korean charred-rice AMS dates cross-referenced with pottery typology): rice-agriculture adoption at roughly **12th/11th century BCE in South Korea**, **9th/8th century BCE in Japan** — i.e., earlier than the traditional 5th/4th-century-BCE date but NOT as early as NMJH's proposed 10th century BCE. Specific calibrated (OxCal v4.1.1/IntCal04) Korean charred-rice comparanda cited by Shoda: Gyodong 2860±20 BP → 1116–940 cal BC (2σ); Songdamri 2720±20 BP → 907–819 cal BC; Jagaeri 2480±20 BP → 766–514 cal BC. Source: [Shoda 2010 Cambridge Core PDF, as above].
  - By 2010, per Shoda: "most Japanese archaeologists…had basically accepted the chronological shift, although to varying degrees" — but the exact new start date remained unsettled; Kyūshū-based archaeologists in particular "strongly rejected" NMJH's specific dating.
  - Methodological criticisms of NMJH (not of AMS dating per se, but of its application): Mizunoe (2009) — argued the redating circularly altered assemblage type-definitions; Miyaji (2009) — argued pottery misidentification produced results ~100 years too old; Yoshida (2005) — questioned what the charred material dated actually was; Ishikawa (2006) — criticized NMJH's *interpretation* of the AMS results rather than the AMS method itself.
  - Independent corroboration of an *earlier* Yayoi start (supporting the general direction of the NMJH shift, though not necessarily its full magnitude): AMS dating of early wet-rice paddy-field remains shows irrigated rice cultivation in northern Kyūshū **by the 10th century BCE**; separately, AMS dating of food residue on early Yayoi-style pottery gives **780–830 BCE**. Sources: [Cambridge Core, "Radiocarbon dating of tree rings from the beginning and end of the Yayoi period"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/radiocarbon/article/radiocarbon-dating-of-tree-rings-from-the-beginning-and-end-of-the-yayoi-period-japan/B7D3C965B0155D8455AC73198547FFBC); [heritageofjapan.wordpress.com, summarizing Imamura et al.](https://heritageofjapan.wordpress.com/yayoi-era-yields-up-rice/the-advent-of-agriculture-and-the-rice-revolution/irrigated-rice-culture-appeared-in-930-bce-in-northern-kyushu/in-the-news-accelerator-mass-spectrometry-dating-gives-early-dates-for-rice-found-in-ten-primitive-yayoi-style-pots-780-to-830-b-c/) — this is a secondary blog re-reporting AMS results; used only as corroborating context, not as the primary numeric citation.
  - Historical trajectory of the boundary estimate itself (useful for showing the shift over time): 1966 data → ~400–500 BC; 1982 data → ~500 BC; 2003 new dates → Early Yayoi begins ~800 cal BC, "Earliest Yayoi" ~900–1000 cal BC (Harunari et al. 2003). Source: [Keally, "Radiocarbon Dating in Japanese Archaeology"](http://www.t-net.ne.jp/~keally/Reports/sir2004.html); [Keally, "Yayoi Culture"](http://www.t-net.ne.jp/~keally/yayoi.html).
- **Significance:** This is one of the most consequential dating controversies in East Asian prehistory — it directly affects how the Jōmon-to-Yayoi transition (foraging to wet-rice agriculture) is periodized, and by extension every downstream Yayoi sub-period date.
- **Flags:** ⚑ LITERATURE MOVED 2003 (as above). No fully settled post-2010 consensus figure was found in this research pass — an authoring pass should present the transition as a debated range (roughly 10th–5th century BCE depending on source and criterion) rather than a single date, and should NOT adopt the NMJH's most extreme figure (10th century BCE) as settled fact, nor dismiss it, since as of the fetched 2010 source the field had moved toward the shift's direction without settling on its full magnitude.

## PART 2 — CHINA: NEOLITHIC CULTURES

### 2.1 Peiligang culture
- **Dates:** **~8,500–7,000 cal BP** (equivalently often rendered ~8,500–6,900 BP), calibrated radiocarbon framework. Sources: [Frontiers in Earth Science 2023 overview, "Radiocarbon dating and its applications in Chinese archeology"](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2023.1064717/full) ("pre-Yangshao cultures (Peiligang) ca. 8500–6900 BP"); [Cambridge Core, *Quaternary Research*, Luoyang Basin paper](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/quaternary-research/article/abs/earlymiddle-holocene-ecological-change-and-its-influence-on-human-subsistence-strategies-in-the-luoyang-basin-northcentral-china/2C600190D3ADAA7DAF61A9D10BEE2391) ("8500–7000 cal yr BP").
- **Significance:** One of the earliest well-dated agricultural Neolithic cultures of the middle Yellow River region; closely related to/overlapping with the Jiahu site (2.2).
- **Flags:** A German-Wikipedia-derived conventional date of "5600–4900 BCE" was found but is locator-only and NOT used as the dating figure here pending non-Wikipedia confirmation; the cal BP figures above are the citable ones.

### 2.2 Jiahu site (Henan; part of/predecessor to Peiligang)
- **Dates (three-phase chronology, radiocarbon/AMS, multiple labs, consistent across independently fetched academic sources):** Phase I **~7000–6600 BC**; Phase II **6600–6200 BC**; Phase III **6200–5800/5500 BC** (sources vary slightly on the precise end date). Sources: [Nature 1999, "Oldest playable musical instruments found at Jiahu"](https://www.nature.com/articles/43865); [Cambridge Core, *Antiquity* 77:31-44, "The early development of music: analysis of Jiahu bone flutes"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/B3F09EEC1AFB5D16C96C0DF8301BC877/S0003598X00113432a.pdf/early_development_of_music_analysis_of_the_jiahu_bone_flutes.pdf); [Met Museum, "Jiahu, ca. 7000–5700 BC"](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/jiahu-ca-7000-5700-b-c) (museum-published, acceptable as corroborating, though not itself a primary dating paper).
- **Jiahu flutes:** 33 bone flutes made from crane wing bones (~20 intact); among the world's oldest known playable, multi-note musical instruments, exhibiting an incipient pentatonic-type scale. Sourced from the Nature 1999 and Cambridge Antiquity papers above.
- **Jiahu symbols:** 11 pictographic/incised symbols (9 on tortoise shell, 2 on bone) have been proposed as possible proto-writing. A BBC News report states the associated site material was radiocarbon dated to **6,600–6,200 BC**, matching Phase II above. Source: [BBC News, "Earliest writing found in China"](http://news.bbc.co.uk/2/hi/science/nature/2956925.stm).
- **Significance:** Jiahu provides some of the earliest evidence anywhere for both playable musical instruments and possible proto-writing symbols, within a securely multi-lab-dated three-phase sequence.
- **Flags:** COMMON MISCONCEPTION — a detailed table of lab-code-level ¹⁴C dates for Jiahu (Li 2003) was found ONLY on a pirated-PDF host (sci-hub) during this research; per sourcing rules that source is EXCLUDED and NOT cited here — the phase dates above rest instead on the Nature and Cambridge Antiquity papers, which are consistent with the excluded source's headline figures but were independently verified. Popular sources (e.g. tourism/culture sites) give looser "8,000–9,000 years ago" or "7,000–5,000 BC" framings that are less precise than the three-phase academic chronology — flagged as an imprecision in popular treatment, not a factual dispute.

### 2.3 Cishan culture
- **Dates:** Millet cultivation evidence pushed back to the **Pleistocene–Holocene boundary, ~10,400–10,100 cal BP** (three oldest AMS dates in the cited study). Source: [PNAS 2009, "Agricultural origins in North China pushed back to the Pleistocene–Holocene boundary"](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.0903375106). Broomcorn millet domestication proposed as early as **~10 kyr BP**; foxtail millet **~8.7 kyr BP**, per [PNAS, "Early millet use in northern China"](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1115430109).
- **Significance:** Central site for the north-China millet-domestication debate (see also 2.10 flag on Xinglongwa/millet dating disputes).
- **Flags:** Genuine scholarly disagreement — an Oxford doctoral thesis flags the very earliest Lu et al. (2009) dates (which would push millet cultivation to 8619–8287 BCE-equivalent) as "highly controversial," criticized by Zhao (2011) on sample-context and archaeological grounds. Source: [Oxford ora.ox.ac.uk PDF](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:b265f3b2-402a-46fc-ba13-bee750c9a185/files/rbk1289975). Present BOTH the PNAS 2009 claim and Zhao's critique; do not average them.

### 2.4 Xinglongwa culture
- **Dates:** Earliest DIRECT radiocarbon date on millet grains from Xinglongwa: **5670–5610 BCE** (Zhao 2011), per [Oxford ora.ox.ac.uk PDF](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:b265f3b2-402a-46fc-ba13-bee750c9a185/files/rbk1289975). Broader culture context (5-phase northeast-China sequence: Xiaohexi → Xinglongwa → Zhaobaogou → Hongshan → Xiaoheyan): Xiaohexi **~8.5–8.2 ka cal BP**; Xinglongwa flourishing **after 8.2 ka cal BP**. Source: [static.cambridge.org, *Antiquity* supplementary material PDF](https://static.cambridge.org/content/id/urn:cambridge.org:id:article:S0003598X20002367/resource/name/S0003598X20002367sup001.pdf).
- **Significance:** Key node in the debate over the earliest secure direct-dated millet domestication, distinct from (and more conservative than) the disputed ultra-early Cishan claims.
- **Flags:** A South China Morning Post news article states the site "dates back more than 7,100 years" (~5100 BCE); this is a general site-age claim, not the specific millet-grain AMS date, and is used here only as loose corroborating context, not as the primary date. Source: [SCMP](https://www.scmp.com/article/42160/mystery-chinas-past).

### 2.5 Yangshao culture
- **Dates:** Widely corroborated at **~5000–3000 BCE** (a PMC genetics paper states "approximately 5000 to 2900 BCE"). Source: [PMC, "demic expansion of Yangshao culture"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12218936/). A regional radiocarbon study (Shangluo area) gives a narrower **~4200–2900 BCE** for Yangshao specifically in that area. Source: [Cambridge Core, *Radiocarbon*, "Establishing a firm chronological framework for Neolithic and early dynastic archaeology in the Shangluo area"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/radiocarbon/article/establishing-a-firm-chronological-framework-for-neolithic-and-early-dynastic-archaeology-in-the-shangluo-area-central-china/8E67FAD0B90114132F0102B46A887DB8). The Frontiers 2023 overview gives "ca. 6900–4500 BP" (equivalent to ~4950–2550 BCE), broader than the other two. Source: [Frontiers 2023, as above](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2023.1064717/full).
- **Significance:** The archetypal middle-Yellow-River painted-pottery Neolithic culture, spanning and connecting many regional variants across the middle-late Neolithic.
- **Flags:** Regional dating varies (broad "5000–3000 BCE" pan-cultural framing vs. narrower Shangluo-specific "4200–2900 BCE"); an authoring pass should specify which regional dataset a given sub-entity date is drawn from rather than citing a single national figure.

### 2.6 Hongshan culture
- **Dates — genuine source disagreement, presented as a range with caveats, not averaged:**
  - Beijing Review (news, not primary dating source): "3,000–4,000 BCE" (i.e. radiocarbon dated). Source: [Beijing Review](https://www.bjreview.com/culture/txt/2013-07/22/content_556664_3.htm).
  - UNESCO Courier: "approximately 6,500 to 5,000 years ago" (≈4500–3000 BCE), with late Hongshan jade dragons dated "~5,500–5,000 years ago" (≈3500–3000 BCE). Source: [UNESCO Courier](https://courier.unesco.org/en/articles/archaeological-discovery-hongshan-culture-jade-dragons).
  - Nature 2020 paper: **4500–3000 BCE**. Source: [Nature Communications 2020](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-020-16557-2).
  - Persée (French academic journal): working timetable **ca. 4000–2500 BC**, citing specific calibrated ¹⁴C dates: Dongshanzui 5485±110 BP → calibrated 3490±110 BC; Niuheliang samples 3020±80 BC, 3625±110 BC, 3630±110 BC. Source: [Persée, *Arts asiatiques* 1991](https://www.persee.fr/doc/arasi_0004-3958_1991_num_46_1_1303).
- **Significance:** Northeast-China Neolithic culture renowned for elaborate jade-working (including the "jade dragon"/pig-dragon forms) and large ritual complexes (Niuheliang), frequently cited in early-state/proto-civilization discussions.
- **Flags:** Genuine, unresolved cross-source date disagreement spanning roughly 4500–2500 BCE depending on source and sub-site; an authoring pass must NOT collapse this into a single average date. The Persée source's individual calibrated ¹⁴C dates are the most rigorously sourced figures found and should anchor any child-entity date.

### 2.7 Dawenkou culture
- **Dates:** **~4300–2600 BCE** (three-phase internal sequence: Early 4300–3500 BCE; Middle 3500–2800/3000 BCE; Late 2800/3000–2400/2600 BCE — sources vary on exact internal boundaries). Sources: [factsanddetails.com, citing Princeton University Art Museum 2004](https://factsanddetails.com/china/cat2/sub1/entry-5372.html); [china-underground.com](https://china-underground.com/2024/02/04/prehistoric-china-from-neolithic-cultures-to-the-dawn-of-dynasties/) (both secondary — used for the phase framework, cross-checked against the ScienceDirect paper below). A ScienceDirect Bayesian chronological-modeling paper gives a modeled onset of **~4500–3900 BCE** and end of **~2100–1800 BCE**. Source: [ScienceDirect, Bayesian chronology paper](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352409X16305648).
- **Dawenkou pottery inscriptions:** radiocarbon dated to **~2780 BCE ± 145 years** (late Dawenkou phase). Source: [Brill, book chapter](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004714311/BP000012.xml).
- **Significance:** Eastern-China Neolithic culture noted for early pottery inscriptions sometimes discussed (cautiously) in relation to the later development of Chinese writing.
- **Flags:** The overall span (4300–2600 BCE popular framing vs. 4500–1800 BCE Bayesian-modeled framing) diverges by several centuries at both ends; the Bayesian source is the more rigorous statistical treatment and should be preferred for precise child-entity boundaries.

### 2.8 Hemudu site/culture
- **Dates — presented with an explicit tension between two dating approaches, not resolved by averaging:**
  - Radiocarbon (older tradition): earliest occupation (Layer 4) **~5000–4900 BCE**; main village activity ceasing **~4600 BCE**; later storage-pit layer (3B) **4400–4000 BCE**. Source: [UCL, Hemudu review PDF](http://www.homepages.ucl.ac.uk/~tcrndfu/articles/Hemudu%20review.pdf). Two earliest radiocarbon dates specifically for rice cultivation: **~5000 and ~4800 BC**. Source: [Archaeologs](https://www.archaeologs.com/w/hemudu/en).
  - Paleomagnetic chronology (Zheng et al. 2021): Hemudu culture began **~7,000 cal BP** (i.e., roughly 5050 BCE-equivalent, but derived from an entirely different dating method). Source: [ScienceDirect 2021](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0031018221000821).
  - Broader Hemudu-culture range from a separate paleoenvironmental paper: **7230–5070 cal BP**. Source: [ljmu-9.eprints-hosting.org PDF](https://ljmu-9.eprints-hosting.org/id/eprint/4021/1/Catena%20manuscript%20accepted%20version.pdf).
- **Significance:** Key early-rice-farming site of the lower Yangtze, contemporary with/complementary to the Yangtze rice centre entity already in the dataset (not re-researched here).

- **Significance:** Liangzhu is one of the strongest currently available archaeological cases for pre-Xia/pre-Erlitou state-level social complexity in China, directly relevant to how the Erlitou/Xia debate (Part 3) should be framed.
- **Flags:** None regarding the core PNAS/UNESCO/Cambridge figures (well corroborated); the Wikipedia sub-site figures are explicitly marked locator-only and should be re-verified against a primary excavation report before being written into a dated child entity.

## PART 3 — ⚑ ERLITOU AND THE XIA QUESTION

### 3.1 Erlitou site — radiocarbon dating (multiple successive revisions)
- **⚑ LITERATURE MOVED, in (at least) three successive waves:**
  1. **20th-century radiocarbon (wide error margins):** Erlitou culture dated broadly to **2100–1300 BC** — a range that fit comfortably within traditionally reconstructed Xia dates. (Reported via Wikipedia, locator only — [Xia dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xia_dynasty) — needs independent non-Wikipedia primary-source confirmation; treat this specific figure as `n.a.` pending that confirmation.)
  2. **Xia-Shang-Zhou Chronology Project (1996–2000):** refined Erlitou-culture range to **1880–1520 BC**; assigned all four Erlitou phases to the Xia period; dated Xia dynasty's beginning to **c. 2070 BCE** (vs. a traditional textual estimate of 2205 BCE); Shang beginning to **c. 1600 BCE** (associated with the Yanshi/Zhengzhou walled cities); Zhou beginning to **c. 1046 BCE**, the latter anchored partly via astronomical/wiggle-matching evidence (King Wu's conquest dated to 20 January 1046 BC, within a ¹⁴C-constrained 1050–1020 BC window).
  3. **Cambridge Core *Antiquity* (Li Liu, 2009), independently fetched and directly stating a chronological range for Erlitou culture:** **c. 1900–1500 BC**. Source: [Cambridge Core, Li Liu 2009, "Academic freedom, political correctness, and early civilisation in Chinese archaeology"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/academic-freedom-political-correctness-and-early-civilisation-in-chinese-archaeology-the-debate-on-xiaerlitou-relations/C5AB38AAD524216D82B9B31F713F6D22).
  4. **2005–2006 refinement:** Xinzhai phase dated **1870–1720 BC**; Erlitou culture narrowed to **1735–1530 BC**.
  5. **Wu Xiaohong (Peking University, 2007) study on lower-layer organic samples:** pushed the START of Erlitou EVEN LATER, to **~1750 BCE** — making it, in the words of the secondary science-journalism source, "much more difficult to connect Erlitou with the Xia, which was supposedly ending at the time Erlitou was evolving." Source: [Andrew Lawler, *Science*, "Founding Dynasty or Myth?"](https://www.andrewlawler.com/scimag2010jandynasty/) (author's own site hosting the *Science* article; used as the fetched source for this claim).
  6. **UC eScholarship paper (citing Qiu, Cai & Zhang 2006):** "wiggle-matching" ¹⁴C techniques dated the site to **1750–1520 BCE**, compressing Erlitou's total occupation span to roughly 200 years, with only the final ~50 years of that span proposed as Shang (rather than Xia) occupation. Source: [escholarship.org PDF](https://escholarship.org/content/qt9df4w6kn/qt9df4w6kn_noSplash_a8c2cbb73ce509220814d06ba4327e0f.pdf).
  7. **January 2025 Chinese state-media report** (China.org.cn), citing new architectural/infrastructure findings and carbon dating: site dated **3,750 to 3,520 years ago** (≈1750–1520 BCE-equivalent), interpreted in that report as supporting Erlitou being a late Xia capital. Source: [china.org.cn, Jan 2025](http://www.china.org.cn/arts/2025-01/17/content_117670346.htm). **Note bias caveat:** this is Chinese state media reporting on a claim favorable to the Xia-identification position; cross-check against independent peer review before treating as a neutral chronological update.
- **Significance:** The successive narrowing and lateward shift of Erlitou's dated span (from a comfortable fit with textual Xia dates toward a range that starts closer to or after the traditionally reconstructed END of Xia) is itself the central empirical fact driving skepticism about a simple Erlitou = Xia equation.
- **Flags:** ⚑ LITERATURE MOVED (multiple waves, as listed). Do not present any single one of these ranges as "the" dating of Erlitou — the field has genuinely moved across at least three distinct radiocarbon-refinement episodes (Chronology Project 1996–2000; Wu Xiaohong 2007; and note the China.org.cn 2025 popular report should not be conflated with a new independent peer-reviewed dating study, since it does not name or link the underlying paper).

### 3.2 The Xia–Erlitou identification debate (Chinese vs. Western/overseas scholarship) — BOTH SIDES, NOT AVERAGED
This is a genuinely contested, historiographically and politically sensitive question. Two independently fetched academic sources were used and are summarized separately.

**Source A — Chen Chun, sociostudies.org, "Erlitou and Xia: A Dispute between Chinese and Overseas Scholars"** ([full article](https://www.sociostudies.org/journal/articles/1843103/)):
- **Position 1 (most Chinese scholars, plus a few overseas colleagues):** Erlitou culture *is* the material manifestation of the Xia state/dynasty.
  - Tong Zhuchen: correlated the *Zhushu Jinian: Xiaji* textual claim of a **471-year** Xia duration with two Erlitou radiocarbon dates, and used the Yi/Luo river reference in *Guo Yu: Zhou Yu* to fix Erlitou as the Xia capital geographically.
  - Zou Heng: defined three "diagnostic" Xia-culture artifact traits (round-bottomed pottery; a square pottery *ding* implying early bronze casting; ritual vessels *gu*, *jue*, *he*) and concluded Erlitou = Xia culture.
  - Xia–Shang–Zhou Chronology Project / Zhuang Chunbo: compared the C14 range of Erlitou's four phases (**2000–1600 BC**, per this source) against the textually estimated Xia chronology (**2061–1554 BC**) and concluded all four Erlitou phases fell within Xia.
  - Wangchenggang site (Gaocheng, Dengfeng) C14 range **2190–1965 BC** cited as corresponding to Xia chronology.
  - Xia Nai (1977) catalogued four competing internal positions on which Longshan/Erlitou phases belonged to Xia vs. Shang (see article for full breakdown).
- **Position 2 (a middle position):** Liu Li & Chen Xingcan argue Erlitou phase II shows state-level political organization but prefer the neutral label "the Erlitou State" rather than asserting "the Xia Dynasty." Liu Li & Xu Hong further argue Chinese scholarship has focused excessively on ethnic/dynastic labeling at the expense of craft-production, agricultural, and settlement-pattern research.
- **Position 3 (most overseas/Western scholars):** Erlitou cannot be securely identified as Xia without contemporaneous written evidence; the very dynastic status of "Xia" as described in later historical texts cannot be confirmed by archaeology alone.
  - R. L. Thorp: questions whether Erlitou's large rammed-earth foundations were really "palaces" (proposes "palace-temples" instead, or a ceremonial-center function); criticizes extrapolating social stratification from a small number of large burials (noting Erlitou's funerary remains are dwarfed by those at Taosi and at Liangzhu); questions treating bronze/jade possession as an inherent marker of royal power.
  - R. W. Bagley: notes the earliest written record of "Xia" appears only in Zhou-dynasty texts, and that Shang oracle-bone texts show no sign of a Xia concept at all; raises the possibility that Xia was a Western Zhou-era historical fiction invented to justify the Zhou overthrow of Shang.
- **Article's own stated overall distribution:** "most Chinese scholars and a few overseas colleagues" affirm Erlitou = Xia; "most overseas scholars" maintain the identification is unconfirmable without contemporaneous texts. The article's own concluding position is that it is currently unnecessary to force an Erlitou = Xia correspondence, and that archaeology can investigate early Chinese state formation on its own terms.

**Source B — Li Liu, 2009, Cambridge Core *Antiquity*** ([full article](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/academic-freedom-political-correctness-and-early-civilisation-in-chinese-archaeology-the-debate-on-xiaerlitou-relations/C5AB38AAD524216D82B9B31F713F6D22)):
- States plainly: "most Chinese archaeologists and historians believe that the Erlitou site represents the material culture of an early dynasty, identified as Xia or Shang," while "most scholars in the West have reservations regarding interpretations identifying Erlitou with the material culture of an early dynasty, Xia or Shang." This source frames the divide as partly a matter of academic-freedom and political-correctness pressures within Chinese archaeology specifically, and gives Erlitou's chronological range as **c. 1900–1500 BC** without further radiocarbon breakdown on the fetched page.
- **Note on the two sources' internal consistency:** Source A gives an Erlitou C14 range of "2000–1600 BC" when reporting the Chinese-scholarship position, while Source B (an Antiquity peer-reviewed article) gives "c. 1900–1500 BC" as the general range — these are close but not identical, consistent with §3.1's point that the range has been repeatedly revised across different studies; do not treat either single figure as definitive.

- **Significance:** This is the single most politically charged dating question in the entire brief. The historiographic core of the dispute is NOT primarily about radiocarbon precision (though that matters, see §3.1) but about whether "archaeological culture" can legitimately be equated with "ethnic group," "state," and "dynasty" as named in much later historical texts — a methodological disagreement, not merely a factual one.
- **Sources:** [sociostudies.org, Chen Chun](https://www.sociostudies.org/journal/articles/1843103/); [Cambridge Core Antiquity, Li Liu 2009](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/academic-freedom-political-correctness-and-early-civilisation-in-chinese-archaeology-the-debate-on-xiaerlitou-relations/C5AB38AAD524216D82B9B31F713F6D22); [Andrew Lawler/Science, "Founding Dynasty or Myth?"](https://www.andrewlawler.com/scimag2010jandynasty/).
- **Flags:** COMMON MISCONCEPTION — popular/general audiences often assume "Erlitou = Xia" is settled archaeological fact; per BOTH fetched academic sources, this is explicitly NOT the case in Western/overseas scholarship, and even within Chinese scholarship there is an internal minority position (Liu Li, Chen Xingcan, Xu Hong) preferring the neutral "Erlitou State" label over "Xia Dynasty." Present both positions with their respective standing; do not adjudicate a winner.

## PART 4 — PEOPLING AND EARLY HOLOCENE / LATE PLEISTOCENE

### 4.1 Xianrendong Cave (Jiangxi) — early-pottery claim
- **Dates — a genuine, currently unresolved dispute between research groups, presented as competing claims:**
  - **Wu et al. 2012 (Science):** earliest pottery-bearing archaeological contexts dated **20,000–19,000 cal BP** (calendar years before present), i.e. contemporaneous with the Last Glacial Maximum — "2,000 to 3,000 years older than other pottery found in East Asia and elsewhere." Method: 10 AMS radiocarbon dates from the lowest sherd-bearing levels (layers 2B/2B1), led by Wu Xiaohong with Peking University, Boston University, Harvard, and Eberhard Karls University Tübingen collaborators. Source: [Science, Wu et al. 2012, "Early Pottery at 20,000 Years Ago in Xianrendong Cave, China"](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1218643).
  - **Yanshina 2019, critical re-analysis:** disputes the ~20,000 cal BP figure, arguing the underlying dates "have an obscure context, contradict the TL-datings of the ceramics themselves, and are in poor agreement with the paleoclimatic data," and concludes the most likely date for the earliest South China pottery is instead near "the border of the Younger Dryas and the Holocene" (i.e., much closer to ~11,700 cal BP, substantially younger than Wu et al.'s claim). Source: [Yanshina, *Kunstkamera* journal 2019, "Some Remarks on the Ages of the Earliest..."](https://journal.kunstkamera.ru/en/archive/2019_3/yanshina_ov_o_vozraste_drevnejshej_keramiki_yuzhnogo_kitaya).
  - **Kuzmin (core.ac.uk hosted paper), also critical:** explicitly states "the claim for earlier pottery in South China at the Xianrendong Cave, supposedly dated to ca. 20,000 cal BP, cannot be substantiated," and instead re-derives a date of ca. **14,700 cal BP** (using the youngest reliable value, 12,530±140 BP from the overlying stratum 3C1A) as more defensible, while separately noting the *oldest* component (western section) computes to a ¹⁴C age of ~16,915 BP → **19,950–20,880 cal BP**, i.e. acknowledging the raw calibrated range while disputing its archaeological reliability. Source: [core.ac.uk PDF, "The origins of pottery in East Asia and neighboring regions"](https://core.ac.uk/download/pdf/287445762.pdf).
  - A separate academic paper (Kuzmin-affiliated, academia.edu-hosted) gives yet another figure: "recent estimates place the age of pottery at Xianrendong Cave between approximately 17,500 to 16,100 BP." Source: [academia.edu, "The Earliest Pottery of Xianrendong Cave: What Do We Know About It?"](https://www.academia.edu/37683755/THE_EARLIEST_POTTERY_OF_XIANRENDONG_CAVE_WHAT_DO_WE_KNOW_ABOUT_IT).
- **Significance:** If the Wu et al. 2012 figure stands, Xianrendong holds the current record for the world's oldest known pottery, predating the Japanese and Yuchanyan claims; but this figure is genuinely disputed in the specialist ceramic-dating literature (Yanshina, Kuzmin), unlike most other entries in this dataset.
- **Flags:** ⚑ Do NOT present "20,000 cal BP" as settled fact — a real methodological dispute exists between the original excavation team (Wu et al., Science, peer-reviewed) and independent re-analysts (Yanshina; Kuzmin) who argue for a substantially younger, though still Late-Pleistocene/early-Holocene-boundary, date. Present both positions with their sourcing. COMMON MISCONCEPTION: many popular news accounts (Guardian, Korea Times, chemistry-trade press) report "20,000 years ago" as an uncontested fact; the specialist re-analysis literature shows this is contested.

### 4.2 Yuchanyan Cave (Hunan) — early-pottery claim (previously flagged in this dataset as unresolved — treated cautiously per task instructions)
- **Dates — again a case of divergent claims across studies, not averaged:**
  - **Boaretto et al. 2009 (PNAS), the dedicated dating study fetched directly:** dates range from **~21,000 to 13,800 cal BP** across the sampled sequence; the pottery itself is dated to **18,300–15,430 cal BP**, with the most secure earliest-pottery estimate at **18,300–17,500 cal BP (1 SD)**. Specific associated dates: charcoal closely associated with sherds → 16,700–15,850 cal BP; organic residue from the ceramic itself → 17,750–16,900 cal BP. Source: [PNAS 2009, Boaretto et al., "Radiocarbon dating of charcoal and bone collagen associated with early pottery at Yuchanyan Cave"](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.0900539106).
  - **Keally (U. Arizona Radiocarbon journal PDF), citing Zhao and Wu 2000 for earlier lab results:** charcoal from a pottery/rice-grain-bearing layer at Yuchanyan → 13,680±270 BP (uncalibrated, lab BA95058); pottery organics → 14,390±230 BP and 11,970±120 BP (uncalibrated). This same source states the "oldest pottery in East Asia and the whole Old World is now reliably dated to about 13,700–13,300 BP (about 17,200–14,700 cal BP)" across three regions (Japan; lower Amur/Russian Far East; south China), a claim that treats Yuchanyan/Xianrendong and Japanese Incipient Jōmon pottery as roughly contemporaneous rather than the south-China sites being unambiguously older. Source: [Keally, U. Arizona Radiocarbon PDF](https://journals.uair.arizona.edu/index.php/radiocarbon/article/viewFile/4273/3698).
  - Frontiers 2023 overview treats Boaretto et al.'s 18,300–15,430 cal BP figure as the accepted refinement over earlier, less secure estimates of "16.0 cal ka BP" (Yuan et al. 1997). Source: [Frontiers 2023, as above](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2023.1064717/full).
- **Significance:** Alongside Xianrendong, one of the two leading claimants for the world's oldest pottery; unlike Xianrendong's 20,000 cal BP claim, Yuchanyan's Boaretto et al. 2009 PNAS dating (18,300–15,430 cal BP) is comparatively less contested in the fetched literature, though Keally's alternative framing (13,700–13,300 uncalibrated BP / ~17,200–14,700 cal BP) is not identical and should be treated as a second, slightly younger estimate rather than confirmation of the same number.
- **Flags:** Per the task's explicit caution (this site was "previously researched for this dataset and NOT authored because sources conflicted"), this pass continues to find some numerical inconsistency between (a) the dedicated 2009 PNAS dating study (18,300–15,430 cal BP) and (b) Keally's citation of the older Zhao & Wu 2000 lab results (13,680–14,390 uncalibrated BP, converting to a younger calibrated range). AN AUTHORING PASS SHOULD PREFER THE PNAS 2009 STUDY as the dedicated, most rigorous, most recent primary dating paper, but should retain a flag that older lab results (Zhao & Wu 2000) gave a somewhat younger calibrated range, and should NOT claim full consensus.

- Broader north-China Late Pleistocene lithic context: sites with externally-derived lithic technologies distributed largely north of 34°N date to roughly **38,000–24,000 BP**, with one specific layer-5/6 sequence dated to **28,490–26,989 BP** (radiocarbon). Source: [interesjournals.org, "Dispersion of ancient humans in east Asia during the late Pleistocene"](https://www.interesjournals.org/articles/dispersion-of-ancient-humans-in-east-asia-duringthe-late-pleistocene-geography-archaeology-andecology.pdf).
- **Significance:** These sites establish the pre-Holocene human-occupation backdrop against which the early-pottery and early-agriculture developments (Parts 1, 2, 4.1, 4.2) took place; none of them show evidence of pottery or agriculture themselves.
- **Flags:** The interesjournals.org source's editorial rigor was not independently verified beyond the specific radiocarbon figures cited (which are attributed to a lab-code-referenced table); treat the general "38,000–24,000 BP" north-China framing as broadly indicative context rather than a precisely pinned figure, but the Tianyuan Cave figure (34,430±510 BP / 38,120–40,940 cal BP) is a specific lab-coded date and can be cited with confidence.

## PART 5 — KOREA: CHULMUN AND MUMUN PERIODS

### 5.1 Chulmun (Jeulmun) period — overall span and internal sub-periods
- **Dates — multiple sourced sub-period schemes, generally consistent on the overall span but differing on internal phase boundaries:**
  - Overall span most commonly given: **c. 8,000–1,500 BC** (per [Wikipedia, "Mumun pottery period," locator only](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mumun_pottery_period), stating "the Mumun period is preceded by the Jeulmun pottery period (c. 8000–1500 BC)" — locator only, cross-checked below against academic sources giving compatible ranges).
  - Academic four-phase scheme (Cambridge Core, "Archaeolinguistic evidence for the farming/language dispersal of Koreanic"): Incipient Neolithic 7500–5000 BC; Early Neolithic 5000–3500 BC; Middle Neolithic 3500–2000 BC; Late Neolithic 2000–1300 BC (all in BC calendar years, ceramic-style-defined). Source: [Cambridge Core PDF](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/CE1DF81011ED49CED531AA6475959157/S2513843X20000493a.pdf/archaeolinguistic-evidence-for-the-farminglanguage-dispersal-of-koreanic.pdf).
  - Alternative three-phase scheme (Current Anthropology, cal BP framing): Early Chulmun **7500–5500 cal BP**; Middle Chulmun **5500–4000 cal BP**; Late Chulmun **4000–3400 cal BP**. Source: [University of Chicago Press Journals, *Current Anthropology* 52:S4, "The Transition from Foraging to Farming in Prehistoric Korea"](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/658488).
  - A comparative Jōmon/Chulmun table (already cited in Part 1.1) gives: Chulmun Final N/A; Late 4000–3300 cal BP; Middle 5500–4000 cal BP; Early 7000–5500 cal BP; Incipient 11,500–7000 cal BP. Source: [studocu-hosted course document citing Habu, as in Part 1.1](https://www.studocu.com/en-us/document/tulsa-community-college/ancient-and-medieval-western-civilization-h/temporal-and-regional-variability-of-jomon-and-chulmun-cultures-ch22/140129705).
  - "The Neolithic in Korea began around 10,000 BP" per a University of Arizona *Radiocarbon* journal paper, which also notes small-scale millet cultivation was probably present in Korea by the Middle Neolithic (~5500–4200 BP). Source: [journals.uair.arizona.edu PDF, "Reconstructing human subsistence strategies..."](https://journals.uair.arizona.edu/index.php/radiocarbon/article/download/16271/pdf).
- **Significance:** Chulmun is Korea's Neolithic ceramic-and-forager-to-early-cultivator period, directly comparable to (and used in cross-dating with) the Japanese Jōmon sequence.
- **Flags:** The various academic schemes above disagree on both the total starting point (7500 BC vs. ~10,000 BP vs. 8000 BC) and on internal phase boundaries by centuries; this is presented as a real range of scholarly framings, not resolved into one canonical scheme. All calendar-year (BC) figures above are from the Cambridge/Koreanic paper; all cal BP figures are from the Current Anthropology and Arizona Radiocarbon papers — these unit families are NOT drop-in interchangeable without explicit calendar conversion, and any authoring pass must keep the unit label attached to its source.

### 5.2 Millet arrival in Korea (Chulmun period)
- **Dates:** Middle Chulmun period; earliest secure DIRECT AMS date on a millet grain: foxtail millet, Beta-252973, 4740±40 BP → calibrated **3640–3370 cal BC** ("earliest secure date"). Source: [UCL Discovery PDF, Stevens & Fuller, "The spread of agriculture in eastern Asia"](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10052010/1/Stevens_Fuller_Agricultural%20Bases%20in%20East%20Asia%20final%20ms.pdf).
- Corroborating figures: foxtail millet from Tongsamdong site AMS-dated to **3360 cal BC**; foxtail millet from Neunggok site AMS-dated to **3520±100 cal BC**; millet from Pyeonggeodong AMS-dated to **2970±50 cal BC**. Source: [Kyushu University repository PDF, "The Initial Spread of Early Agriculture into Northeast Asia," *Asian Archaeology* Vol. 3](https://api.lib.kyushu-u.ac.jp/opac_download_md/1812318/Asian%20Archaeology%20Vol.3.pdf).
- A recent Bayesian lipid-residue paper gives a posterior estimate for millet's arrival in Korea of **90% HPDI: 5,772–3,490 cal BCE**, with a narrower "first 500 years" posterior median window of **6,109–5,609 BP**. Source: [ercrema.github.io PDF, Craig et al. 2025, "Lipid residue analysis reveals divergent culinary practices"](https://ercrema.github.io/files/Craig_etal_2025.pdf).
- **Significance:** Millet (foxtail and broomcorn) was Korea's first cultivated cereal, arriving via northeast China roughly two millennia before rice.
- **Flags:** The Bayesian 2025 paper's wide 90% HPDI (5,772–3,490 cal BCE) is a broad uncertainty interval, not a point estimate — do not report its lower or upper bound alone as "the" date; the UCL/Kyushu direct-AMS dates (~3640–2970 cal BC) represent the tighter, more commonly cited "secure" window within that broader Bayesian interval.

### 5.3 Mumun period — overall span and rice arrival
- **Dates:** Overall span most consistently given as **c. 1500–300 BC** (per [Wikipedia, "Mumun pottery period," locator only](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mumun_pottery_period) — used here only as a locator, cross-checked against the academic figures below, which are compatible). Academic corroboration: "It is generally believed that early agriculture on the Korean Peninsula was established during the Mumun period (1500–100 BC)" and "Early Mumun period (1500–800 BC)." Source: [Hanyang University scholarworks PDF, "Direct isotopic evidence for human millet consumption in the Middle Mumun period"](https://scholarworks.bwise.kr/hanyang/bitstream/2021.sw.hanyang/173162/1/Direct%20isotopic%20evidence%20for%20human%20millet%20consumption%20in%20the%20Middle%20Mumun%20period%20Implication%20and%20importance%20of%20millets%20in%20early%20agriculture%20on%20the%20Korean%20Peninsula.pdf). A University of Toronto PhD thesis gives a calibrated span for the Mumun Pottery Period of **2903–2450 cal BP**. Source: [tspace.library.utoronto.ca PDF, Bale doctoral thesis](https://tspace.library.utoronto.ca/bitstream/1807/31685/1/Bale_Martin_T_201111_PhD_thesis.pdf).
- **Middle Mumun human remains, directly AMS-dated:** three individuals date to approximately **770–410 cal BC (2σ)**, with median probability dates of 625 cal BC, 535 cal BC, and 635 cal BC respectively — placing them in the "Middle Mumun period (800–400 BC)" by the paper's own periodization. Source: [Hanyang scholarworks PDF, as above].
- **Rice arrival:** Rice reached the Korean Peninsula with the Mumun culture, associated with irrigated agriculture, via a Shandong → Liaodong → Korea route, "around 1500 BCE" or, per a stricter linguistic-archaeological source, associated with Proto-Japanic dispersal "around 1300 BCE." Sources: [ninjal.ac.jp PDF, Whitman 2012, "Rice is nice"](https://www2.ninjal.ac.jp/past-projects/languagedispersal/Whitman2012Rice.pdf); [Cambridge Core Koreanic paper, as in 5.1](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/CE1DF81011ED49CED531AA6475959157/S2513843X20000493a.pdf/archaeolinguistic-evidence-for-the-farminglanguage-dispersal-of-koreanic.pdf).
- **Earliest directly AMS-dated rice grains in Korea (Oun-1 site, Jinju, along the Nam River):** AMS range **2860–1320 cal BC** (An 2004); the same samples are alternatively calibrated by Crawford & Lee (2003) to **1950–1000 cal BC**. Source: [University of Hawaiʻi ScholarSpace PDF, "Sedentism, Territorial Circumscription..."](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/4b87d491-b666-4fb4-8497-d9c432910073/content).
- **Significance:** Mumun marks Korea's Bronze-Age transition to intensive dry-field and paddy-field rice agriculture and is the direct chronological/cultural predecessor of the Korean Bronze Age proper.
- **Flags:** Two different calibration treatments of the SAME Oun-1 rice-grain samples (An 2004: 2860–1320 cal BC; Crawford & Lee 2003: 1950–1000 cal BC) diverge substantially — an explicit example of how calibration-program/parameter choice can shift a reported range by centuries even from identical raw data; always specify which calibration was used. COMMON MISCONCEPTION: extremely old outlier claims exist for "rice" in Korea (e.g., carbonized rice-like material from Sorori radiocarbon-dated to **17,000–13,000 BP**, per [journal.archaeology.nsc.ru PDF](https://journal.archaeology.nsc.ru/jour/article/viewFile/250/319)) — these are explicitly described in that same source as involving "quasi-rice" and as heavily disputed/challenged claims, NOT accepted evidence of Late-Pleistocene rice cultivation in Korea; do not conflate with the Mumun-period cultivated-rice dates above.
