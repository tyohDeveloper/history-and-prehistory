# Empires Research: Dates, Disputes, Conventions, and Misconceptions

Compiled as a sourcing audit for a historical dataset. House rules applied throughout: every date is sourced with a URL; disagreements are reported as competing named positions, not averaged; conventions are flagged as conventions; misconceptions are flagged; and where a periodisation is irreconcilable or arbitrary, that is stated explicitly with a recommendation not to author a single value.

---

## 1. The British Empire

### 1.1 Start date — no single defensible date; depends on definition

There is no scholarly consensus start date because the answer depends on which criterion is used ("first claim" vs. "first colony declared" vs. "first permanent settlement"). Three candidates, each defensible under a different definition:

- **1497 — John Cabot's voyage / claim.** Henry VII sponsored Cabot's 1496–1497 voyages, which established England's first overseas *claim* (not a settlement) — cited via [Encyclopedia.com, "Empire, British"](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/empire-british). No permanent settlement resulted from this voyage.
- **1583 — Humphrey Gilbert's Newfoundland settlement/ceremony.** Gilbert "established a settlement in Newfoundland in 1583," per [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/empire-british). Often cited as the first formal English overseas colony *claim with ceremony*, though it did not produce a lasting colony.
- **1607 — Jamestown.** Most commonly cited as England's first **permanent** settlement in the Americas: [Britannica, "British Empire Timeline"](https://www.britannica.com/summary/British-Empire-Timeline); [Britannica, "In What Order Were the 13 American Colonies Established?"](https://www.britannica.com/topic/In-What-Order-Were-the-13-American-Colonies-Established); [Wikipedia, "British colonization of the Americas"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_colonization_of_the_Americas).

**Assessment**: This is a genuine definitional fork, not a factual dispute — sources do not disagree about what happened in 1497, 1583, or 1607; they disagree about which event counts as "the start of the empire." **Recommendation: do not author a single "start date" without specifying the criterion used** (claim vs. settlement attempt vs. permanent settlement). If a single field is required, 1607 (Jamestown) is the most defensible under the "permanent settlement" criterion and is what most tertiary sources default to, but this should be labeled as a choice of criterion, not an uncontested fact.

### 1.2 First Empire / Second Empire split — a real historiographical framework, but contested as an oversimplification

The claim that the loss of the American colonies (1783, Treaty of Paris) marks a hinge between a "First" and "Second" British Empire is a genuine, long-standing framework in the literature — not merely a textbook invention — but it is actively challenged by specialist historians as too clean.

- **Standard/mainstream framing**: [Britannica, "Loss of the American colonies"](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Western-colonialism/Loss-of-the-American-colonies) — "In 1763 the first British Empire primarily centred on North America. By 1815, despite the loss of the 13 colonies, Britain had a second empire..."
- **Wikipedia's historiography summary**, reflecting standard textbook treatment: [Historiography of the British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historiography_of_the_British_Empire) — "the first British Empire was largely destroyed by the loss of the American colonies, followed by a 'swing to the east'... So when it began, when it ended, and what stages it went through is a matter of opinion, not official orders or laws."
- **Specialist challenge #1**: P. J. Marshall, in *The Making and Unmaking of Empires* (Oxford University Press), explicitly **contests** the clean split: "This book contests that view, arguing that both losses in America and gains in India were part of a single phase of British imperial history" — [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/book/2601).
- **Specialist challenge #2**: Cambridge University Press notes, "Historians of the eighteenth-century British Empire have protested against any easy separation between the 'First' and 'Second' British Empires on the grounds that the two overlapped in time, that they shared common purposes and personnel..." — [Cambridge excerpt](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805215/90815/excerpt/9780521590815_excerpt.pdf).

**Verdict**: This is a genuine scholarly disagreement, not a convention-vs-fact issue. Popular/tertiary sources (Britannica, Encyclopedia.com) treat the two-empire split as standard periodisation; specialist historians (Marshall; Cambridge scholars) argue it is artificial and overdrawn because the "first" and "second" empires overlapped in time, geography, and personnel. **Report both positions; do not present the First/Second Empire split as settled fact.**

### 1.3 End date — genuinely disputed among at least four competing positions

There is no single agreed end date for the British Empire. Four positions, each held by identifiable historians or institutions:

- **1947 (Indian independence)** — the most common institutional/scholarly marker for the end of the empire's core: [British Academy, Judith Brown, "The making and breaking of states"](https://www.thebritishacademy.ac.uk/documents/1547/JBA-001-133-Brown.pdf); [Britannica, "British Raj"](https://www.britannica.com/event/British-raj); [National Army Museum, "Independence and Partition 1947"](https://www.nam.ac.uk/explore/independence-and-partition-1947).
- **1956 (Suez Crisis)** — held by diplomatic historians as the moment that exposed Britain's loss of great-power/imperial status: "Diplomatic historians...seem agreed that the...invasion of Egypt in early November 1956 signalled the approaching demise of empire" — [Australian Institute of International Affairs](https://www.internationalaffairs.org.au/australianoutlook/the-suez-dispute-and-the-death-of-empire/); see also [BBC, "Suez: End of Empire"](http://news.bbc.co.uk/2/hi/middle_east/5199392.stm). Other historians argue this overstates a single event relative to longer-term American pressure and British financial exhaustion — the claim is not universal even among those who treat Suez as significant.
- **1960 ("Wind of Change")** — Macmillan's 3 February 1960 speech to the South African Parliament is used by many historians as **shorthand** for the acceleration of decolonization, not a literal end date. Historian Wm. Roger Louis treats 1959–1963 as a distinct final imperial stage; the speech is described as "frequently used as shorthand for decolonization by scholars" — [University of Kansas, ScholarWorks thesis](https://kuscholarworks.ku.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/d5517686-6703-4d1b-91c3-2a79cd1d4560/content); see also [Oxford Academic, *Twentieth Century British History* review](https://academic.oup.com/tcbh/article/26/2/340/1675852).
- **1997 (Hong Kong handover)** — held by "many, including King Charles III," per Wikipedia, as the symbolic end, since it left Britain without significant overseas territory for the first time since the 1707 Acts of Union: [Wikipedia, "Handover of Hong Kong"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handover_of_Hong_Kong); [Britannica, "Handover of Hong Kong"](https://www.britannica.com/event/handover-of-Hong-Kong). This is best characterized as a popular/symbolic marker rather than a technical historians' consensus.

**Verdict**: This is a genuine, unresolved historiographical and popular disagreement with at least four distinct, source-attributable positions. **Do not pick a winner or average these dates. Report all four with their proponents and rationale**, as done above.

### 1.4 Act of Union 1707 — "English" vs. "British" Empire naming

Overseas colonization begun in the late 16th and early 17th centuries was carried out by the Kingdom of England (with separate, failed Scottish colonization attempts, e.g., the Darien scheme). It only becomes legally and terminologically the "**British**" Empire after Great Britain — created by the Acts of Union 1707, uniting the Kingdoms of England and Scotland — inherited England's colonial possessions.

- [Wikipedia, "British Empire"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire) — the empire "began with... overseas possessions and trading posts established by England in the late 16th and early 17th centuries, and colonisation attempts by Scotland."
- [Wikipedia, "Handover of Hong Kong"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handover_of_Hong_Kong) — notes Great Britain "inherited the colonial possessions of the Kingdom of England upon the passing of the Acts of Union 1707."
- [Wikipedia, "Acts of Union 1707"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acts_of_Union_1707) — the Acts, ratified 1 May 1707, joined the Kingdoms of England and Scotland into the single political state of Great Britain.
- Academic treatment of the imperial dimension of Union: Allan I. Macinnes, *Union and Empire: The Making of the United Kingdom in 1707* (Cambridge University Press) — explicitly "sets the Act of Union within a broad European and colonial context" and "assesses the imperial significance of the creation of the United Kingdom," per the publisher description at [University of Strathclyde PurePortal](https://pureportal.strath.ac.uk/en/publications/union-and-empire-the-making-of-the-united-kingdom-in-1707/) and reviewed in the [American Historical Review](https://academic.oup.com/ahr/article-abstract/114/2/481/42594).
- A specific scholarly treatment of the East India Company angle: "A Union for Empire? Scotland, the English East India Company and the British Union" argues that a December 1707 act extending the English East India Company's monopoly to all of Great Britain shows "what kind of empire Scotland joined in 1707" — the pre-Union English imperial structures were largely carried over unchanged, with Scots now admitted to them — [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/edinburgh-scholarship-online/book/18983/chapter-abstract/177354462?redirectedFrom=fulltext).
- A doctoral thesis on this exact terminological question notes some historians hold that only after 1707 did the "English Atlantic" become "British" (because only then was there meaningful Scottish involvement), while historian David Armitage attributes the absence of a "British" empire before 1707 to the Stuart monarchs' failure to consolidate their multiple kingdoms into a unified state — [St Andrews Research Repository, Joseph Wagner PhD thesis](https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/bitstream/handle/10023/21680/Thesis-Joseph-Wagner-complete-version.pdf).

**Verdict**: The English-vs-British naming point is well-supported, not seriously contested, and worth flagging in any dataset: pre-1707 colonial activity should be labeled "English" (with a note on failed Scottish attempts), and only post-1707 activity is properly "British."

## 2. The Dutch Empire and the VOC

### 2.1 VOC founding — 20 March 1602 (well-supported, uncontroversial)

- [Wikipedia, "Dutch East India Company"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_East_India_Company): chartered by the States General on 20 March 1602 with a 21-year trade monopoly, formed from the amalgamation of six regional pre-companies (*voorcompagnieën*).
- Heidelberg University Press: "The charter (octrooi) of the VOC, signed on 20 March 1602, marked the formal creation of the Company" — [Heidelberg University Press](https://books.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/arthistoricum/catalog/view/499/1049/87966).
- Radboud University repository thesis corroborates the same founding date — [Radboud University](https://repository.ubn.ru.nl/bitstream/handle/2066/226488/226488.pdf).

### 2.2 VOC dissolution — 1799 (charter lapse) is standard, but minor date variance exists in secondary sources (1798/1799/1800)

- **31 December 1799** is the most commonly cited date, marking the formal lapse of the VOC's charter: [Wikipedia, "Dutch East India Company"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_East_India_Company) infobox ("Defunct: 31 December 1799... Dissolved and nationalised as Dutch East Indies"); [UMass Dartmouth course PDF, "The VOC 1602–1799"](https://bpb-us-w2.wpmucdn.com/sites.umassd.edu/dist/4/628/files/2017/02/thevoc.pdf); [GlobalSecurity.org, "VOC Bankruptcy"](https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/world/indonesia/history-dutch-2.htm).
- However, several sources instead give **1800** as the year of dissolution/nationalization, apparently counting from when the government actually assumed administrative control rather than the charter's technical expiry date: [Wikipedia, "Dutch East Indies"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_East_Indies) infobox lists "Dutch East India Company: 1603–1799" then "Direct Dutch control: 31 December 1799," yet the body text says "The company was formally dissolved in 1800 and its colonial possessions... were nationalized under the Dutch Republic." [Wikipedia, "Company rule in the Dutch East Indies"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Company_rule_in_the_Dutch_East_Indies) states flatly: company rule "ended in 1800 when the bankrupt company was dissolved." [FactsAndDetails.com](https://factsanddetails.com/indonesia/History_and_Religion/sub6_1b/entry-3950.html) says the charter lapsed at the end of 1799 and the VOC "was liquidated" then, dating the effective transition to 1800.
- A Dutch source ([isgeschiedenis.nl](https://isgeschiedenis.nl/nieuws/ontbinding-van-de-verenigde-oost-indische-compagnie)) gives yet another date, 17 March 1798, for the state's assumption of the VOC's debts and assets under the new Batavian Republic — a full nationalization step distinct from the later charter lapse.

**Verdict**: There is a real (if narrow) three-way date inconsistency across sources — 1798 (Batavian Republic assumes VOC debts/assets), 31 December 1799 (charter formally lapses — the date most frequently cited as "dissolution"), and 1800 (year commonly used as the label for when direct Dutch state control began, and used in some infoboxes as "dissolved"). This is not a deep historiographical dispute so much as a process that unfolded across 1798–1800 in stages, described inconsistently by secondary sources depending on which administrative step they treat as decisive. **Recommend citing 31 December 1799 as the VOC's charter-lapse/dissolution date, and 1800 as the year the Dutch East Indies began as a formal state colony — treating both as correct but referring to different steps, not as competing claims about the same event.**

### 2.3 Dutch West India Company (WIC) — two successive charters; the dataset's "1621–1794" figure conflates them

The WIC's history is more complex than a single start/end pair suggests, and the sources found do show two distinct charters:

- **First charter**: WIC founded 3 June 1621, granted a 24-year monopoly over Dutch trade in the Americas and West Africa — [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/modern-europe/benelux-history/dutch-west-india-company); [Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/item/2021666732/); [Britannica, "Today in History: June 3, 1621"](https://www.britannica.com/today-in-history/June-3-1621-Dutch-West-India-Company-Charter); [New York State Archives Partnership Trust](https://www.nysarchivestrust.org/download_file/view/2044/971).
- The Library of Congress states this first company "ceased to exist" in 1674 — [Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/item/2021666732/).
- [Wikipedia, "Dutch colonial empire"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_colonial_empire) gives the WIC's overall span as **1621–1792**, and separately states "The Dutch West India Company was abolished in 1791, and its colonies in Suriname and the Caribbean brought under the direct rule of the state" — a one-year discrepancy (1791 vs. 1792) between the infobox and body text of the same article.
- Britannica's topic page lists the company's dates as "1621–1794" — [Britannica, "Dutch West India Company"](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Dutch-West-India-Company).

**Verdict**: The evidence is consistent with a **first WIC charter (1621–1674)** followed by a **second, reconstituted WIC** operating from the mid-1670s until its final abolition in **1791 or 1792** (sources vary by one year on this final date; Britannica's "1794" appears to be an outlier not corroborated elsewhere in this research). **This is a genuine minor sourcing inconsistency (1791 vs. 1792 vs. 1794) that should be flagged rather than resolved by fiat** — report the range as "1791/1792 (with at least one source giving 1794)" rather than asserting a single precise year. The dataset's presumed "1621–1794" figure, if that is what it currently shows, should at minimum be corrected to note that it spans two distinct corporate charters with a gap/reorganization around 1674–1675, not one continuous company.

### 2.4 Company rule vs. state colonialism — when did the Dutch East Indies become a state colony?

This transition is reasonably well-documented, though the precise "hand-off" year is described with some variation because it happened in stages (bankruptcy → nationalization → wartime interruption → restored state control):

- **1799/1800**: VOC charter lapses; company rule in the Dutch East Indies formally ends and its possessions are nationalized under the Batavian Republic. [Wikipedia, "Company rule in the Dutch East Indies"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Company_rule_in_the_Dutch_East_Indies): "Company rule in the Dutch East Indies began when the Dutch East India Company appointed the first governor-general of the Dutch East Indies in 1610, and ended in 1800 when the bankrupt company was dissolved and its possessions were nationalized as the Dutch East Indies."
- **1806–1816**: A "French and British interregnum" — the Dutch East Indies passed under French client-state control and then British occupation (Raffles' governorship) during the Napoleonic Wars, before independent Dutch state control was restored. [Wikipedia, "Dutch East Indies"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_East_Indies) infobox: "1800–1810 1816–1942"; body text: "Following Napoleon's defeat at the 1815 Battle of Waterloo and the Congress of Vienna, independent Dutch control was restored in 1816 on the basis of the Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1814."
- [Britannica, "History of Indonesia: Dutch rule from 1815 to c. 1920"](https://www.britannica.com/topic/history-of-Indonesia/Dutch-rule-from-1815-to-c-1920) corroborates 1815/1816 as the point of restored, direct Dutch state administration.
- A further institutional milestone: the Dutch Ministry of Colonies was established in **1834**, cited by some sources as the point at which the Netherlands "assumed full sovereignty" in a modern administrative sense — [FactsAndDetails.com](https://factsanddetails.com/indonesia/History_and_Religion/sub6_1b/entry-3950.html).

**Verdict**: There is no single "moment" state colonialism began; it is a multi-stage process (1799/1800 nationalization → 1806–1816 French/British interregnum → 1816 restoration of Dutch sovereignty → 1834 formal ministry-level administration). **1800 is defensible as a conventional marker for "state colony begins" (following VOC dissolution) but should be flagged as a simplification** that elides the 1806–1816 interregnum, during which the Dutch state did not actually control the territory.

### 2.5 The dataset's "dutch-eic 1800–1949" entry — labeling and end-date issues

- **Labeling issue**: The label "dutch-eic" (implying "Dutch East India Company") is a **mislabeling** if the date range 1800–1949 is intended, since the VOC (the actual East India Company) had already dissolved by 1799/1800. The 1800–1949 span corresponds to the **Dutch East Indies as a state colony**, not to company rule. This is confirmed directly by multiple sources using exactly this framing and date range: [Wikipedia, "Dutch East Indies"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_East_Indies) infobox lists company rule as "1603–1799" separately from the colony's own existence; [TimelineLibrary.com, "Dutch East Indies under colonial administration (1800–1949)"](https://www.timelinelibrary.com/exploration-expansion/colonial-expansion/asian-colonial-territories/dutch-colonial-territories/dutch-east-indies-under-colonial-administration-1800-1949) titles the state-colony period exactly "1800–1949"; a Korean-language reference site gives the same range, "1800-1949," for the colony itself (not the company) — [NamuWiki, "Dutch East Indies"](https://en.namu.wiki/w/%EB%84%A4%EB%8D%9C%EB%9E%80%EB%93%9C%EB%A0%B9%20%EB%8F%99%EC%9D%B8%EB%8F%84). **Recommend renaming the entry to something like "dutch-east-indies" or "dutch-state-colony," reserving "VOC" / "dutch-eic" for 1602–1799/1800.**
- **End date — 1945 vs. 1949 is a genuine, important legal/historiographical disagreement, not just a rounding question**:
  - **17 August 1945**: Sukarno and Hatta proclaimed Indonesian independence, two days after Japan's surrender. This is the date Indonesia itself and most historical narratives treat as independence: [Wikipedia, "Dutch East Indies"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_East_Indies) ("a Dutch colony... which declared independence on 17 August 1945"); [TheIndoProject.org](https://theindoproject.org/a-brief-history-of-the-dutch-east-indies-part-1/) ("Sukarno and Hatta declared Indonesia 'merdeka' (independent) on 17 August 1945").
  - **27 December 1949**: The Netherlands formally recognized Indonesian sovereignty, following four years of armed conflict (the Indonesian National Revolution, including Dutch "police actions") and international (especially US) diplomatic pressure: [TheIndoProject.org](https://theindoproject.org/a-brief-history-of-the-dutch-east-indies-part-1/) — "it would take another four years of conflict for the Dutch government to officially hand over sovereignty to the new nation on 27 December 1949"; [ehne.fr, "Decolonization of the Dutch East Indies/Indonesia"](https://ehne.fr/en/encyclopedia/themes/europe-europeans-and-world/europeans-and-decolonisations/decolonization-dutch-east-indiesindonesia) ("sovereignty was transferred to the nationalist government of Indonesia in 1949"); [indonesiaknowledge.com](https://indonesiaknowledge.com/articles/dutch-colonial-rule) similarly frames 1945–1949 as an unresolved interregnum ending in a "transfer of sovereignty in December 1949."
  - A further wrinkle: the [Wikipedia "History of Indonesia"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Indonesia) article notes that in December 1946 the United Nations already recorded the Netherlands' own acknowledgment that the "Netherlands Indies" was a UN Charter Article 73 non-self-governing territory — evidence that even the Dutch government's own international position during 1945–1949 was ambiguous about sovereignty.

**Verdict**: This is a genuine competing-dates situation with real stakes (whose declaration counts — the colonized people's proclamation, or the colonizer's recognition). **Report both 1945 (Indonesian proclamation, the date favored by Indonesian national history and most general narratives) and 1949 (Dutch legal recognition, the date a strictly "Dutch colonial administration" framing would use) rather than picking one.** The dataset's "1949" end date is defensible specifically if the entry is understood as "the end of the *Dutch* Dutch East Indies administration" (i.e., dated to Dutch recognition) — but this framing should be made explicit, since it implicitly privileges the colonizer's timeline over the colonized nation's own founding date.

## 3. The Spanish and Portuguese Empires

### 3.1 Spanish Empire

**Start**: Conventionally dated to 1492 (Columbus's voyage), though the first actual permanent Spanish settlement in the Americas began in 1493 on Hispaniola: [Wikipedia, "Spanish colonization of the Americas"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_colonization_of_the_Americas) — "The Spanish colonization of the Americas began in 1493... after the initial 1492 voyage." This mirrors the English case (claim vs. settlement) but is less contested since 1492 is near-universally used as shorthand.

**End date — genuinely and substantively disputed; NOT simply "1898," and this matters**:

- **1898 (Spanish–American War)** is widely used, especially in popular and American sources, as the end of the Spanish colonial era, marking the loss of Cuba, Puerto Rico, and the Philippines: [Wikipedia, "Spanish colonization of the Americas"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_colonization_of_the_Americas) — "In 1898, the United States achieved victory in the Spanish–American War with Spain, ending the Spanish colonial era." A Spanish-language academic source (Universidad de Murcia) explicitly frames 1898 as the end of a "**first**" Spanish colonial empire (the Americas/Pacific empire, 1492–1898), distinguishing it from a smaller "**second**" Spanish colonial empire in Africa that continued into the 20th century: [Universidad de Murcia, revistas.um.es](https://revistas.um.es/analeshc/article/download/87411/84141/356521).
- **1975/1976 (withdrawal from Western Sahara)** is treated by other sources as the true final end of the Spanish Empire, since Spain retained African colonial territories (Equatorial Guinea, Western Sahara, Ifni, parts of northern Morocco) well after 1898. [Wikipedia, "Spanish Empire"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Empire) infobox gives the full span as "1492–1976," explicitly listing "Withdrawal from the Spanish Sahara: 1976." The Madrid Accords were signed 14 November 1975, with Spanish administration formally ending 28 February 1976: [Real Instituto Elcano](https://www.realinstitutoelcano.org/en/analyses/between-principles-and-national-interest-50-years-since-the-spanish-withdrawal-from-the-western-sahara/); [RTVE](https://www.rtve.es/noticias/20251108/marcha-verde-50-anos-salida-espana-sahara-occidental/16795289.shtml). An Oxford Academic book epilogue states Spain "shed her last colony, the Spanish Sahara, in 1973 [likely an error for 1975/76 given the Madrid Accords date], embraced democracy in 1976" — [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/book/26162/chapter-abstract/194258812) (note: this source's "1973" figure appears to be an error inconsistent with the Madrid Accords timeline documented elsewhere and should not be relied on for the precise year).

**Verdict**: This is a genuine dual-tradition split, not a simple factual error to correct by picking one date. Historians distinguish a "first" Spanish colonial empire (1492–1898, the Americas/Pacific empire — the one most commonly meant by "the Spanish Empire" in popular and textbook usage) from a "second," smaller African colonial empire (1898–1975/76). **A dataset entry of "1492–1898" is defensible only if explicitly scoped as "the American/Pacific Spanish Empire"; if presented as the unqualified end of Spanish colonialism, it is incomplete and should be corrected to 1976, or better, both figures should be reported with the scope difference made explicit.**

**Treaty of Tordesillas**: Signed 7 June 1494 at Tordesillas, Spain; ratified by Spain on 2 July 1494 and by Portugal on 5 September 1494; later confirmed by Pope Julius II on 24 January 1506. Sources: [Britannica, "Treaty of Tordesillas"](https://www.britannica.com/event/Treaty-of-Tordesillas); [Yale Avalon Project, primary text](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/15th_century/mod001.asp); [UNESCO Memory of the World](https://www.unesco.org/en/memory-world/treaty-tordesillas); [Wikipedia, "Treaty of Tordesillas"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Tordesillas).

**Iberian Union**: 1580–1640. Philip II of Spain became king of Portugal following acclamation at the Cortes of Tomar in April 1581, ending with the Portuguese Restoration and the establishment of the House of Braganza in 1640: [Oxford Academic, *American Historical Review*](https://academic.oup.com/ahr/article/112/5/1359/41045) — "the 'Union of the Crowns' between Spain and Portugal in 1580–1581... continued under his successors... until the 'Restoration' of the House of Braganza in 1640."

### 3.2 Portuguese Empire

**Start**: 1415 — the conquest of Ceuta (21 August 1415) under King John I is the widely and consistently agreed starting point, with comparatively little dispute relative to the British and Spanish cases: [Wikipedia, "Portuguese conquest of Ceuta"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_conquest_of_Ceuta); [Cambridge University Press excerpt, *A History of Portugal and the Portuguese Empire*](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805214/09087/excerpt/9780521409087_excerpt.htm); Portuguese Wikipedia confirms 1415 is "geralmente considerada o ponto de partida" ("generally considered the starting point") — [Império Português](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imp%C3%A9rio_Portugu%C3%AAs).

**End**: 20 December 1999, the handover of Macau to China, treated as essentially uncontested — unlike the British and Spanish cases, there is no serious competing end-date position found in this research: [Wikipedia, "Handover of Macau"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handover_of_Macau); [Wikipedia, "Portuguese Empire"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_Empire) — "The handover of Macau on 20 December 1999 officially marked the end of the Portuguese Empire and the end of colonialism in Asia."

**Internal periodisation nuance (not a start/end dispute, but worth flagging)**: Historian W. G. Clarence-Smith's three-era model, cited in a Brown University e-journal, divides Portuguese imperial history into a "first" empire (Estado da Índia, East Africa to Japan), a "second" empire (South Atlantic: Brazil plus Africa), and a "third" empire (mostly Africa, following Brazilian independence in 1822) — [Brown University, e-Journal of Portuguese History PDF](https://www.brown.edu/Departments/Portuguese_Brazilian_Studies/ejph/html/issue27/pdf/v14n1a04.pdf). Portuguese Wikipedia similarly frames a "Primeiro Império (1415–1580)" ending with the Iberian Union. **The overall 1415–1999 span is well-supported, but a dataset that treats "the Portuguese Empire" as a single undifferentiated 584-year unit is eliding an internal periodisation that specialists do use — this should be noted as a simplification, not corrected as an error.**

## 4. The French Colonial Empire

### 4.1 First French colonial empire

**Start** — a genuine minor discrepancy exists between an exploration/claim date and a settlement date, echoing the English/Spanish pattern:
- **1534**: Jacques Cartier's claim of the Gaspé Bay area for France, used as the start date in [Wikipedia, "French colonial empire"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_colonial_empire) infobox.
- **1605**: The founding of Port Royal, Acadia — some sources treat this as the true start because it was the first actual French colony, as opposed to an exploration claim: [The Latin Library, "French Colonial Empires"](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/imperialism/notes/frenchempire.html) — "The story of France's colonial empire truly began on July 27, 1605 with the foundation of Port Royal."

**End of the first empire** — sources disagree on the exact year, with two figures found:
- **1814**: [Wikipedia, "French colonial empire"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_colonial_empire) gives this as the end, "by which time most of it had been lost or sold" (encompassing the 1763 Treaty of Paris losses and the 1803 sale of Louisiana).
- **1803**: [GlobalSecurity.org, "French Colonial Empire"](https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/world/europe/fr-colony-1.htm) instead frames the first empire as spanning "1603–1803," ending with the Louisiana sale rather than extending to 1814.

**Verdict**: This is a minor but real dating discrepancy (1803 vs. 1814) reflecting different choices about which loss counts as the terminal event (Louisiana sale in 1803 vs. the broader Napoleonic collapse through 1814–1815). **Flag both figures; do not silently pick one.**

### 4.2 Second French colonial empire

**Start — 1830**, the French conquest/invasion of Algiers, is essentially universally agreed: [Wikipedia, "French colonial empire"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_colonial_empire); [The Latin Library](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/imperialism/notes/frenchempire.html) — "the true beginnings of the second French colonial empire...were laid in 1830 with the French invasion of Algeria."

**End — 1962, Algerian independence**, widely treated as marking the effective end of the empire. Historian Robert Aldrich is cited (via Wikipedia's decolonization article) for the view that "with Algerian independence in 1962, it appeared that the Empire practically had come to an end" — [Wikipedia, "Decolonisation of Africa"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decolonisation_of_Africa).

**Important nuance — the precise day within 1962 is itself contested, and one candidate date is an explicit CONVENTION**:
- The Évian Accords ceasefire was signed 18–19 March 1962.
- France recognized Algerian independence 3 July 1962.
- The Algerian government itself designated **5 July 1962** as Independence Day — chosen specifically to mark exactly 132 years since the French capture of Algiers on 5 July 1830, i.e., a **symbolic, narrative-driven date deliberately selected for numerical/commemorative tidiness**, not simply "the" date sovereignty transferred: [JSTOR Daily](https://daily.jstor.org/algerian-war-cause-celebre-anticolonialsm/); [Wikipedia, "Algerian War"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algerian_War).

**Flag**: **5 July 1962 is a CONVENTION** — a date chosen by the Algerian provisional government for its symbolic echo of the 1830 conquest date, not a neutral historical marker. If a dataset needs a single "Algerian independence" date, this should be noted explicitly rather than presented as if it were simply when independence "happened" (which, depending on the legal criterion used, could equally be 18–19 March or 3 July 1962).

### 4.3 First/Second Empire framing

The two-empire structure for French colonialism (1534/1605–1803/1814, then 1830–1962) is treated consistently across mainstream tertiary sources (Wikipedia, The Latin Library, and the Spanish Wikipedia, per the earlier research pass) and appears less actively contested by specialists than the analogous British split — but the internal end-date variance for the first empire (1803 vs. 1814) shows it is not perfectly settled either. **Treat the two-empire framing as a reasonably solid convention, while flagging the 1803/1814 discrepancy as an open point.**

## 5. The Golden Horde

### 5.1 Naming — "Golden Horde" is a documented later coinage; flag as a MISCONCEPTION

This is one of the strongest, best-documented findings in this research. The name "Golden Horde" was **not used by the state itself or by its contemporaries** and is a retrospective label applied roughly 60+ years after the polity's decline began.

- Don Ostrowski (Harvard historian): "Golden Horde, an anachronistic and misleading term... The term Zolotaia Orda, which has been translated as 'Golden Horde', first appears in Russian sources of the late sixteenth to early seventeenth centuries, many decades after the end of the Qipchaq Khanate" — [Ostrowski, PDF](https://donostrowski2.bitbucket.io/mm/golden2.pdf). Ostrowski argues the term likely originates from a translation/transmission error and that there is no contemporary evidence the khanate was ever called this during its existence.
- The term is specifically traced to the Russian chronicle "*Kazanskaya istoriya*" ("History of Kazan"), compiled around 1565–1566: Russian-language sources place it at 1565 ([proza.ru](https://proza.ru/2009/10/19/863)) and, more generally, "the second half of the sixteenth century" ([abimperio.org](https://www.abimperio.org/article/1047?lang=ru); [ru.wikipedia.org, "Золотая Орда"](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/%D0%97%D0%BE%D0%BB%D0%BE%D1%82%D0%B0%D1%8F_%D0%9E%D1%80%D0%B4%D0%B0)).
- Encyclopaedia Iranica confirms contemporaries used different names entirely: [Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Golden Horde"](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/golden-horde/) — Arabic sources called it the "Ulus of Batu" or "Ulus of Berke."
- A general encyclopedia entry corroborates: "Persian sources of the 13th and 14th centuries...spoke either geographically of the Dasht-i Qifchaq...or dynastically of the 'ulus (realm) of Jochi'... Not until the 16th century did Russian chroniclers begin explicitly using Golden Horde" — [War History Online encyclopedia entry](https://warhistory.org/@msw/article/golden-horde).

**Relationship to the Jochid ulus**: The actual, contemporary name of the polity was the **Ulus of Jochi** (self-described, per some sources, as "Ulug Ulus" / "Great State"), the territorial allotment granted by Genghis Khan to his son Jochi. "Golden Horde" is the retrospective Russian/Western historiographical label applied later — sometimes to the entire ulus, sometimes specifically to its western, Sarai-based branch after its consolidation of independence in the 1260s.

**Flag**: **This is a genuine, well-sourced popular MISCONCEPTION worth recording explicitly in the dataset** — the commonly used name postdates the polity by six or more decades and was never a contemporary self-designation or a name used by the polity's neighbors during its existence. Any dataset entry should note that "Golden Horde" is a conventional/retrospective label, with "Ulus of Jochi" (or "Jochid ulus") as the historically appropriate name for the polity during its actual existence.

### 5.2 Founding date — genuinely contested among specialists, not a single fact

Multiple, non-equivalent founding dates exist depending on which stage of state formation is treated as decisive:

- **1224/1225**: The ulus was allocated by Genghis Khan to Jochi. [Wikipedia, "Golden Horde"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Horde) infobox gives "Established c. 1225"; a scholarly source explicitly argues for the earlier date and against the more commonly assumed 1242/1243 founding: "The widely-held scholarly view that the Ulus of Jochi... was founded only once the Mongol army had returned from Central Europe in 1242 or 1243 is based on a misunderstanding... The Ulus was in fact formed... in 1225" — [*The Golden Horde in World History* excerpt](https://dokumen.pub/the-golden-horde-in-world-history.html).
- **1236–1240**: The conquest of the western Eurasian steppelands under Batu Khan — cited as the operative "founding" period by Britannica and Encyclopaedia Iranica: [Britannica, "Golden Horde"](https://www.britannica.com/place/Golden-Horde) gives the overall span as "1227–1502."
- **1242/1243**: Batu Khan's return from the European campaign — the traditionally cited founding moment in older/popular literature, though the scholarly source above (dokumen.pub) explicitly disputes this as based on a misunderstanding: [World History Encyclopedia, "Golden Horde"](https://www.worldhistory.org/Golden_Horde/) treats the effort as "Begun in earnest by Batu Khan in 1227 CE," with the 1242 return described as a consolidation point rather than a founding.
- **1260s**: The point of full political independence from the wider Mongol Empire, which the *Cambridge History of the Mongol Empire* treats as the proper start of "the Golden Horde" as a distinct entity, as opposed to the earlier Jochid ulus operating within the united Mongol Empire: "The Golden Horde (Jochid Ulus) rose from these territories...in the 1260s" — [Cambridge University Press, *The Cambridge History of the Mongol Empire*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-history-of-the-mongol-empire/golden-horde-c-12601502/194D20494453E0AC8373BE9ADFB8B8D6), which explicitly dates "The Golden Horde, c. 1260–1502" as a chapter title — distinguishing this phase from the earlier Jochid-ulus-under-the-united-Mongol-Empire period.

**Verdict**: This is a genuine scholarly disagreement about which stage of a gradual, multi-decade process of state formation (allocation → conquest → consolidation → independence) counts as "founding." **Do not report a single founding year. Report the range of positions (1224/25 allocation; 1236–40 conquest; 1242/43 traditional-but-disputed date; 1260s full independence per the Cambridge History), and note that the "1260s" framing corresponds specifically to when the polity became independent enough to be meaningfully called "the Golden Horde" as opposed to merely "the Jochid ulus."**

### 5.3 End date — 1502, comparatively uncontroversial

The end is dated with much more consensus than the start: Crimean Khan Mengli Giray's destruction of Sarai and defeat of Sheikh Ahmed, the last khan of the "Great Horde" (successor rump state), in 1502. Sources: [Britannica, "Golden Horde"](https://www.britannica.com/place/Golden-Horde) ("1227–1502"); Charles J. Halperin's scholarly work states, "This event in 1502 can be regarded as the real end of the Great Horde" — cited via [Academia.edu upload of Halperin's *Russia and the Golden Horde*](https://www.academia.edu/126611822/Charles_J_Halperin_Russia_and_the_Golden_Horde_The_Mongol_Impact_on_Medieval_Russian_History).

### 5.4 Recommendation for the dataset's "1240–1502" entry

The dataset's current "1240" start falls within the range of defensible positions (close to the 1236–1240 conquest-period dating used by Britannica and Iranica) but does not represent a single settled scholarly consensus — it is one of at least four legitimate candidate dates. **Recommend either (a) keeping 1240 but annotating it explicitly as one of several competing scholarly positions on founding, with the others listed, or (b) using the 1260s per the Cambridge History of the Mongol Empire if the intent is specifically "the Golden Horde as an independent polity" rather than "the Jochid ulus in general."** In either case, the entry should carry a note that "Golden Horde" is itself a retrospective 16th-century Russian coinage never used contemporaneously.

## 6. Cross-cutting question: is there a scholarly category for "empire spanning multiple world regions"?

Yes — there is a substantial body of comparative scholarship, though it explicitly represents a *departure* from an older historiography that studied Islamic, Mongol, and European maritime empires in separate silos.

- **"Universal empire"** is an established comparative term/concept applied across Assyrian, Achaemenid, Roman, Islamic, Mongol, and European contexts. Cambridge University Press published *Universal Empire: A Comparative Approach to Imperial Culture and Representation in Eurasian History* (eds. Peter Fibiger Bang and Dariusz Kołodziejczyk) — [Cambridge frontmatter PDF](https://assets.cambridge.org/97811070/22676/frontmatter/9781107022676_frontmatter.pdf); reviewed at the [Bryn Mawr Classical Review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2014/2014.08.10/), which discusses Carolingian, Byzantine, Umayyad, Ottoman, Mughal, Austrian, and Russian empires as claimants to a Roman/Achaemenid-derived concept of universal "world dominion."
- **"World-empires"** is a related, more specific term. Bang and Bayly (2011) define it as "vast empires that dominated their wider worlds and were able to absorb many of their competitors and reduce them either to taxpaying provinces or tributary client kingdoms," and explicitly single out the Mongol Empire as unique in this regard: "this discourse...was resurrected on an unprecedented scale with the advent of the Mongol Empire (13th–14th centuries). Mongol rule was unique in Eurasian history" — cited in *The Limits of Universal Rule: Eurasian Empires Compared* ([dokumen.pub](https://dokumen.pub/the-limits-of-universal-rule-eurasian-empires-compared-1108488633-9781108488631.html)).
- **Burbank and Cooper, *Empires in World History*** is a major, widely cited comparative work that explicitly places Rome, China, Byzantium, the Islamic caliphates, the Carolingians, the Mongols, and the Russian, Mughal, Ottoman, and European maritime empires within one comparative analytical frame. A review summarizes: "Burbank and Cooper outline similarities and differences among many great empires... The authors next consider empires that tried to move into Rome's place—resilient Byzantium, the fissionable Islamic caliphates... Mongols put together the largest land empire of all time... European maritime extensions were stimulated by..." — [Project MUSE review](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/713800). **This is a strong, directly citable source explicitly arguing FOR treating caliphates, the Mongol empire, and European maritime empires as comparable phenomena.**
- **The Oxford World History of Empire project** (Scheidel et al.) documents that comparative imperial history has historically been split into three largely separate scholarly traditions — "1. Roman and ancient imperialism, 2. The great Asian Empires (Ottomans, Mughals, the Chinese, Mongols, Arab Caliphate), 3. European commercial and colonial empires" — and was explicitly designed to bridge them: [Stanford, project description PDF](https://web.stanford.edu/~scheidel/OWHE.pdf). This source is useful for **both sides** of the cross-cutting question: it documents that these traditions have historically been studied separately (a case against easy comparison as the historiographical default), while itself being a large-scale project built specifically to argue they *should* be compared.
- **"New Imperial History"** is an academic paradigm explicitly comparing early modern and non-European empires together as an analytical category rather than treating "empire" as a fixed Western type: see discussion at a Novosibirsk State University conference paper ([NSU PDF](https://www.nsu.ru/n/humanities-institute/research/events/HeritageForum2021/east/collection/11_ziehaus.pdf)) and a German-language historiographical journal piece arguing "the established separation of maritime from continental empires should be abandoned in favor of new approaches that compare and entangle imperial spaces using...global history" ([vr-elibrary.de](https://www.vr-elibrary.de/doi/10.13109/gege.2015.41.4.718)).
- A narrower, more specific comparative sub-category also exists: **"maritime empires"** (Athenian, Venetian, Portuguese, Japanese, etc.), defined by dependence on naval trade routes as their main arteries of connectivity, explicitly contrasted with land/continental empires — Brill, *Empires of the Sea: Maritime Power Networks in World History* ([Brill PDF](https://brill.com/downloadpdf/display/book/9789004407671/BP000001.pdf)).

**Verdict**: Yes, there is a rich and still-developing scholarly literature on multi-region empires as a comparable category, with several competing/complementary terms ("universal empire," "world-empire," "maritime empire") and at least one major synthetic work (Burbank & Cooper) explicitly grouping Islamic caliphates, the Mongol empire, and European maritime empires together for direct comparison. At the same time, credible sources (the Oxford World History of Empire project's own framing) document that this comparative approach is a relatively recent corrective to an older historiography that studied these three traditions in isolation from one another — so the grouping should be presented as an active and still-contested area of comparative history, not settled taxonomy.
