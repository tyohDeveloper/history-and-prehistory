# Europe 10,000–2,500 BCE: Sourced Research Findings

Compiled for a sourced historical dataset. Every numeric date below is tied to a URL fetched during this research pass. Wikipedia entries used only as locators are explicitly labeled; a non-Wikipedia dating source is given alongside wherever a date is asserted. Where the literature disagrees, both positions are given rather than averaged. `n.a.` marks anything not confirmed against a fetched source.

Format per entity: **Name → Dates (method) → Significance → Sources → Flags**

## SECTION 1: MESOLITHIC EUROPE

### Maglemosian (Maglemose culture)
- **Dates:** c. 11,600–8,400 cal BP (≈ c. 9,600–6,400 cal BC), per Lundby Mose site report; a related synthesis gives "Maglemose (c. 9600–6500 BC)" and one site notes bone-point chronology "Maglemose culture (c. 11,000–8000 BP)." Northern German/Polish papers place its Preboreal beginning at c. 10,000–8,600 BP for the earliest Boreal occupations.
  - Dating method: radiocarbon (conventional and AMS), calibrated.
  - A significant internal hiatus at **c. 10,300 cal BP** (~600 years) divides an "Early Maglemose Complex" (fine-barbed bone points) from a "Late Maglemose Complex" (large-barbed bone points), per AMS dating of bone-point series.
- **Significance:** The founding "Early Mesolithic" culture of Northern Europe (Scandinavia/North Sea littoral), named for the type-site Mullerup Mose (Sjælland, Denmark); defined by microliths and organic (bone/antler) tool technology exploiting post-glacial forest/wetland resources.
- **Sources:**
  - [Early Maglemosian culture in the Preboreal landscape... Lundby Mose, Sjælland (ScienceDirect/CORE)](https://core.ac.uk/download/pdf/82106402.pdf)
  - [New radiocarbon dates for ornamented Mesolithic objects from northwest Poland (Cambridge/Antiquity)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/8A12B95243E8043511BC7C6E63D17B22/S0003598X22001429a.pdf/new_radiocarbon_dates_for_ornamented_mesolithic_objects_from_northwest_poland_chronology_and_regional_connections_in_the_western_baltic_region.pdf)
  - [An integrated analysis of Maglemose bone points reframes the Early Mesolithic of Southern Scandinavia (Nature Scientific Reports, 2020)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-020-74258-8)
  - [Grave of the Maglemosian culture at Bołków, Western Pomerania (rcin.org.pl, Polish excavation report)](http://rcin.org.pl/Content/235434/270374.pdf)
- **Flags:** None specific; note the c. 10,300 cal BP hiatus is a 2020 refinement (Nature Scientific Reports) that subdivides what older literature treated as a single uniform culture — worth flagging as a **moderate literature refinement (2020)**, not a full ⚑ overturning.

### Kongemose culture
- **Dates:** c. 8,300/7,700–6,600/6,000 cal BP depending on source; in calendar terms roughly **6,600–5,200/5,400 BC**. One Danish/Swedish excavation-report source: "early Kongemose (ca. 7,500–7,000 BP)" and "late Kongemose (Vedbæk phase, ca. 7,000–6,500 BP)." Wikipedia (locator only) gives "ca. 6000 BC–5200 BC," consistent with the calibrated ranges from radiocarbon papers.
  - Dating method: radiocarbon (conventional, several sites), pollen zone correlation (Pollen Zone VI).
- **Significance:** Middle Mesolithic culture of southern Scandinavia, transitional between Maglemosian and Ertebølle; marked by broad trapeziform microliths and increasing coastal/aquatic adaptation.
- **Sources:**
  - [Ringsjöholm. A Boreal–Early Atlantic Settlement in Central Scania, Sweden (Lund University journals)](https://journals.lub.lu.se/lar/article/download/21687/19526)
  - [New radiocarbon dates for ornamented Mesolithic objects from NW Poland (Cambridge/Antiquity, gives Kongemose c. 6500–5400 BC)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/8A12B95243E8043511BC7C6E63D17B22/S0003598X22001429a.pdf/new_radiocarbon_dates_for_ornamented_mesolithic_objects_from_northwest_poland_chronology_and_regional_connections_in_the_western_baltic_region.pdf)
  - [Economic Prehistory in Southern Scandinavia (The British Academy PDF, radiocarbon dates 8300-8100 BP etc.)](https://www.thebritishacademy.ac.uk/documents/3848/99p125.pdf)
  - [Copenhagen radiocarbon dates X — Henrik Tauber (Radiocarbon journal PDF)](https://journals.uair.arizona.edu/index.php/radiocarbon/article/download/337/342)
  - Locator only (not a dating authority): [Kongemose culture — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kongemose_culture)
- **Flags:** Note the range genuinely varies by ~500 years between sources depending on whether uncalibrated BP or calibrated BC figures are quoted carelessly — authoring pass must keep cal BP vs cal BC distinct per source.

### Ertebølle culture
- **Dates:** c. 5,400–4,000 BC (calibrated), per Oxford Academic edited volume ("Ertebølle period, *c.* 5400–4000 bc"); consistent with a Swedish source: "From at least 5500 BC until 4000 BC the Ertebølle culture existed in the whole of Southern Scandinavia." Individual site spans: Björnsholm midden 5050–4050 BC; late Ertebølle phase c. 4600–4000 BC.
  - Dating method: radiocarbon-calibrated (cal BC).
- **Significance:** Final Mesolithic culture of Denmark/southern Sweden/northern Germany; a "complex hunter-gatherer" society with shell middens, the world's earliest known hunter-gatherer pottery in the region, and prolonged coexistence with/resistance to incoming farming (LBK) populations to its south for centuries — a key case study in the delayed "Neolithic frontier."
- **Sources:**
  - [Places of Settlement in Southern Scandinavia (Oxford Academic edited volume)](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/35019/chapter/298822185)
  - [HumaNetten Nr 30 (Linnaeus University, open access journal)](https://open.lnu.se/index.php/hn/article/download/297/283/1154)
  - [Inland Ertebølle Culture: freshwater reservoir effect in radiocarbon dates (Internet Archaeology)](https://intarch.ac.uk/journal/issue37/philippsen_index.html)
- **Flags:** None additional beyond what's already in the dataset's LBK entry (5500–4900 BCE) — Ertebølle's persistence to c. 4000 BC alongside/after LBK's end (4900 BCE) is itself the well-documented "Mesolithic-Neolithic frontier" phenomenon in this region; not a misconception but worth noting explicitly for authoring.

### Azilian
- **Dates:** French Early Azilian radiocarbon series clusters c. 14,900–12,200 cal BP (≈ **12,900–10,200 cal BC**), per a table of dated French Azilian sites (Pont d'Ambon, Bois-Ragot, Abri Murat, Le Closeau). A French synthesis places Azilian societies as "already in existence in southern France for several centuries" by "14 to 12.5 ka cal BP" (≈ 12,000–10,500 cal BC), overlapping in time with the Late Epigravettian.
  - Dating method: radiocarbon-calibrated (cal BP → cal BC conversion given directly in the source tables).
- **Significance:** Terminal Upper Palaeolithic/earliest post-glacial culture of southwestern France and northern Spain, preceding the local Mesolithic (Sauveterrian); marked by flat harpoons and painted pebbles; its beginning predates the dataset's 10,000 BCE Mesolithic threshold, so it bridges Palaeolithic and Mesolithic Europe.
- **Sources:**
  - [Divergence in the evolution of Paleolithic symbolic and Mesolithic technological traditions (PMC, includes radiocarbon table for French Early Azilian)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5336238/)
  - [Prehistoire.org conference volume S03 (radiocarbon dates table for Pont d'Ambon etc.)](https://www.prehistoire.org/offres/doc_inline_src/515/S03_complet_red.pdf)
- **Flags:** Note the Azilian's *end* (transition into Sauveterrian) largely precedes 10,000 BCE and thus falls mostly outside this dataset's nominal Mesolithic starting point of 10,000 BCE — flag for authoring: Azilian should probably be entered with an end date near the threshold, not treated as a full Mesolithic-period culture.

### Sauveterrian
- **Dates:** In NE Italy (Romagnano rock shelter, Bayesian-modelled): Early Sauveterrian 9,740–8,490 cal BCE (95%); Middle Sauveterrian 8,300–7,680 cal BCE; Recent Sauveterrian 7,732–7,581 cal BCE; transition to Castelnovian 7,643–6,771 cal BCE. French tradition (Wikipedia French, locator only) gives "globally between 9,500 and 7,500 years BP" (≈ 7,550–5,550 cal BC), which is a different — later — window than the Italian AMS Bayesian series; treat as region-specific chronologies, not a single pan-European span.
  - Dating method: radiocarbon (AMS), Bayesian modelling (Italian series); conventional radiocarbon (older French/Iberian series).
- **Significance:** Early Mesolithic microlithic industry (narrow-backed points, triangles) of France, Italy, and Iberia; named after the type-site Sauveterre-la-Lémance; regionally the counterpart to the Maglemosian further north.
- **Sources:**
  - [Table 3, Results of Romagnano rock shelter, Bayesian-modelled radiocarbon distributions (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12440202/table/pone.0331392.t003/)
  - [Exploring the "Microburin Blow": Mondeval de Sora new radiocarbon dates (University of Ferrara repository)](https://sfera.unife.it/retrieve/cf8672dd-ffec-4db8-8e72-1f2d0bc9581a/Fasser%20et%20al%202025_Exploring%20the%20Microburin%20Blow.pdf)
  - Locator only: [Sauveterrien — Wikipédia](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sauveterrien)
- **Flags:** ⚑ Regional chronology mismatch — Italian AMS/Bayesian dates (c. 9,700–6,800 cal BCE) and older French typological dating (c. 7,550–5,550 cal BC per non-authoritative locator) diverge by roughly a millennium; authoring pass should not merge these into one date range without specifying region.

### Tardenoisian
- **Dates:** Widely cited as spanning **c. 9,000–4,000 BC** in northern France/Belgium (English Wikipedia, locator only). A French regional excavation report (Aisne) gives directly radiocarbon-dated Tardenoisian layers at **6,110 BC** (charcoal) with overlying hazelnut shells at **5,310 BC**. A French encyclopedic source (Universalis) states the Tardenoisien runs "de 7000 à 4000 avant J.-C." A Paris Basin excavation report gives a direct radiocarbon date of **10,510 ± 110 BP** (uncalibrated) for the "avènement du Tardenoisien" (its emergence) — this uncalibrated date is notably older than the other ranges and has not been independently calibrated in the fetched source.
  - Dating method: radiocarbon (conventional, various French labs — Gif, etc.); some figures uncalibrated BP, others calendar BC — sources are inconsistent about which.
- **Significance:** Mesolithic microlithic (trapeze-armature) culture of the Paris Basin/northern France and Belgium; culturally continuous into the local Neolithic transition, alongside the Sauveterrian to its south.
- **Sources:**
  - [Les civilisations "mésolithiques" du Tardenois (histoireaisne.fr, regional excavation report with radiocarbon dates)](https://www.histoireaisne.fr/memoires_numerises/chapitres/tome_35/Tome_035_page_017.pdf)
  - [Evolution des cultures épipaléolithiques et mésolithiques dans le Bassin parisien (Persée, includes 10510±110 BP date)](https://www.persee.fr/doc/pica_0752-5656_1990_num_3_1_1609)
  - [TARDENOISIEN — Encyclopédie Universalis](https://www.universalis.fr/encyclopedie/tardenoisien/)
  - Locator only: [Tardenoisian — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tardenoisian)
- **Flags:** ⚑ Source inconsistency — the fetched sources disagree by up to 3,000 years on start date (9000 BC vs. 7000 BC vs. a direct uncalibrated 10,510 BP date at one site), and it is unclear how much of this is regional variability vs. calibration-vs-uncalibrated confusion in the secondary literature. Recommend authoring pass mark Tardenoisian dates as `n.a. — contested/inconsistently calibrated across sources` unless a modern Bayesian re-dating paper can be found.

### Star Carr (Britain)
- **Dates:** Occupation begins **9,335–9,275 cal BC**, lasting roughly 800 years to **8,525–8,440 cal BC**, per Wikipedia's summary of current radiocarbon data (locator; cross-checked against primary sources below). Peer-reviewed primary sources converge closely: PLOS ONE (2024) states occupation "c. 9300–8500 cal BC"; central structure "in use between 9300–9200 cal BC"; western structure built "8945–8760 cal BC (95% probability)." Historic England heritage record separately gives "Radiocarbon c8770-8350BC" for the same site's hut/floor features (a narrower sub-range within the larger occupation span). Older analyses (starcarr.com background PDF) cite raw uncalibrated dates of "11,040 to 10,450 BP."
  - Dating method: radiocarbon (AMS), Bayesian chronological modelling (Bayliss et al. 2018 framework as used in the PLOS ONE paper).
- **Significance:** The best-preserved and most intensively studied Early Mesolithic site in Britain (North Yorkshire); yielded the earliest known evidence of built (post-hole) structures in Britain and important organic (antler headdress, bone/antler tool) assemblages from wetland preservation.
- **Sources:**
  - [Spatial organisation within the earliest evidence of post-built structures... Star Carr (PLOS ONE, 2024)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0306908)
  - [The interpretation of Mesolithic structures (University of Reading repository, cites Bayliss et al. 2018 Bayesian dates)](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/77894/1/The%20Interpretation%20of%20Mesolithic%20Structures.pdf)
  - [Historic England Research Records — Star Carr](https://www.heritagegateway.org.uk/Gateway/Results_Single.aspx?uid=80206&resourceID=19191)
  - [New Radiocarbon Accelerator Dates on Artefacts from Star Carr (Archaeometry, via Wiley)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1475-4754.2006.00250.x)
  - Locator only: [Star Carr — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_Carr)
- **Flags:** None major; dating is well-converged across multiple peer-reviewed AMS studies (2018, 2024, 2025 excavation-project publications), though popular sources still occasionally cite outdated uncalibrated BP figures from the original 1950s Clark excavation.

### Lepenski Vir (Danube Gorges)
- **Dates:** Per the definitive Bayesian AMS chronology (Nature Scientific Reports, Borić et al. 2018, 111 radiocarbon measurements): site occupation begins **9,900–9,280 cal BC** (95% probability; likely 9,660–9,370 cal BC at 68%); the Early-Middle Mesolithic/"Proto-Lepenski Vir" phase ends **7,480–6,860 cal BC**; after a **hiatus of at least 700 years**, the iconic Mesolithic–Neolithic Transition phase (trapezoidal buildings, boulder art) begins **6,170–6,070 cal BC** and ends **6,010–5,940 cal BC**; the Early Neolithic Phase III runs from **5,990–5,900 cal BC** to **5,900–5,660 cal BC** (all 95% probability ranges). An earlier academic source (Cardiff repository, Borić & Dimitrijević) similarly finds Mesolithic duration "from around 9400 to around 7500 cal BC."
  - Dating method: AMS radiocarbon on charcoal, animal bone, and (reservoir-corrected) human bone; formal Bayesian statistical modelling (OxCal 4.3.2, Reimer et al. calibration curve).
- **Significance:** The type-site for the "Lepenski Vir culture" (Iron Gates Mesolithic) of the Danube Gorges; famous for trapezoidal buildings and carved boulder sculptures, and a key site for studying the local Mesolithic–Neolithic transition and its associated ancient-DNA record of hunter-gatherer/farmer interaction (see Section 3).
- **Sources:**
  - [High-Resolution AMS Dating of Architecture, Boulder Artworks and the Transition to Farming at Lepenski Vir (Nature Scientific Reports, 2018)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-31884-7)
  - [Apsolutna hronologija i stratigrafija Lepenskog Vira (Cardiff University repository, Borić & Dimitrijević)](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/39268/)
  - [Lepenski Vir Chronology and Stratigraphy Revisited (Academia.edu, covers Danube Gorges c. 10,000–5,500 cal BC)](https://www.academia.edu/41272165/Lepenski_Vir_Chronology_and_Stratigraphy_Revisited)
- **Flags:** ⚑ **LITERATURE MOVED (2018)** — Borić et al.'s Bayesian AMS re-dating substantially revised the older stratigraphic chronology proposed by original excavator Dragoslav Srejović (1969/1972), which had assumed a more continuous, shorter sequence; the 2018 study establishes the ~700-year occupation hiatus and much more tightly bounded phase transitions. Any pre-2018 date for Lepenski Vir's phases should be treated as superseded.

### Doggerland and its inundation
- **Dates:** A brand-new (2026) sediment-core chronology (Bensharada et al. 2026, *Humans* 6(1):5) shows the inundation "began gradually around 10,000 years ago," with the key freshwater-to-marine transitional layer (Area B) radiocarbon-dated to **10,243–10,199 years ago**. The large multi-year "Europe's Lost Frontiers" project (Gaffney et al.) dates final inundation of the studied Outer Dowsing Deep cores to **between 8,200 and 7,200 BP**; other Lost Frontiers data points include a sea-level index point at 10,280±77.5 cal BP and a "final submergence" estimate of c. 7,000 BP for the emergent landscape generally. A separate Science (2020) news piece states Doggerland "disappeared" when sea levels rose "about 8,500 years ago," with a devastating tsunami (Storegga) "around 6150 BCE."
  - Dating method: radiocarbon (organic sediment layers, shells), optically stimulated luminescence (OSL) dating of sediments — both used in combination in the "Europe's Lost Frontiers" chronologies.
- **Significance:** Doggerland was the now-submerged land bridge/archipelago connecting Britain to continental Europe through the Mesolithic; its drowning is a defining environmental event of Mesolithic Europe, displacing and fragmenting hunter-gatherer populations and eventually isolating Britain.
- **Sources:**
  - [New timeline for the drowning of Doggerland — The Past (2026, reporting Bensharada et al. 2026)](https://the-past.com/news/new-timeline-for-the-drowning-of-doggerland/)
  - [Europe's Lost Frontiers, Volume 1: Context and Methodology (VLIZ open-access PDF)](https://www.vliz.be/imisdocs/publications/407325.pdf)
  - [Europe's lost frontier (Science, 2020 news feature)](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.367.6477.499)
- **Flags:** ⚑ **LITERATURE MOVED (2026)** — the Bensharada et al. 2026 sediment-core study is the most precise chronology yet published for the *start* of inundation (c. 10,243–10,199 years ago), refining/confirming the older, vaguer "10,000–7,000 years ago" window. Separately, note ongoing methodological uncertainty: the University of Groningen's active "FLOOD" project (2025–2027) states explicitly that "the accuracy of several radiocarbon dates from Doggerland has been questioned" and is actively working to resolve a "Doggerland radiocarbon conundrum" — flag this as an **open, unresolved dating problem**, not a settled chronology. **Common misconception to flag:** popular accounts often describe Doggerland's loss as a single catastrophic tsunami event (Storegga); the peer-reviewed and project literature is consistent that it was a **gradual, multi-stage inundation over millennia**, with Storegga (c. 8,150 cal BP) as one damaging but non-final episode superimposed on that longer process.

### Storegga tsunami
- **Dates:** Best current calibrated age: **7,300 ± 20 ¹⁴C years BP → 8,080–8,180 cal yr BP** (mean 8,140 ± 55 cal yr BP), from AMS radiocarbon dating of green moss fragments killed by the tsunami, per a 2024 Nature Communications re-analysis using IntCal20/Marine20 calibration curves. This refines/confirms an earlier widely-cited figure of "c. 8,150 cal BP" (Bondevik et al.). An older (2008) synthesis by Weninger et al. gives a slightly different but compatible figure: "7,300 ± 30 ¹⁴C-BP (95%), or 8,100 ± 100 cal BP." The event is tied to the coldest decades of the well-known **8.2 ka climate event**.
  - Dating method: AMS radiocarbon dating of green moss macrofossils in tsunami deposits, calibrated with IntCal20/Marine20 (2024 reanalysis) or IntCal09/Marine09 (older studies); cross-correlated against Greenland ice-core (GRIP) oxygen-isotope and methane records.
- **Significance:** One of the largest known Holocene tsunamis, generated by the Storegga submarine slide off Norway; struck the North Sea coastlines including remaining Doggerland, contributing to (but not solely causing) the final loss of Doggerland's landscape.
- **Sources:**
  - [Contamination of 8.2 ka cold climate records by the Storegga tsunami in the Nordic Seas (Nature Communications, 2024)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-47347-9) **[URL corrected during authoring: the research pass gave s41467-024-51335-4, which is the Fort & Perez-Losada interbreeding paper cited further down, not this one]**
  - [Contamination of 8.2 ka cold climate records by the Storegga tsunami (IFREMER archive PDF, methodological detail on recalibration)](https://archimer.ifremer.fr/doc/00854/96596/109828.pdf)
  - [The catastrophic final flooding of Doggerland by the Storegga Slide Tsunami (Weninger et al., 2008 PDF)](https://profmarkcollard.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/09/Weninger_et_al_2008.pdf)
- **Flags:** None beyond the misconception flagged above under Doggerland (Storegga was one episode, not the whole story of Doggerland's loss).

## SECTION 2: ARRIVAL OF FARMING IN EUROPE

### Franchthi Cave
- **Dates:** Continuous occupation sequence spans roughly **38,000–6,000 cal BP** (Upper Palaeolithic through end of Neolithic), per PMC paleobotanical study. The key finding for farming's arrival: newly-obtained AMS dates on domesticated cereal seeds show cereal cultivation began in the **first half of the 7th millennium cal BC** (i.e., before 6,500 cal BC) — earlier than previously thought, and earlier than in northern Greece or Bulgaria (Antiquity/Cambridge paper, Perlès et al.). A related paleobotanical PMC source gives "first introduction of domesticated cereal crops to FC is dated to ~8,700 cal BP" (≈ 6,750 cal BC), broadly consistent.
  - Dating method: radiocarbon (conventional and AMS on charred seeds).
- **Significance:** A uniquely long, continuously-excavated sequence in the Argolid (Greece) spanning Palaeolithic through Neolithic; the new early cereal dates support a model of **multiple, possibly maritime/island-hopping routes** for farming's arrival in the Aegean, rather than a single inland diffusion front.
- **Sources:**
  - [Early seventh-millennium AMS dates from domestic seeds in the Initial Neolithic at Franchthi Cave (Antiquity/Cambridge Core, 2013)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/early-seventhmillennium-ams-dates-from-domestic-seeds-in-the-initial-neolithic-at-franchthi-cave-argolid-greece/73AF10B4F6C3422ABA362FDBAC4D71FB)
  - [The impact of environmental change on Palaeolithic and Mesolithic plant use, Franchthi Cave (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6245798/)
  - [Preceramic, Aceramic or Early Ceramic? The radiocarbon dated beginnings of Neolithic Franchthi/Sesklo (ScisSpace-hosted PDF)](https://scispace.com/pdf/preceramic-aceramic-or-early-ceramic-the-radiocarbon-dated-tqz02xg7wf.pdf)
- **Flags:** ⚑ **LITERATURE MOVED** — the 2013 Antiquity paper explicitly states its new dates "cast doubt" on the prior model of farming's spread, pushing Franchthi's cereal cultivation earlier than previously dated and ahead of northern Greece/Bulgaria. **Common misconception to flag:** early farming in Greece is often presented as a simple single-direction spread from Anatolia into Thessaly (Sesklo) and then outward; Franchthi's early dates instead point to **parallel/maritime routes reaching southern Greece independently and possibly earlier**.

### Sesklo and Dimini (Greece)
- **Dates:** Sesklo overall span "c. 7,510 BC — c. 4,400 BC" including a Proto-/Pre-Sesklo phase "c. 7,510 BC – c. 6,190 BC" (Wikipedia, **locator only**). Cross-checked against peer-reviewed radiocarbon compilations: the "pre-pottery" (aceramic) Early Neolithic phase at Greek sites (Sesklo included) has dates as early as **8,130 ± 100 BP** to **7,755 ± 97 BP** (uncalibrated), with pottery-bearing Early Neolithic I dated **7,740 ± 140 BP to 7,320 ± 50 BP**; a 2005/synthesis review (in a scispace-hosted PDF) concludes farming communities appear in Thessaly "around 6,700–6,500 cal BC, and not at 7,000 cal BC" as some older literature claimed. Sesklo culture radiocarbon on charcoal separately indicates "a starting date around 6,500 cal BC."
  - Dating method: radiocarbon (conventional, charcoal); calibration explicitly discussed and revised in the 2005-era synthesis source.
- **Significance:** Sesklo (and neighboring Dimini, its later Late/Final Neolithic successor) are the type-sites for the Greek Neolithic sequence in Thessaly, among the earliest farming settlements in Europe.
- **Sources:**
  - [Preceramic, Aceramic or Early Ceramic? radiocarbon-dated beginnings of the Greek Neolithic (scispace-hosted PDF)](https://scispace.com/pdf/preceramic-aceramic-or-early-ceramic-the-radiocarbon-tqz02xg7wf.pdf)
  - [Radiocarbon dates for Sesklo/Franchthi/Knossos/Argissa (Sidestone Press open-access PDF)](https://www.sidestone.com/openaccess/9789081810975.pdf)
  - [Greek Neolithic Review (Scribd-hosted academic document with radiocarbon date compilation)](https://www.scribd.com/document/85626647/Greek-Neolithic-Review)
  - Locator only: [Sesklo — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sesklo)
- **Flags:** ⚑ **LITERATURE MOVED** — modern recalibration explicitly revises the Neolithic-start date for southern Thessaly downward, from an older "7,000 calBC" figure to "6,700–6,500 calBC," per the fetched synthesis. Authors should not use the Wikipedia figure of "c. 7,510 BC" for Sesklo's Proto/Pre-Sesklo start without a non-Wikipedia radiocarbon source — the fetched peer-reviewed synthesis suggests this is too early relative to the reassessed regional Neolithic chronology.

### Starčevo–Körös–Criș
- **Dates:** For Central Balkans (Serbia), the Starčevo culture start is "conventionally dated to **6,200 cal BC**," ending c. **5,300 cal BC** (coinciding with the rise of Vinča), per a PLOS ONE demographic-modelling paper. Radiocarbon dates for settlements "across the entire SKC geographical range span the period from c. **6,200–5,500 cal BC**," per a Durham University repository thesis. A Bayesian-modelled Hungarian site (Alsónyék) gives a much narrower/later window: Starčevo activity "probably began in 5,775–5,740 cal BC" and "probably ended in 5,560–5,525 cal BC" (68% probability) — a markedly later and shorter estimate for that specific site than the broader culture-wide range.
  - Dating method: radiocarbon-calibrated (cal BC); Bayesian modelling for the Alsónyék site-specific dates.
- **Significance:** Starčevo–Körös–Criș is the foundational Early Neolithic culture complex of the Balkans/Carpathian Basin (Serbia, Hungary, Romania), representing farming's initial inland ("Danubian route") push north of Greece; it is ancestral to the LBK and to Vinča.
- **Sources:**
  - [Demography of the Early Neolithic Population in Central Balkans (PLOS ONE, summed radiocarbon probability study)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0160832)
  - [Production and function of Neolithic black-painted pottery from Schela Cladovei (Durham repository, gives SKC-wide span 6200-5500 cal BC)](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1319467)
  - [The early days of Neolithic Alsónyék: The Starčevo occupation (Oxford Archaeology publication page)](https://www.arch.ox.ac.uk/publication/675885/scopus)
- **Flags:** ⚑ Note the internal disagreement — culture-wide summary dates (6,200–5,300 cal BC) versus a specific Bayesian-modelled site (Alsónyék, 5,775–5,525 cal BC) diverge by several centuries; this reflects genuine regional/local variation in when Starčevo-culture farming arrived, not a simple error — authoring should specify region/site when citing a narrow range.

### Cardial / Impressed Ware (Mediterranean route)
- **Dates:** A rigorous PNAS radiocarbon study (Zilhão, 2001) using only short-lived, diagnostic samples finds first appearance of the full "Neolithic package" (domesticates, pottery, polished axes, villages) is **statistically indistinguishable from central Italy to Portugal**, clustering around **5,400 cal BC**; regionally the paper cites Liguria ≈5,800 cal BC, Valencia ≈5,600 cal BC, and central Portugal ≈5,400 cal BC. A separate Iberia-focused paper (Journal of Mediterranean Archaeology, 2015) gives the appearance of the "Neolithic package" in Iberia at **ca. 5,500 cal BC**. These support a "maritime pioneer colonization" model — implying the ~2,000 km spread from Italy to Portugal took only about six generations, i.e., a rate of roughly 5 km/year.
  - Dating method: radiocarbon (short-lived, diagnostic single-entity samples only — an explicit methodological safeguard in the PNAS paper against "old wood" bias).
- **Significance:** Cardial/Impressed Ware marks the "Mediterranean route" of Neolithic spread — separate from (and faster than) the inland Danubian/LBK route — reaching the western Mediterranean via island-hopping/coastal seafaring.
- **Sources:**
  - [Radiocarbon evidence for maritime pioneer colonization at the origins of farming in west Mediterranean Europe (PNAS, 2001)](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.241522898)
  - [Radiocarbon Dating the Beginning of the Neolithic in Iberia: New Results, New Problems (Journal of Mediterranean Archaeology, 2015)](https://journal.equinoxpub.com/JMA/article/view/952)
- **Flags:** None additional; this is a well-established, methodologically strong finding (explicitly designed to counter "old wood" radiocarbon bias in earlier literature) — worth flagging as methodologically authoritative.

### Danubian vs Mediterranean route debate
- **Dates/framing:** Both routes are well attested from c. 9,000–4,000 BCE (broad framing from a 2024 Nature Communications paper), with the inland/Danubian route associated with LBK pottery and averaging ~50 km/generation of movement, and the Mediterranean/coastal route associated with Impressa/Cardial pottery and averaging ~70 km/generation (faster). A hybrid model (Zilhão, cited in a University of Chicago Current Anthropology piece) proposes the spread began "around 6,800–6,400 uncal BP" along both routes simultaneously, with a resulting **gap of at least 1,000 radiocarbon years** between the Early Neolithic in the Balkans/northern Greece and that of the western Mediterranean.
  - Dating method: radiocarbon-calibrated dates compiled across many sites; spatial/demic-diffusion simulation modelling.
- **Significance:** This is the central organizing debate for how farming physically reached most of Europe — not one but (at least) two distinct diffusion processes with different mechanisms (demic diffusion/colonization along the Mediterranean vs. more mixed demic/cultural diffusion along the Danubian route), later reinforced by ancient-DNA evidence of differing admixture patterns between routes.
- **Sources:**
  - [Interbreeding between farmers and hunter-gatherers along the Mediterranean and Danubian routes (Nature Communications, 2024)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-51335-4)
  - [A Regional Biological Approach to the Spread of Farming in Europe (Current Anthropology, University of Chicago, 2004 — discusses Zilhão's hybrid two-pulse model)](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/422085)
  - [Palaeogenetic evidence supports a dual model of Neolithic spread (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2706191/)
- **Flags:** **Common misconception to flag:** popular narratives often imply a single, uniform "wave of advance" of farming across Europe. The peer-reviewed literature instead consistently documents (a) two geographically and materially distinct routes, (b) different spread rates between them, and (c) a genuinely different underlying process (demic diffusion vs. more cultural-diffusion-influenced spread) — not a single mechanism.

### Vinča culture
- **Dates:** Multiple independent Bayesian/AMS radiocarbon studies converge closely: **Vinča A** (earliest) **5,400/5,300–5,200 cal BC**; **Vinča B** 5,200–5,000/4,950 cal BC; **Vinča C** 5,000/4,950–4,850/4,800 cal BC; **Vinča D** (latest) 4,850/4,800–4,650/4,600 (or 4,600/4,550) cal BC — per a University of Lancashire repository paper citing Borić 2009/2015, Tasić et al. 2016, and Whittle et al. 2016, consistent with the Wikipedia summary table (used here only as a cross-check, not authority) and with an independent Bayesian dating of the Vinča-Belo Brdo type-site itself, which found occupation there began **5,205–5,095 cal BC** (95% probability; likely 5,170–5,110 cal BC at 68%).
  - Dating method: AMS radiocarbon, Bayesian chronological modelling (multiple independent studies: 76 dates in 1996, 155 dates in 2009, 600 dates in 2016 — an unusually well-replicated chronology).
- **Significance:** The major Late Neolithic culture of the central Balkans (Serbia), known for large tell settlements, distinctive figurines, and among the earliest copper metallurgy in Europe; directly follows and partly overlaps Starčevo.
- **Sources:**
  - [A Vinča Culture Enclosed and Fortified Settlement in the Balkans (University of Lancashire repository)](https://knowledge.lancashire.ac.uk/id/eprint/21446/13/21446%20vinca%20paper.pdf)
  - [Interwoven strategies: Vinča (University of Stirling repository, Bayesian dating of Vinča-Belo Brdo)](https://dspace.stir.ac.uk/bitstream/1893/23547/1/RESUBMITTED-Interwoven-strategies-Vinca-v7.pdf)
  - [Dating the Late Neolithic of the central Morava region: Stublina/Supska, Serbia (Cambridge/Radiocarbon journal)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/0B9570D263973110BF61FD53724E00BB/S0033822225101136a.pdf/div-class-title-dating-the-late-neolithic-of-the-central-morava-region-the-bayesian-chronological-modeling-of-archival-samples-from-the-site-of-stublina-supska-serbia-div.pdf)
- **Flags:** Note this is a rare case of strong scholarly **convergence** across three independent large radiocarbon datasets (1996/76 dates; 2009/155 dates; 2016/600 dates) all landing on essentially the same 5,400/5,300–4,500 cal BC span — worth flagging as high-confidence, well-replicated dating, in contrast to more contested cultures above.

### Cucuteni–Trypillia (including its mega-sites)
- **Dates:** Overall culture span given inconsistently across sources: c. **5,500–2,750 BC** (Wikipedia summary of settlements, locator only) vs. a peer-reviewed range of **Precucuteni beginning c. 5,050–4,600 cal BCE** and the culture continuing to **c. 3,000 BCE** in some regional/late phases (Sidestone Press open-access academic volume). The **mega-sites specifically** are dated to the "middle phases" (Tryp. B1/B2–C1), **c. 4,300/4,100–3,650 BCE**, per a peer-reviewed subsistence-regime study. Individual mega-sites: **Nebelivka** — occupation begins 3,980–3,820 cal BCE (95%), ends 3,870–3,750 cal BCE (95%), Bayesian-modelled on ~80–82 AMS dates (Springer journal); **Maidanetske** — total duration ~350 years, **3,990–3,640 cal BCE** (Cambridge Archaeological Journal).
  - Dating method: AMS radiocarbon on human/animal bone and botanical macroremains; Bayesian chronological modelling for the mega-sites specifically (Nebelivka, Maidanetske).
- **Significance:** Cucuteni–Trypillia is the major Chalcolithic farming culture of Romania/Moldova/Ukraine; its "mega-sites" (up to 320 ha, with population estimates as high as 15,000–17,500 at Nebelivka/Maidanetske) are among the largest known prehistoric settlements in the world and a major case study in prehistoric urbanism without centralized hierarchy.
- **Sources:**
  - [A complex subsistence regime revealed for Cucuteni–Trypillia sites (ekmair.ukma.edu.ua institutional repository / d-nb.info mirror)](https://ekmair.ukma.edu.ua/server/api/core/bitstreams/1ef22ee0-a636-4443-a6e8-038d70b3070b/content)
  - [What was the ecological impact of a Trypillia megasite occupation? Nebelivka (Vegetation History and Archaeobotany, Springer)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00334-019-00730-9)
  - [Trypillia Megasites in Context: Independent Urban Development in Chalcolithic Eastern Europe (Cambridge Archaeological Journal)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/cambridge-archaeological-journal/article/trypillia-megasites-in-context-independent-urban-development-in-chalcolithic-eastern-europe/C33D85AF4EE4BA2D61AAB77D3E399E4D)
  - [Scales of Transformation in Prehistoric and Archaic Societies I (Sidestone Press open-access, full phase chronology)](https://www.sidestone.com/openaccess/9789088908484.pdf)
  - [Genomes from Verteba cave suggest diversity within the Trypillians in Ukraine (Nature Scientific Reports, 2022)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-11117-8)
- **Flags:** ⚑ Note wide variability in mega-site duration estimates even within peer-reviewed literature: Nebelivka's occupation length is given as "not more than 2 centuries" / "c. 3,950–3,750 cal BC" in one Springer paper, but other researchers (cited within that same paper) "argue for at least 200 years" — treat exact mega-site duration as still actively debated. Also note a 2026 popular-science report (Popular Mechanics, citing a PLOS ONE paper) of a newly excavated Cucuteni-Trypillia megastructure at Stăuceni-'Holm', Romania, dated to **c. 4,000 BCE**, described as "one of the earliest examples" of such structures — a genuinely recent (2026) addition to the megastructure corpus.

### Varna necropolis (the gold)
- **Dates:** The most rigorous, peer-reviewed AMS Bayesian chronology (Higham, Chapman et al., published in *Radiocarbon* journal, Cambridge Core) models cemetery activity starting **4,596–4,516 cal BC** and ending **4,427–4,341 cal BC** (95.4% probability), for an overall span of roughly **120–260 years**. This is *c.* 200 years younger than the "traditional" (pre-AMS) chronology, which had placed the cemetery around 4,600–4,200 cal BC based on older, less rigorous datasets — per an Academia.edu-hosted paper by Krauß, Zäuner & Pernicka discussing the Higham/Chapman revision directly ("the cemetery ends around 4400 cal BC, which represents a date some 200 years older than the traditional chronology"). Popular/museum-adjacent sources (Smithsonian Magazine) give a rounder "4,600 to 4,200 BC" figure consistent with the older chronology.
  - Dating method: AMS radiocarbon, Bayesian modelling (published in the peer-reviewed journal *Radiocarbon*).
- **Significance:** Varna I is the site of the world's oldest known gold artifacts and among the earliest evidence for pronounced social/wealth hierarchy in prehistoric Europe (the "Varna Man" burial with concentrated gold goods); central to debates about the emergence of social stratification in the Chalcolithic Balkans.
- **Sources:**
  - [AMS Dating of the Late Copper Age Varna Cemetery, Bulgaria (Radiocarbon journal, Cambridge Core)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/radiocarbon/article/abs/ams-dating-of-the-late-copper-age-varna-cemetery-bulgaria/706A7F638D010BB6C943FBDFC1062749)
  - [R. Krauß/S. Zäuner/E. Pernicka, Statistical and Anthropological Analysis of the Varna Necropolis (Academia.edu, discusses the AMS revision explicitly)](https://www.academia.edu/13437312/R_Krau%C3%9F_S_Z%C3%A4uner_E_Pernicka_Statistical_and_Anthropological_Analysis_of_the_Varna_Necropolis)
  - [Mystery of the Varna Gold (Smithsonian Magazine)](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/travel/varna-bulgaria-gold-graves-social-hierarchy-prehistoric-archaelogy-smithsonian-journeys-travel-quarterly-180958733/)
- **Flags:** ⚑ **LITERATURE MOVED (2006/2018 AMS re-dating)** — the peer-reviewed AMS/Bayesian chronology (dated cemetery use to c. 4,596–4,341 cal BC) is roughly **200 years younger** than the older, widely-repeated "4,600–4,200 BC" figure still found in many popular and museum-adjacent sources. Note the already-in-dataset value should be checked against the peer-reviewed AMS range (4,596–4,341 cal BC, 95.4%) rather than the older traditional range.

### Funnelbeaker (TRB)
- **Dates:** Multiple regional radiocarbon studies give differing sub-regional windows: overall culture "c. 4,300 BC – c. 2,800 BC" across north-central Europe (a WordPress-hosted academic compilation citing standard TRB literature); "c. 4,800–4,100 BP" for the Netherlands (Academia.edu, Dutch beaker chronology paper — note this is BP, i.e. roughly 2,850–2,150 cal BC, a notably later/shorter range than the north-central European span); in **southern Sweden** specifically, the earliest TRB dates are "all placed around 3,950–3,900 BC cal," with the TRB "very hard to see... before c. 4,000 BC" there (Lund University journal, regional radiocarbon dataset) — i.e. Funnelbeaker arrives markedly later in southern Scandinavia than in its core central European area. In **western Ukraine**, TRB dates run "3,700/3,600–3,000/2,900 BC" (Institute of Archaeology and Ethnology, Polish Academy of Sciences journal).
  - Dating method: radiocarbon-calibrated (cal BC), region-specific compilations.
- **Significance:** Funnelbeaker (Trichterbecherkultur/TRB) is the major farming culture of north-central Europe following LBK-derived and other post-LBK groups; associated with the spread of megalithic tomb-building (the dataset's existing "Atlantic Megalithic Tradition," 4794–1500 BCE, overlaps and interacts with TRB) and eventual absorption/succession by Corded Ware.
- **Sources:**
  - [The Early Funnel Beaker Culture in Southern Sweden and Central Europe (Lund University open-access journal)](https://journals.lub.lu.se/lar/article/download/21767/19601/53159)
  - [Chronology of the Funnel Beaker Culture Settlement in Western Ukraine in the Context of Radiocarbon Dating (Academia.edu)](https://www.academia.edu/42194690/Chronology_of_the_Funnel_Beaker_Culture_Settlement_in_Western_Ukraine_in_the_Context_of_Radiocarbon_Dating)
  - [Radiocarbon dataset for the TRB central-place at Kałdus, Poland (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39113785/)
  - [Dutch beaker chronology re-examined (Academia.edu)](https://www.academia.edu/2304446/Dutch_beaker_chronology_re_examined)
- **Flags:** ⚑ Note strong regional variability in TRB's arrival — as late as c. 3,950 BC in southern Sweden vs. c. 4,300 BC in the culture's core central European area — authoring should treat TRB as a diachronic spread, not a single simultaneous start date across its whole range, similar to the LBK/Cardial "route" distinctions above.

### Michelsberg culture
- **Dates:** German Wikipedia (locator only, cross-checked) gives overall radiocarbon range "etwa 4,400 bis 3,500 v. Chr." (c. 4,400–3,500 BC). This is corroborated by multiple peer-reviewed/institutional sources: a PLOS ONE ancient-DNA paper states the culture "appeared" around **4,400 cal BC**, with radiocarbon on human bones from a mass-burial context ranging **4,100–3,500 cal BC**; a Heidelberg University journal article on Hesse gives three sub-phases: "older" (ältere) c. 4,340/4,200–3,960 cal BC, "younger" (jüngere) c. 3,950–3,800/3,700 cal BC, "youngest" (jüngste) 3,800/3,700–3,500 cal BC. A late/final Michelsberg enclosure at Calden (Offa-Journal PDF, Wartberg chronology paper) is dated to "the 37th century calBC," i.e. c. 3,700–3,600 BC.
  - Dating method: radiocarbon-calibrated (cal BC); phase sub-division from typo-chronological and radiocarbon synthesis (Heidelberg paper).
- **Significance:** Michelsberg is a major Middle/Late Neolithic culture of the Rhineland, Paris Basin, and adjoining regions of France/Germany/Belgium, known for large causewayed enclosures and flint-mining complexes; broadly contemporary with, and partly overlapping the geographic range of, early TRB groups to its north/east.
- **Sources:**
  - [Multi-scale ancient DNA analyses confirm the western origin of Michelsberg farmers and document probable practices of human sacrifice (PLOS ONE)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0179742)
  - [EV - 88 (Heidelberg University journal, Michelsberg phase chronology for Hesse)](https://journals.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/index.php/germania/libraryFiles/downloadPublic/763)

  - Locator only: [Michelsberger Kultur — Wikipedia (German)](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelsberger_Kultur)
- **Flags:** None major; multiple independent peer-reviewed sources converge on the c. 4,400–3,500 BC span.

## SECTION 3: IF TIME PERMITS

### Skara Brae
- **Dates:** Modern re-evaluation (2017, cited by the Ness of Brodgar research team) revises the start of occupation earlier, to **c. 3,300 BC**, though "most of the larger buildings we can view today date from around 2,900 BC." The site was abandoned briefly, then **re-occupied 2,800–2,700 BC**, and finally abandoned **c. 2,500 BC**. This differs somewhat from the older standard figure (still repeated by Wikipedia, locator only, and Britannica) of continuous occupation "c. 3,180 BC to c. 2,500 BC" (~600 years) established by 1970s radiocarbon dating.
  - Dating method: radiocarbon (1970s original dating establishing Neolithic date, superseding an earlier mistaken "Iron Age/Pictish, c. 500 BC" attribution); 2017 re-evaluation of the Orcadian radiocarbon dataset.
- **Significance:** Skara Brae is Europe's most complete preserved Neolithic village, located in Orkney, Scotland; part of the Heart of Neolithic Orkney UNESCO World Heritage complex alongside the Ness of Brodgar and the stone circles of Stenness/Brodgar.
- **Sources:**
  - [Skara Brae – the date and extent of the settlement (Ness of Brodgar research site, citing the 2017 radiocarbon re-evaluation)](https://www.nessofbrodgar.co.uk/skarabrae-date-and-extent/)
  - [Skara Brae (Geological Society of London, "100 Great Geosites")](https://www.geolsoc.org.uk/science-and-policy/100-great-geosites/human-habitation/skara-brae/)
  - Locator only: [Skara Brae — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skara_Brae)
- **Flags:** ⚑ **LITERATURE MOVED (2017)** — the re-evaluation pushed Skara Brae's first occupation back to c. 3,300 BC (from the older 3,180 BC figure) and clarified a previously underappreciated abandonment/re-occupation cycle (occupied c. 3,300 BC → largely abandoned shortly after 2,900 BC → re-occupied 2,800–2,700 BC → finally abandoned c. 2,500 BC) rather than one continuous 600-year occupation. **Common misconception to flag:** Skara Brae is very often described as continuously inhabited for ~600 years; the 2017 reassessment instead documents at least one significant occupation gap.

### Ness of Brodgar
- **Dates:** Formal Bayesian radiocarbon modelling (Oxford/Cardiff research, 65 radiocarbon measurements) finds the main dated phase of activity began **3,060–2,950 cal BC** (95% probability); the "piered architecture" specifically was in use from **3,020–2,920 cal BC** to **2,855–2,665 cal BC** (95% probability each); the massive Structure Ten's hearth was last used **c. 2,500 cal BC**. Popular reporting (National Geographic) rounds this to "first settled around 3,200 B.C." — a looser figure than the peer-reviewed Bayesian model. Separately, an early Mesolithic hazelnut shell from Orkney's East Mainland was independently radiocarbon-dated to **6,820–6,660 BC**, showing human presence in Orkney at least 1,500 years before the Ness of Brodgar's Neolithic structures.
  - Dating method: radiocarbon (conventional ages, Stuiver & Polach 1977 convention) with formal Bayesian chronological modelling across two alternative models.
- **Significance:** A major Late Neolithic ceremonial/monumental complex on Orkney, situated between the stone circles of Stenness and Brodgar; excavation (ongoing until a 2024 pause/hiatus) revealed some of the largest and most architecturally elaborate Neolithic buildings in northwestern Europe, predating the main phase of Stonehenge.
- **Sources:**
  - [To cut a long story short: formal chronological modelling for the Ness of Brodgar (University of Oxford / Cardiff repository PDF)](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/96028/1/Ness_paper_text%20v18%20Resubmitted%20(002).pdf)
  - [The rise and fall of the Ness complex (Ness of Brodgar official research site)](https://www.nessofbrodgar.co.uk/about/rise-and-fall/)
  - [Palaeolithic and Mesolithic — Orkney (Ness of Brodgar research site, on the 6820-6660 BC hazelnut shell)](https://www.nessofbrodgar.co.uk/orkney-prehistory/the-first-settlers/)
- **Flags:** None beyond noting the difference between the precise Bayesian-modelled peer-reviewed dates (c. 3,060–2,950 cal BC start) and looser popular rounding (e.g., "3,200 BC") — authoring should prefer the Bayesian range.

### Newgrange
- **Dates:** Peer-reviewed sources converge on construction/primary use **c. 3,300–3,000 cal BC** (Cambridge/Proceedings of the Prehistoric Society, 2025/2026 paper, citing O'Kelly 1982 and Lynch et al. 2014), with continued deposition inside the tomb until **c. 2,700 cal BC**, when the interior was sealed. A separate excavation-report critical analysis (newgrange.com PDF, referencing Schulting et al. 2017 and new 2025 animal-bone dates) states construction/primary use of "developed passage tombs" (including Newgrange) is "well-established at c. 3,300–3,000 cal BC," and reports five *new* radiocarbon measurements on animal bone (Smyth et al. 2025) ranging **3,300–2,700 cal BC**, confirming the chamber was undisturbed between initial sealing (before ~2,500 BC) and its 1699 CE antiquarian rediscovery.
  - Dating method: radiocarbon (AMS on animal/human bone and charred organic material); the original 1960s excavation also used radiocarbon (giving an uncalibrated date of "around 2,500 bc," which calibrates to roughly 3,200 BC — a fact explicitly explained in a Newgrange.com institutional history page to correct a common confusion).
- **Significance:** Newgrange (Brú na Bóinne, County Meath, Ireland) is a major passage tomb, older by construction date than Stonehenge's main stone phase and the Great Pyramid of Giza; renowned for its winter solstice roof-box alignment.
- **Sources:**
  - [Pigs, pannage, and the solstice: isotopic insights from prehistoric feasting at Newgrange (Cambridge/Proceedings of the Prehistoric Society)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/proceedings-of-the-prehistoric-society/article/pigs-pannage-and-the-solstice-isotopic-insights-from-prehistoric-feasting-at-newgrange/ECC3D799B42C21FAA0EDF3045C230F33)
  - [King of Newgrange: critical analysis (newgrange.com, citing Smyth et al. 2025 new radiocarbon dates)](https://www.newgrange.com/pdf/king-of-newgrange-critical-analysis.pdf)
  - [Newgrange – Archaeology, Art and Legend (newgrange.com institutional history, explains uncalibrated-vs-calibrated date confusion)](https://www.newgrange.com/newgrange-archaeology-book.htm)
- **Flags:** **Common misconception to flag:** many popular sources (and some older academic ones) still cite Newgrange's radiocarbon date as an *uncalibrated* "c. 2,500 BC," which is a raw lab-conventional-age figure, not a true calendar date. The correct **calibrated** construction date is c. 3,200 BC (~3,300–3,000 cal BC per the most recent Bayesian-informed peer-reviewed work) — a 600–700 year difference that is a classic and instructive case of calibration confusion in the literature, explicitly documented on the newgrange.com institutional page.

### Ancient-DNA picture: Anatolian farmer ancestry replacing hunter-gatherers, and the later steppe influx
- **Dates/findings:**
  - A landmark 2015 Nature paper (Haak et al.) using ancient genomes established that Neolithic farmers appearing in Germany, Hungary, and Spain by **~8,000–7,000 years ago** were genetically distinct from indigenous hunter-gatherers; by **~6,000–5,000 years ago** European farmers show increasing admixture with local hunter-gatherer ancestry; and **~4,500 years ago**, Corded Ware people in Germany derived **~75% of their ancestry from Yamnaya** steppe pastoralists, documenting a "massive migration" event. This steppe ancestry "persisted in all sampled central Europeans until at least ~3,000 years ago."
  - A newer, larger-scale synthesis (PMC, 2022, "spatiotemporal patterns of major human admixture events") refines the timing: Anatolian-farmer-related gene flow becomes "widespread across Europe" starting **~6,400 BCE**, and is "present in almost all parts of continental Europe and the British Isles" by **~4,300 BCE**; the mixed Corded-Ware-associated population is shown to have formed rapidly, within **~3,000–2,900 BCE**, "across diverse regions."
  - A very recent (2026) Nature paper on Bell Beaker origins states that between **6,500 and 4,000 BCE**, Anatolian-farmer descendants replaced **70–100% of local (hunter-gatherer) ancestry** across most of Europe, with a notable exception: wetland/riverine/coastal areas of the Netherlands, Belgium, and western Germany retained **~50% hunter-gatherer ancestry** for roughly 3,000 years longer than most of the rest of Europe, only assimilating fully around/after the Bell Beaker period.
  - Steppe-ancestry arrival into the Corded Ware culture: Wikipedia (locator only) states "the earliest radiocarbon dates for Corded Ware... point to the period around 3,000 BC" in Poland, with the culture "appearing after 2,880 BC" elsewhere in central Europe — consistent with the peer-reviewed genetic dating above.
  - Dating method for all of the above: ancient-DNA admixture-dating statistical methods (e.g., DATES, qpAdm) calibrated against archaeologically/radiocarbon-dated skeletal remains; underlying sample dates are typically direct AMS radiocarbon on the sequenced individuals.
- **Significance:** This is arguably the single most consequential empirical revision to European prehistory in the last fifteen years: ancient-DNA studies (beginning in earnest c. 2014–2015) demonstrated that the Neolithic transition in Europe involved substantial **population replacement by incoming Anatolian-derived farmers** (not simple cultural diffusion among static populations), and that a second, comparably large replacement event brought **Yamnaya-derived steppe ancestry** into Corded Ware (and subsequently Bell Beaker) populations around 3,000–2,900 BCE, an event now widely linked to the spread of Indo-European languages into Europe.
- **Sources:**
  - [Massive migration from the steppe was a source for Indo-European languages in Europe (Haak et al., Nature, 2015)](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature14317)
  - [The spatiotemporal patterns of major human admixture events during the European Holocene (PMC, 2022)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9293011/)
  - [Lasting Lower Rhine-Meuse forager ancestry shaped Bell Beaker expansion (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam repository, 2026)](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/lasting-lower-rhine-meuse-forager-ancestry-shaped-bell-beaker-exp/)
  - [Ancient DNA Study Reveals the Prehistory of Southeastern Europe (Max Planck Institute for the Science of Human History, discusses Lepenski Vir individuals directly)](https://www.shh.mpg.de/843656/genomic-history-of-southeastern-europe)
  - [Corded Ware cultural complexity uncovered using genomic and isotopic analysis from south-eastern Poland (Nature Scientific Reports)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-020-63138-w)
  - Locator only: [Corded Ware culture — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corded_Ware_culture)
- **Flags:** ⚑ **LITERATURE MOVED (2015, and continuously refined through 2022–2026)** — this entire framework did not exist in anything like its current, quantified form before the ancient-DNA revolution beginning c. 2014–2015 (Haak et al.; Allentoft et al. same year). It should be flagged prominently: **pre-2015 narratives** of the Neolithic transition as primarily "cultural diffusion" (farming ideas spreading among genetically stable populations) are now known to be **substantially wrong** for most of Europe — the dominant mechanism was demographic replacement. **Common misconception to flag directly:** the idea that modern Europeans are essentially the descendants of Mesolithic hunter-gatherers who "adopted" farming and later "adopted" Indo-European culture; genetics instead shows two large, distinct population-replacement events (Anatolian farmers c. 6,500–4,000 BCE; steppe pastoralists c. 3,000–2,900 BCE onward) with only partial, geographically uneven admixture of the preceding populations — the Dutch/Belgian/west-German wetlands "hunter-gatherer refuge" (per the 2026 Nature paper) is a genuinely new and important exception to the general replacement pattern, not yet in most textbook accounts.

## SUMMARY OF ⚑ LITERATURE-MOVED / MISCONCEPTION FLAGS (for quick authoring reference)

1. **Lepenski Vir** — ⚑ 2018 Bayesian AMS re-dating (Borić et al., *Nature Scientific Reports*) substantially revised the original excavator's 1969/1972 stratigraphic chronology, adding a documented ~700-year occupation hiatus.
2. **Doggerland** — ⚑ 2026 sediment-core study (Bensharada et al., *Humans*) gives the most precise start-of-inundation date yet (10,243–10,199 years ago); separately, an active (2025–2027) University of Groningen project explicitly flags unresolved problems with existing Doggerland radiocarbon dates. Misconception: Doggerland's loss is often wrongly described as a single tsunami event rather than a millennia-long gradual inundation.
3. **Franchthi Cave / Sesklo** — ⚑ Cereal-cultivation dates at Franchthi were pushed earlier (into the first half of the 7th millennium cal BC) by 2013 AMS work; separately, Thessaly's Neolithic start was revised from an older "7,000 cal BC" to "6,700–6,500 cal BC" in a peer-reviewed synthesis.
4. **Varna necropolis** — ⚑ Peer-reviewed AMS/Bayesian dating (Higham & Chapman) placed cemetery use ~200 years later (c. 4,596–4,341 cal BC) than the older, still widely-repeated "4,600–4,200 BC" traditional chronology.
5. **Skara Brae** — ⚑ 2017 re-evaluation pushed first occupation to c. 3,300 BC and revealed an occupation gap, correcting the popular "continuous 600-year occupation from 3,180 BC" narrative.
6. **Newgrange** — Misconception: an uncalibrated radiocarbon age of "c. 2,500 bc" is still sometimes quoted as if it were a calendar date; the correct calibrated date is c. 3,200 (3,300–3,000 cal) BC.
7. **Ancient DNA / Anatolian farmer & steppe ancestry** — ⚑ The entire quantitative population-replacement framework is a post-2015 development that overturns older "cultural diffusion" models of the Neolithic and Bronze Age transitions; refined again as recently as 2026 with the discovery of a persistent Lower Rhine-Meuse hunter-gatherer-ancestry refuge.
8. **Danubian vs. Mediterranean route debate** — Misconception: popular treatments often imply one uniform "wave of advance"; the sourced literature is consistent that these were two distinct routes with different speeds and different underlying demographic mechanisms.
9. **Tardenoisian / Sauveterrian** — Flagged as internally inconsistent across fetched sources (calibration ambiguity, regional divergence); recommend `n.a.` for any single pan-European date range until a modern Bayesian synthesis is located.
