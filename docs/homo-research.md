# Human Prehistory Chronology Dataset — Genus *Homo* and Stone-Tool Industries

**Compiled:** 2026-08-07. **Scope:** 24 entities (12 hominin taxa of genus *Homo*, 12 stone-tool industries/technocomplexes), plus an anchor entry for the first appearance of genus *Homo*.

**Conventions used in this file**
- **Ma** = millions of years ago; **ka** = thousands of years ago; **cal BP** = calibrated radiocarbon years before present (AD 1950); **BP** without "cal" = uncalibrated radiocarbon years.
- Uncertainties are reproduced exactly as the cited source states them (± values, 1σ/2σ, or 95.4% probability ranges).
- **"n.a."** means the value could not be confirmed from a source fetched for this report. It is not an assertion that no such value exists in the literature.
- Every date and factual claim carries a markdown link to the exact page the value was read from.
- *Homo ergaster* convention: this report treats **African early *Homo erectus* fossils as *H. erectus sensu lato***, i.e. *H. ergaster* is **not** given a separate entry, and is noted as a splitting convention within the *H. erectus* entry. This follows the usage of the [Smithsonian Human Origins *Homo erectus* page](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-erectus), which notes that some scientists distinguish African *H. ergaster* from Asian *H. erectus sensu stricto*.
- *Homo longi* is **included** (entity 12), but classified as **contested-undecided/definitional**: 2025 palaeoproteomic and mtDNA work indicates the Harbin cranium belongs to the Denisovan population, which makes *H. longi* partly redundant with entity 8.

## ANCHOR — FIRST APPEARANCE OF GENUS *HOMO*

**Best current estimate: ≈2.78 Ma**, based on the LD 350-1 mandible from Ledi-Geraru, Afar, Ethiopia.

- The Ledi-Geraru hominin-bearing deposits are bracketed by dated tuffs: the **Gurumaha Tuff at 2.782 ± 0.006 Ma (1σ)**, the **Lee Adoyta Tuffs at 2.631 ± 0.011 Ma**, and the **Giddi Sands Tuff at 2.593 ± 0.006 Ma**, dated by **single-crystal ⁴⁰Ar/³⁹Ar incremental heating** at the Berkeley Geochronology Center, with **magnetostratigraphic** control ([Nature 2025, Reed/Villmoare et al.](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09390-4)).
- The same 2025 paper reports additional *Homo* material (LD 302-23 premolar; AS 100-1a/b assigned *Homo* sp. indet.) and notes the next-oldest widely accepted *Homo* specimens: **A.L. 666-1 at 2.3 Ma** and **MLP-1549 at 2.5–2.4 Ma** ([Nature 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09390-4)).
- The original announcement placed *Homo* in the region at **2.80–2.75 Ma** ([Villmoare et al. 2015, Science — PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25739410/)).
- **Dispute:** a published Comment challenges the unequivocal assignment of LD 350-1 to *Homo* ([PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26089505/)). The *date* of the fossil is largely settled (tight ⁴⁰Ar/³⁹Ar bracketing); the *taxonomic attribution* is contested.
- **Credible range across the literature:** first *Homo* somewhere between **~2.8 Ma** (Ledi-Geraru, if accepted) and **~2.3 Ma** (A.L. 666-1, if Ledi-Geraru is rejected) ([Nature 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09390-4)).

# GROUP A — HOMININ SPECIES (genus *Homo*)

## 1. *Homo habilis*

**A. First appearance / start:** **2.4 Ma** ([Smithsonian Human Origins](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-habilis)).

**B. Last appearance / end:** **1.4 Ma**; the late specimen **KNM-ER 42703 is dated to 1.44 Ma** ([Smithsonian Human Origins](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-habilis)).

**C. Uncertainty bounds:** **~2.4 Ma to ~1.4 Ma** as the accepted span ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-habilis)). The older end is soft because the earliest attributions overlap with the *Homo* sp. indet. material now reported from Ledi-Geraru at 2.78–2.59 Ma ([Nature 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09390-4)). The young end is constrained by KNM-ER 42703 at 1.44 Ma, which is younger than the *H. erectus* specimen KNM-ER 42700 at 1.55 Ma, demonstrating temporal overlap between the two species ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-habilis)).

**D. Dating method(s):** n.a. for method-specific figures on this page; the East African chronology for these deposits rests on **⁴⁰Ar/³⁹Ar dating of tuffs plus magnetostratigraphy**, as documented for the adjacent Ledi-Geraru/Afar sequence ([Nature 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09390-4)).

**E. Geographic range:** Eastern and southern Africa; the Turkana Basin of Kenya and Olduvai Gorge in Tanzania are the principal areas ([Smithsonian *H. habilis*](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-habilis); [Smithsonian *H. rudolfensis*](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-rudolfensis)).

**F. Type site / key site:** **OH 7, Olduvai Gorge, Tanzania** — found in 1960, named in 1964 by Leakey, Tobias and Napier ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-habilis)).

**G. Dispute status:** **contested-undecided.** The date range is not seriously disputed, but the *validity of the species* is: the Smithsonian page explicitly records ongoing debate over whether the *H. habilis* hypodigm is a single species and whether it belongs in *Homo* at all ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-habilis)). Recent revision: none of the dates for this taxon has been widely revised in the last 15 years; what changed is the surrounding context — the earliest *Homo* record moved back to ~2.78 Ma in 2015 and was reinforced in 2025 ([Nature 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09390-4)).

**H. One-sentence summary:** An early, small-brained human that lived in Africa from about 2.4 to 1.4 million years ago and overlapped with other human species.

## 2. *Homo rudolfensis*

**A. First appearance / start:** **1.9 Ma** ([Smithsonian Human Origins](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-rudolfensis)).

**B. Last appearance / end:** **1.8 Ma** ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-rudolfensis)).

**C. Uncertainty bounds:** The accepted span is narrow, **1.9–1.8 Ma**, but the wider context is a Turkana Basin record in which **four hominin species coexisted between 2.0 and 1.5 Ma**, so the true span of *rudolfensis*-like morphology may be broader ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-rudolfensis)).

**D. Dating method(s):** n.a. for method-specific figures on this page; Turkana Basin ages rest on **⁴⁰Ar/³⁹Ar tuff dating and magnetostratigraphy** as documented for the equivalent Afar/Turkana tuff framework ([Nature 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09390-4)).

**E. Geographic range:** Eastern Africa, specifically the Lake Turkana basin of northern Kenya ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-rudolfensis)).

**F. Type site / key site:** **KNM-ER 1470, Koobi Fora, Lake Turkana, Kenya** ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-rudolfensis)).

**G. Dispute status:** **contested-undecided.** The taxon itself is disputed: it was originally named *Pithecanthropus rudolfensis* by Alexeev in 1986, and some researchers argue the material belongs in *Australopithecus* rather than *Homo* ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-rudolfensis)). No major date revision in the last 15 years is documented on the fetched page.

**H. One-sentence summary:** A flat-faced, big-toothed early human from Kenya about 1.9 million years ago whose place in the human genus is still argued over.

## 3. *Homo erectus* (*sensu lato*, including African "*H. ergaster*")

**A. First appearance / start:** **1.89 Ma**; earliest fossils **~1.9 Ma** ([Smithsonian Human Origins](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-erectus)).

**B. Last appearance / end:** Two figures are in play.
- Smithsonian gives a species range ending at **110,000 years ago**, and notes that *H. erectus* coexisted with *H. sapiens* in Indonesia as recently as **~143,000 years ago** ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-erectus)).
- The dedicated redating of the Ngandong bone bed places the **last known occurrence of the species at 117–108 thousand years ago** ([Rizal et al. 2019/2020, Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1863-2)).

**C. Uncertainty bounds:** Oldest credible end of the range: **~1.9 Ma** ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-erectus)). Youngest credible end: the Ngandong modelled interval of **117–108 ka**, with the Smithsonian's rounder **110 ka** falling inside it ([Nature 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1863-2); [Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-erectus)).

**D. Dating method(s):** For the terminal Ngandong occurrence, **Bayesian modelling of 52 radiometric age estimates**, combining **uranium-series dating of speleothems**, **luminescence dating**, **⁴⁰Ar/³⁹Ar dating**, **uranium-series dating of non-human fossils**, and **uranium-series/electron-spin-resonance (US–ESR) dating** of non-human fossils ([Rizal et al., Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1863-2)).

**E. Geographic range:** Africa (Turkana Basin), the Caucasus (Dmanisi, Georgia) and East/Southeast Asia (China, Java) ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-erectus)).

**F. Type site / key site:** **Trinil 2, Trinil, Java, Indonesia** — the first specimen, found by Eugène Dubois in 1891 and named *Pithecanthropus erectus* in 1894 ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-erectus)). Key terminal site: **Ngandong, Solo River terrace, Central Java, Indonesia** ([Nature 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1863-2)).

**G. Dispute status:** **definitional**, with a method-driven revision at the young end.
- Definitional: African early specimens are treated by some authors as a separate species ***H. ergaster***, with the Asian fossils as ***H. erectus sensu stricto***; others lump both as *H. erectus sensu lato* ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-erectus)). This report uses the lumped convention.
- **Widely revised date (within the last 15 years):** the last appearance at Ngandong. Earlier literature allowed a much younger survival; the 2019 Nature study established a robust **117–108 ka** bone-bed age from Bayesian modelling of 52 radiometric estimates, confirming Ngandong as the youngest known occurrence of the species ([Nature 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1863-2)). The cause was a systematic multi-method dating campaign of the terrace and bone bed rather than a single new technique.

**H. One-sentence summary:** The first widely travelled human species, present from about 1.9 million years ago until roughly 110,000 years ago in Java.

## 4. *Homo antecessor*

**A. First appearance / start:** **857 ka** as the old end of the combined age range for the fossil-bearing level TD6 at Gran Dolina; the range is **780–857 ka (2σ on the weighted mean age)** ([Falguères et al., "Earliest humans in Europe: the age of TD6 Gran Dolina", CNRS-HAL PDF](https://cnrs.hal.science/hal-03739352/file/DolinaJHEHAL.pdf)). A broader Spanish-fossil bracket of **800,000–1.3 Ma** is given by the Smithsonian in its discussion of the Spanish Early/Middle Pleistocene material ([Smithsonian *H. heidelbergensis*](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-heidelbergensis)).

**B. Last appearance / end:** **780 ka**, the young limit of the TD6 range, set by the **reversed (Matuyama) magnetic polarity** of the level, which requires an age **greater than 780 ka** ([Falguères et al., HAL PDF](https://cnrs.hal.science/hal-03739352/file/DolinaJHEHAL.pdf)). The species is known from a single level, so "last appearance" and "first appearance" collapse onto the same interval.

**C. Uncertainty bounds:** Individual results on the same level span a wide range:
- **EU-ESR ages: 626–685 ka**; **LU-ESR ages: 736–807 ka** ([HAL PDF](https://cnrs.hal.science/hal-03739352/file/DolinaJHEHAL.pdf)).
- **Combined U-series/ESR ages: 676 ± 101 ka, 762 ± 114 ka, 770 ± 116 ka**, weighted mean **731 ± 63 ka** ([HAL PDF](https://cnrs.hal.science/hal-03739352/file/DolinaJHEHAL.pdf)).
- Palaeomagnetism forces the age above 780 ka, giving the published preferred range **780–857 ka** ([HAL PDF](https://cnrs.hal.science/hal-03739352/file/DolinaJHEHAL.pdf)).
- The whole Gran Dolina sequence records human occupation from about **800 to 300 ka** ([HAL PDF](https://cnrs.hal.science/hal-03739352/file/DolinaJHEHAL.pdf)).

**D. Dating method(s):** **Electron spin resonance (ESR) on fossil tooth enamel** (g = 2.0018 signal, additive dose method, ⁶⁰Co gamma irradiation) combined with **U-series (alpha spectrometry) on enamel, dentine and cementum**, using early-uptake (EU), linear-uptake (LU) and combined U-series/ESR models; plus **magnetostratigraphy** (Matuyama–Brunhes boundary at 780 ka, located in overlying level TD7) and **biostratigraphy** (arvicolid rodents *Mimomys savini*, *Pliomys episcopalis*, *Iberomys huescarensis*) ([HAL PDF](https://cnrs.hal.science/hal-03739352/file/DolinaJHEHAL.pdf)).

**E. Geographic range:** Western Europe — known only from northern Spain ([HAL PDF](https://cnrs.hal.science/hal-03739352/file/DolinaJHEHAL.pdf)).

**F. Type site / key site:** **Gran Dolina, level TD6 ("Aurora Stratum"), Sierra de Atapuerca, near Burgos, Spain** ([HAL PDF](https://cnrs.hal.science/hal-03739352/file/DolinaJHEHAL.pdf)).

**G. Dispute status:** **method conflict** on the date, plus a **contested taxon**.
- Method conflict: ESR alone (EU model) would put TD6 at 626–685 ka, several hundred thousand years younger than the palaeomagnetically required >780 ka; only the combined U-series/ESR treatment reconciles the two ([HAL PDF](https://cnrs.hal.science/hal-03739352/file/DolinaJHEHAL.pdf)).
- Contested taxon: it is disputed whether *H. antecessor* is a distinct species or should be folded into *H. heidelbergensis* ([Smithsonian *H. heidelbergensis*](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-heidelbergensis)).
- The Smithsonian's dedicated *H. antecessor* page was not retrievable (404) at the time of writing, so no museum-level dedicated source is cited here.

**H. One-sentence summary:** An early European human from a Spanish cave, about 800,000 years old, whose status as a separate species is still argued.

## 5. *Homo heidelbergensis*

**A. First appearance / start:** **700,000 years ago**, with the caveat that the range "may reach **1.3 Ma**" if the older Spanish and Italian material is included ([Smithsonian Human Origins](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-heidelbergensis)).

**B. Last appearance / end:** **200,000 years ago** ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-heidelbergensis)).

**C. Uncertainty bounds:** Oldest credible end **~1.3 Ma** (if Spanish fossils at 800 ka–1.3 Ma and the Ceprano, Italy cranium at ~1 Ma are included); youngest credible end **~200 ka** ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-heidelbergensis)). Anchor points inside the range: **Sima de los Huesos, Atapuerca, ~400 ka** with roughly 30 individuals; the **Schöningen spears at 400 ka**; and controlled fire at **Gesher Benot Ya'aqov, Israel, at 790 ka** ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-heidelbergensis)).

**D. Dating method(s):** n.a. on the fetched museum page; no method is specified for the *H. heidelbergensis* range there ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-heidelbergensis)).

**E. Geographic range:** Europe, Africa and western Asia — Germany, Spain, Italy, Israel and eastern/southern Africa ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-heidelbergensis)).

**F. Type site / key site:** **Mauer mandible, Rösch sandpit near Heidelberg, Germany**, found 1908 and named by Schoetensack ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-heidelbergensis)).

**G. Dispute status:** **definitional.** The species functions partly as a wastebasket for Middle Pleistocene humans, and several proposed splits — ***H. antecessor***, ***H. cepranensis***, ***H. rhodesiensis*** — are disputed ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-heidelbergensis)). Genetic context: the Neanderthal–modern human divergence is placed at **350,000–400,000 years ago**, which constrains how the taxon can be positioned ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-heidelbergensis)). No single date revision is flagged on the fetched page.

**H. One-sentence summary:** A large-brained Middle Pleistocene human in Europe, Africa and the Near East, roughly 700,000 to 200,000 years ago.

## 6. *Homo naledi*

**A. First appearance / start:** **335,000 years ago** ([Smithsonian Human Origins](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-naledi)).

**B. Last appearance / end:** **236,000 years ago** ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-naledi)).

**C. Uncertainty bounds:** **335,000–236,000 years ago** is the published interval, established in 2017 (Dirks et al. 2017, *eLife* 6:e24231) ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-naledi)). The fetched museum page does not report a wider credible envelope, so the bounds beyond 335–236 ka are **n.a.**

**D. Dating method(s):** n.a. on the fetched page; the page attributes the 335–236 ka result to Dirks et al. 2017 without listing techniques ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-naledi)).

**E. Geographic range:** Southern Africa — known only from one cave system in South Africa ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-naledi)).

**F. Type site / key site:** **Rising Star Cave (Dinaledi and Lesedi Chambers), South Africa**; discovered 2013, published 2015, with more than **1,550 specimens from at least 15 individuals** ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-naledi)).

**G. Dispute status:** **contested-undecided.** The **phylogenetic position of the species is unresolved** ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-naledi)). **Widely revised date within the last 15 years:** yes, in the sense that the fossils had no reliable age at all when announced in 2015; the 2017 dating study fixed them at **335,000–236,000 years ago**, far younger than the primitive anatomy had suggested to many commentators ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-naledi)).

**H. One-sentence summary:** A small-brained human species from a South African cave, surprisingly recent at about 335,000 to 236,000 years ago.

## 7. *Homo neanderthalensis*

**A. First appearance / start:** **400,000 years ago** ([Smithsonian Human Origins](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-neanderthalensis)).

**B. Last appearance / end:** **40,000 years ago** — "all traces disappeared by about 40,000 years ago" ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-neanderthalensis)). The associated Mousterian technocomplex ends across Europe at **41,030–39,260 cal BP (95.4% probability)** ([Higham et al. 2014, Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature13621)).

**C. Uncertainty bounds:** Oldest end **~400 ka**, with the common ancestor of Neanderthals and modern humans placed between **700,000 and 300,000 years ago** ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-neanderthalensis)). Youngest end: the museum figure of ~40 ka is tightened by the 40-site radiocarbon programme to **41,030–39,260 cal BP** for the disappearance of the Mousterian ([Nature 2014](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature13621)). Regionally the end is earlier: in Cantabrian Spain the Mousterian ends at **47.9–45.1 ka cal BP (95.4%)** ([Marín-Arroyo et al. 2018, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0194708)). Overlap with modern humans is estimated at **2,600–5,400 years (95.4%)** in the pan-European model ([Nature 2014](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature13621)), while the Smithsonian describes an overlap in western Asia of **30,000–50,000 years** ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-neanderthalensis)).

**D. Dating method(s):** **AMS radiocarbon dating with Bayesian modelling** for the terminal chronology, across 40 sites from Russia to Spain ([Nature 2014](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature13621)); **AMS ¹⁴C plus OSL and TL with OxCal Bayesian modelling** for the Iberian sequences ([PLOS ONE 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0194708); [Rios-Garaizar et al. 2022, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0265219)).

**E. Geographic range:** Europe and southwestern to central Asia ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-neanderthalensis)); the terminal-Mousterian dataset spans **Russia to Spain** ([Nature 2014](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature13621)).

**F. Type site / key site:** **Neanderthal 1, Feldhofer Cave, Neander Valley, Germany**, found 1856 and named by William King in 1864; earlier-found specimens include Engis (Belgium, 1829) and Forbes' Quarry (Gibraltar, 1848) ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-neanderthalensis)).

**G. Dispute status:** **rival chronologies** at the young end. The taxon is secure; the extinction date is not uniform.
- **Widely revised date within the last 15 years:** the Neanderthal/Mousterian extinction date. Before the 2014 programme, individual sites produced apparently much younger Neanderthal survivals; the recalibrated, ultrafiltration-pretreated AMS dataset compressed the disappearance into **41,030–39,260 cal BP** ([Nature 2014](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature13621)). The cause was improved radiocarbon pretreatment plus Bayesian site modelling, which removed contamination-driven young outliers.
- Regional conflict remains: Cantabrian Spain gives **47.9–45.1 ka cal BP** ([PLOS ONE 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0194708)), and southern Iberia at Bajondillo has the last Mousterian at **~50–46 cal ka BP** with the Middle Palaeolithic ceasing around **45 cal ka BP** ([Cortés-Sánchez et al. 2019, PDF](http://www.ugr.es/~grupo179/pdf/CortesSanchez%202019.pdf)) — several millennia earlier than the pan-European figure.

**H. One-sentence summary:** Cold-adapted humans of Europe and western Asia who lived from about 400,000 years ago and vanished around 40,000 years ago.

## 8. Denisovans (Denisovan hominin; sometimes *Homo denisova*)

**A. First appearance / start:** The oldest Denisovan fossil at Denisova Cave dates to as early as **195,000 years ago (95.4% probability)** ([Douka et al. 2019, Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-018-0870-z)). Outside Siberia, the **Xiahe 1 mandible is at least 160 ka**, identified as Denisovan from ancient proteins ([Chen et al. 2019, Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1139-x)).

**B. Last appearance / end:** The youngest Denisovan fossil dates to **52,000–76,000 years ago** ([Douka et al. 2019, Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-018-0870-z)).

**C. Uncertainty bounds:** Fossil-based Denisovan presence spans roughly **195 ka to 52 ka** ([Douka et al. 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-018-0870-z)), but the *site* was occupied from about **300,000 to 20,000 years ago** on the sediment chronology ([Jacobs et al. 2019, Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-018-0843-2)), so the credible envelope for Denisovan occupation extends older than the oldest dated fossil. Xiahe 1 pushes the geographic and temporal reach to **≥160 ka on the Tibetan Plateau** ([Chen et al. 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1139-x)). Neanderthal fossils at the same cave date to **80,000–140,000 years ago**, including **Denisova 11**, a Neanderthal–Denisovan first-generation hybrid ([Douka et al. 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-018-0870-z)). Upper Palaeolithic tooth pendants and bone points at the cave date to **43,000–49,000 cal BP** ([Douka et al. 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-018-0870-z)).

**D. Dating method(s):** For the fossils, a **Bayesian model combining radiocarbon, uranium-series and optical (luminescence) ages with stratigraphy and genetic data**, with fossil identification by **ZooMS/MALDI-ToF-MS collagen peptide mass fingerprinting** ([Douka et al. 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-018-0870-z)). For the deposits, **single-grain OSL on quartz and post-IR IRSL (pIRIR) on K-feldspar**, 47 samples, modelled in **OxCal 4.2.4** ([Jacobs et al. 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-018-0843-2)). For Xiahe 1, **U-Th dating of the carbonate crust** adhering to the mandible, with taxonomic assignment by **ancient protein analysis** ([Chen et al. 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1139-x)).

**E. Geographic range:** Southern Siberia (Altai Mountains) and the Tibetan Plateau of China ([Douka et al. 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-018-0870-z); [Chen et al. 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1139-x)).

**F. Type site / key site:** **Denisova Cave, Altai Mountains, Russia** ([Douka et al. 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-018-0870-z)). Second key site: **Baishiya Karst Cave, Xiahe, Gansu, China** ([Chen et al. 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1139-x)).

**G. Dispute status:** **definitional.** The population is genetically unambiguous, but it has no formally accepted species name and no type specimen in the conventional sense; it is defined by DNA and proteins rather than morphology ([Chen et al. 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1139-x)).
- **Widely revised chronology within the last 15 years:** yes. The previous Denisova Cave chronology relied on radiocarbon and therefore could not resolve anything older than about **50,000 years**; the 2019 luminescence programme extended the sequence to **~300,000–20,000 years ago** ([Jacobs et al. 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-018-0843-2)), and the companion Bayesian study placed the oldest Denisovan fossil at **~195 ka** ([Douka et al. 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-018-0870-z)). The cause was the application of single-grain OSL and pIRIR luminescence dating beyond the radiocarbon limit.

**H. One-sentence summary:** A human group known mainly from DNA, living in Siberia and Tibet from at least 195,000 until about 52,000 years ago.

## 9. *Homo floresiensis*

**A. First appearance / start:** Fossil remains from **~100 kyr ago**; associated stone artefacts from **~190 kyr ago** ([Sutikna et al. 2016, Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature17179)). The Smithsonian gives the species range as **100,000–50,000 years ago**, fossils **100,000–60,000 years ago**, and tools **190,000–50,000 years ago** ([Smithsonian Human Origins](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-floresiensis)).

**B. Last appearance / end:** **~60 kyr ago** for the skeletal remains, with artefacts continuing to **~50 kyr ago** ([Sutikna et al. 2016](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature17179)).

**C. Uncertainty bounds:** Fossils **~100–60 kyr**, artefacts **~190–50 kyr** ([Sutikna et al. 2016](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature17179)); the Smithsonian's slightly wider species bracket runs to **50,000 years ago** ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-floresiensis)). Superseded values, now rejected: LB1 at **~18 kyr cal BP** and a last appearance at **~17 kyr cal BP** or **13–11 kyr cal BP**, from deposits previously thought to span **95–12 kyr** ([Sutikna et al. 2016](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature17179)).

**D. Dating method(s):** **Stratigraphic re-excavation** combined with **thermoluminescence on quartz**, **IRSL/pIRIR on feldspar**, **U-series on bone and speleothems**, and **⁴⁰Ar/³⁹Ar** on hornblende from tephra T1, which yielded **79 ± 12 kyr (1σ)** ([Sutikna et al. 2016](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature17179)).

**E. Geographic range:** Island Southeast Asia — known only from the island of Flores, Indonesia ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-floresiensis)).

**F. Type site / key site:** **Liang Bua cave, Flores, Indonesia** — the only site ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-floresiensis)).

**G. Dispute status:** **largely settled**, after a major revision.
- **Widely revised date within the last 15 years — the flagship case in this dataset.** Old value: LB1 at **~18 kyr cal BP**, with the species surviving to **~17 kyr** or even **13–11 kyr cal BP**. New value: remains **~100–60 kyr**, artefacts **~190–50 kyr**. Approximate year of revision: **2016**. Cause: renewed excavation showed that the original dated samples came from a stratigraphically younger, unconformably overlying sequence, not from the layers containing the skeleton; new TL, IRSL/pIRIR, U-series and ⁴⁰Ar/³⁹Ar ages were obtained on the correct deposits ([Sutikna et al. 2016, Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature17179)).
- Taxonomic dispute: the claim that LB1 was a pathological modern human is described by the Smithsonian as now largely resolved in favour of a valid distinct taxon ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-floresiensis)).

**H. One-sentence summary:** The tiny "hobbit" humans of Flores, now dated to roughly 100,000 to 60,000 years ago rather than 18,000.

## 10. *Homo luzonensis*

**A. First appearance / start:** **at least 130,000 years ago**, revised in 2023 ([Natural History Museum, London](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/homo-luzonensis-small-ancient-human-species-philippines.html)). The published figure is a minimum age of **134 ± 14 ka**, spanning the MIS 6 to MIS 5 transition ([Grün & Stringer 2023, *Quaternary Science Reviews* — summary of the paper](https://www.southeastasianarchaeology.com/2023/11/24/paper-direct-dating-of-human-fossils-and-the-ever-changing-story-of-human-evolution/)).

**B. Last appearance / end:** **n.a.** No youngest-remains date is established; the original metatarsal age of **67,000 years** is now interpreted as an artefact of post-burial uranium uptake rather than a true age ([NHM London](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/homo-luzonensis-small-ancient-human-species-philippines.html)). The original publication reported the metatarsal at **67,000 years** with 12 additional elements from at least three individuals, CCH2–CCH9 ([Détroit et al. 2019, Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1067-9)).

**C. Uncertainty bounds:** From **67,000 years** (original U-series minimum, 66.7 ± 1 ka; [Mijares et al. 2010, JHE — PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20569967/)) to **≥134 ± 14 ka** (2023 reassessment) ([Grün & Stringer 2023 summary](https://www.southeastasianarchaeology.com/2023/11/24/paper-direct-dating-of-human-fossils-and-the-ever-changing-story-of-human-evolution/)). Both figures are *minimum* ages, so the true age may be older still ([NHM London](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/homo-luzonensis-small-ancient-human-species-philippines.html)).

**D. Dating method(s):** **Uranium-series dating** directly on the fossils, in both the original and the revised study; the 2023 revision reinterpreted the data by modelling **secondary uranium overprints and leaching** ([NHM London](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/homo-luzonensis-small-ancient-human-species-philippines.html); [Grün & Stringer 2023 summary](https://www.southeastasianarchaeology.com/2023/11/24/paper-direct-dating-of-human-fossils-and-the-ever-changing-story-of-human-evolution/)).

**E. Geographic range:** Island Southeast Asia — known only from northern Luzon, Philippines ([Détroit et al. 2019, Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1067-9)).

**F. Type site / key site:** **Callao Cave, northern Luzon, Philippines** ([Détroit et al. 2019, Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1067-9)).

**G. Dispute status:** **method conflict**, and the taxon is also disputed.
- **Widely revised date within the last 15 years:** old value **~67,000 years** (2010/2019), new value **≥130,000–134 ± 14 ka**, revision year **2023**. Cause: recognition that the fossils absorbed uranium after burial, biasing U-series ages young ([NHM London](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/homo-luzonensis-small-ancient-human-species-philippines.html)).
- Taxonomic dispute: the NHM describes the status of the species as **uncertain**, with some researchers objecting to naming a species on so few fossils, and Chris Stringer raising the possibility of a relationship to *H. floresiensis* while provisionally accepting the name ([NHM London](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/homo-luzonensis-small-ancient-human-species-philippines.html)).

**H. One-sentence summary:** A small archaic human from a cave in the Philippines, now thought to be at least 130,000 years old.

## 11. *Homo sapiens* (anatomically modern — first appearance)

**A. First appearance / start:** **315 ± 34 ka**, the weighted-average thermoluminescence age of fire-heated flints associated with the Jebel Irhoud hominins, described as the oldest reported *H. sapiens* remains ([Richter et al. 2017, Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature22335)).

**B. Last appearance / end:** **extant.**

**C. Uncertainty bounds:** The Jebel Irhoud site deposits span **383–247 kyr**, and a recalculated combined U-series/ESR age on a tooth from the Irhoud 3 mandible gives **286 ± 32 ka** ([Richter et al. 2017](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature22335)). In eastern Africa, Omo I has a revised **minimum age of 233 ± 22 kyr** ([Vidal et al. 2022, Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04275-8)). So the credible range for the first appearance of *H. sapiens* runs from about **350 ka** (older end of the Irhoud TL uncertainty) to about **230 ka** (the eastern African minimum), depending on which fossils are accepted as *H. sapiens*.

**D. Dating method(s):** Where methods and sites disagree, each is reported separately:
- **Thermoluminescence on heated flint** — Jebel Irhoud, **315 ± 34 ka** ([Richter et al. 2017](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature22335)).
- **Combined U-series/ESR on tooth enamel** — Irhoud 3 mandible, **286 ± 32 ka** ([Richter et al. 2017](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature22335)).
- **⁴⁰Ar/³⁹Ar on sanidine from proximal pumice of the Shala Qi2 ignimbrite, geochemically correlated to the KHS Tuff** — Omo I, **minimum 233 ± 22 kyr** ([Vidal et al. 2022](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04275-8)).

**E. Geographic range:** Originating in Africa (Morocco and Ethiopia hold the oldest fossils) and subsequently global ([Richter et al. 2017](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature22335); [Vidal et al. 2022](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04275-8)).

**F. Type site / key site:** **Jebel Irhoud, Morocco** for the oldest remains ([Richter et al. 2017](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature22335)); **Omo-Kibish Formation, lower Omo valley, southern Ethiopia** for the oldest eastern African remains ([Vidal et al. 2022](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04275-8)).

**G. Dispute status:** **rival chronologies**, driven by which fossils count as *H. sapiens*.
- **Widely revised dates within the last 15 years — two cases.** (i) Jebel Irhoud: the 2017 study "almost doubles" the previous age estimates for the site, moving it to **315 ± 34 ka**; cause was systematic TL dating of heated flints plus recalculation of the U-series/ESR tooth age ([Richter et al. 2017](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature22335)). (ii) Omo I: revised in **2022** to a minimum of **233 ± 22 kyr** by geochemically correlating the overlying KHS Tuff to a dated proximal eruption deposit ([Vidal et al. 2022](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04275-8)).
- The dispute is definitional as much as chronometric: the Irhoud fossils are not fully modern in cranial shape, so whether "*H. sapiens* at 315 ka" is the same claim as "anatomically modern humans at 315 ka" is contested ([Richter et al. 2017](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature22335)). A companion morphological paper is [Hublin et al. 2017, Nature 546:289-292](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28593953/).

**H. One-sentence summary:** Our own species, with the oldest known fossils from Morocco at roughly 315,000 years ago, still living today.

## 12. *Homo longi* ("Dragon Man")

**Included, with a strong caveat:** the name is published and formally available, but 2025 molecular work indicates the holotype belongs to the Denisovan population, which makes *H. longi* largely redundant with entity 8.

**A. First appearance / start:** **n.a.** as a species-level first appearance. The holotype cranium **HBSM2018-000018(A)** has a **reliable minimum U-series age of 148 ± 2 ka**, and the enclosing upper Upper Huangshan Formation spans **~138–309 ka** ([Ji, Wu, Ji, Li & Ni 2021, *The Innovation*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8454552/)).

**B. Last appearance / end:** **n.a.** The taxon is known from a single cranium, so no last-appearance date exists ([Ji et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8454552/)). If the Harbin cranium is treated as Denisovan, the relevant last appearance is the Denisovan figure of **52,000–76,000 years ago** ([Douka et al. 2019, Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-018-0870-z)).

**C. Uncertainty bounds:** **≥148 ± 2 ka** as a minimum, with the containing formation allowing **~138–309 ka** ([Ji et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8454552/)).

**D. Dating method(s):** **Uranium-series dating** of the cranium, plus stratigraphic assignment to the upper Upper Huangshan Formation ([Ji et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8454552/)). Note: the cranium was recovered without documented provenance, so the age is a minimum from the fossil itself rather than a bracketed stratigraphic age ([Ji et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8454552/)).

**E. Geographic range:** Northeast Asia — a single locality in Heilongjiang, northeast China ([Ji et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8454552/)).

**F. Type site / key site:** **Harbin City, Heilongjiang, China**, near the Dongjiang Bridge over the Songhua River ([Ji et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8454552/)).

**G. Dispute status:** **contested-undecided / definitional — the taxon itself is disputed.** In 2025, palaeoproteomic analysis recovering **95 endogenous proteins including three Denisovan-derived variants**, together with mitochondrial DNA from dental calculus, indicated that the Harbin cranium belongs to the Denisovan population ([Fu et al. 2025, Science and Cell — PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40531192/)). If accepted, this either makes *H. longi* the valid name for Denisovans or makes it a junior synonym of an unnamed group, depending on nomenclatural preference. The **date** (≥148 ka) is not the point of contention; the **identity** is.

**H. One-sentence summary:** A large fossil skull from northeast China, at least 148,000 years old, that 2025 molecular work identifies as a Denisovan.

# GROUP B — STONE TOOL INDUSTRIES / TECHNOCOMPLEXES

## 13. Oldowan

**A. First appearance / start:** Two competing figures.
- **~2.9 Ma** at Nyayanga, Kenya, where the artefact-bearing deposits are bracketed between **3.032 and 2.581 Ma**, with **~2.9 Ma** favoured ([Oldowan review, HAL open archive](https://hal.science/hal-05406726v1/document)). The Smithsonian describes these as Oldowan tools "3 million years ago", up to **400,000 years older than previously believed** (Plummer et al.) ([Smithsonian Human Origins](https://humanorigins.si.edu/research/whats-hot-human-origins/oldowan-tools-3-million-years-ago)).
- **2.6 Ma** as the securely established start, from Gona, Ethiopia, dated to **2.6–2.5 Myr** ([Semaw et al. 1997, Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/385333a0)); the review states the Oldowan appears "possibly 2.9 Ma, definitely by 2.6 Ma" ([HAL review](https://hal.science/hal-05406726v1/document)).
- Britannica gives the rounder "about 2 million years ago" ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Oldowan-industry)).

**B. Last appearance / end:** **~1.7 Ma**; many researchers view the Oldowan span as **~2.6–1.7 Ma** ([HAL review](https://hal.science/hal-05406726v1/document)). Britannica states the industry was made for nearly **1 million years** before developing into the Acheulean ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Oldowan-industry)). Note the overlap: the earliest Acheulean is dated to **1.76 Myr** ([Lepre et al. 2011, Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature10372)).

**C. Uncertainty bounds:** Oldest credible: **3.032 Ma** (older bracket at Nyayanga) ([HAL review](https://hal.science/hal-05406726v1/document)). Youngest credible: **~1.7 Ma** ([HAL review](https://hal.science/hal-05406726v1/document)). Key dated occurrences inside the range: Ledi-Geraru **2.61–2.58 Ma**; Gona **2.58–2.3 Ma**; Ain Boucherit, Algeria **2.4 Ma**; Hadar **2.36 Ma**; Lokalalei **2.34 Ma**; Omo Shungura **2.34–2.3 Ma**; plus a possible occurrence in the Zarqa Valley, Jordan ([HAL review](https://hal.science/hal-05406726v1/document)). For context, an earlier, separate industry — the **Lomekwian at 3.3 Ma**, Lomekwi 3 (LOM3), West Turkana, Kenya — predates the Oldowan by about 700,000 years and was dated by **palaeomagnetic, tephrostratigraphic and isotopic data** ([Harmand et al. 2015, Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature14464)).

**D. Dating method(s):** **Radio-isotopic (⁴⁰Ar/³⁹Ar and K-Ar) dating of volcanic tuffs combined with magnetic polarity stratigraphy** at Gona ([Semaw et al. 1997, Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/385333a0)); the same tuff-plus-magnetostratigraphy framework underpins the Nyayanga and Turkana ages ([HAL review](https://hal.science/hal-05406726v1/document); [Harmand et al. 2015](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature14464)).

**E. Geographic range:** Predominantly eastern Africa (Ethiopia, Kenya, Tanzania), extending to North Africa (Algeria) and possibly the southern Levant ([HAL review](https://hal.science/hal-05406726v1/document); [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Oldowan-industry)).

**F. Type site / key site:** **Olduvai Gorge, Tanzania** (eponymous type locality), with Omo (Ethiopia) and East Turkana (Kenya) as other principal sites ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Oldowan-industry)).

**G. Dispute status:** **rival chronologies.**
- **Widely revised date within the last 15 years:** the start. Old value **~2.6 Ma**; new claimed value **~2.9 Ma** (bracket 3.032–2.581 Ma) at Nyayanga, Kenya; revision published in **2023** (Plummer et al.), described by the Smithsonian as up to 400,000 years older than previously believed ([Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/research/whats-hot-human-origins/oldowan-tools-3-million-years-ago); [HAL review](https://hal.science/hal-05406726v1/document)). The cause was a new site with a bracketed but relatively wide age range, which is exactly why the older figure is not yet universal — the review still distinguishes "possibly 2.9 Ma" from "definitely by 2.6 Ma" ([HAL review](https://hal.science/hal-05406726v1/document)).
- Also definitional at the young end: the Oldowan and the earliest Acheulean overlap, so the "end" of the Oldowan is a typological boundary rather than a horizon ([HAL review](https://hal.science/hal-05406726v1/document); [Lepre et al. 2011](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature10372)).

**H. One-sentence summary:** The earliest widespread stone toolkit — simple flakes and choppers — made in Africa from about 2.6 million, possibly 2.9 million, years ago.

## 14. Acheulean

**A. First appearance / start:** **1.76 Myr**, the earliest Acheulean, at **Kokiselei 4** in the Nachukui Formation, West Turkana, Kenya — about **350,000 years older than the previous earliest record** ([Lepre et al. 2011, Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature10372)). Britannica gives the start as **1.7–1.5 Ma** ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Acheulean-industry)).

**B. Last appearance / end:** **250,000–200,000 years ago** ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Acheulean-industry)).

**C. Uncertainty bounds:** Oldest credible **1.76 Ma** ([Lepre et al. 2011](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature10372)); youngest credible **~200 ka** ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Acheulean-industry)). The young end blurs into the Mousterian, since the earliest Mousterian assemblages "intergrade thoroughly with the latest Acheulean industries" ([Straus, "The Mousterian, Present and Future of a Concept", Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/pub/173/oa_monograph/chapter/230633)).

**D. Dating method(s):** **Palaeomagnetic (magnetostratigraphic) data** at Kokiselei 4 ([Lepre et al. 2011](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature10372)); **typological** definition for the industry as a whole ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Acheulean-industry)).

**E. Geographic range:** Africa, Europe, the Middle East, and Asia as far east as Kolkata; notably absent from East Asia, which instead has a chopper/chopping-tool industry ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Acheulean-industry)).

**F. Type site / key site:** **Saint-Acheul, Somme valley, northern France** ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Acheulean-industry)). Key early site: **Kokiselei 4, West Turkana, Kenya** ([Lepre et al. 2011](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature10372)).

**G. Dispute status:** **largely settled** on the broad span, with a **method-driven revision at the start** and a **definitional** fuzziness at the end.
- **Widely revised date within the last 15 years:** old value ~1.4 Ma for the earliest Acheulean; new value **1.76 Ma** at Kokiselei 4; revision year **2011**; cause was palaeomagnetic dating of a newly excavated West Turkana locality, which moved the earliest record back about 350,000 years ([Lepre et al. 2011, Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature10372)).
- The terminal boundary is definitional: it is drawn by tool typology against the Mousterian rather than by a dated event ([Project MUSE chapter](https://muse.jhu.edu/pub/173/oa_monograph/chapter/230633)).

**H. One-sentence summary:** The long-lived handaxe tradition, made across Africa, Europe and Asia from about 1.76 million to 200,000 years ago.

## 15. Mousterian

**A. First appearance / start:** **more than 130,000 years ago**, during the Last Interglacial, with the caveat that it is hard to separate from the latest Acheulean ([Project MUSE, "The Mousterian, Present and Future of a Concept"](https://muse.jhu.edu/pub/173/oa_monograph/chapter/230633)). At Bajondillo Cave, southern Spain, the Middle Palaeolithic technological tradition lasted from approximately **160–46 cal ka BP** ([Cortés-Sánchez et al. 2019, PDF](http://www.ugr.es/~grupo179/pdf/CortesSanchez%202019.pdf)).

**B. Last appearance / end:** Three figures, by region and method.
- Pan-European: **41,030–39,260 cal BP (95.4%)** ([Higham et al. 2014, Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature13621)).
- Cantabrian Spain: **47.9–45.1 ka cal BP (95.4%)** ([Marín-Arroyo et al. 2018, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0194708)).
- Southern Spain (Bajondillo): the last Middle Palaeolithic level Bj/14, a Denticulate Mousterian, at **~50–46 cal ka BP**, with the Middle Palaeolithic ceasing at about **45 cal ka BP** ([Cortés-Sánchez et al. 2019](http://www.ugr.es/~grupo179/pdf/CortesSanchez%202019.pdf)).
- The narrative account gives the round figure of replacement by Upper Palaeolithic industries "some **40,000 years ago**" ([Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/pub/173/oa_monograph/chapter/230633)). Britannica dates it to the early Würm, ending c. **40,000 bc**, and says it disappeared abruptly along with the Neanderthals ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Mousterian-industry)).

**C. Uncertainty bounds:** Oldest credible: **~160 ka** if the full southern Iberian Middle Palaeolithic is included ([Cortés-Sánchez et al. 2019](http://www.ugr.es/~grupo179/pdf/CortesSanchez%202019.pdf)), or **>130 ka** on the conservative typological definition ([Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/pub/173/oa_monograph/chapter/230633)). Youngest credible: **39,260 cal BP** (young limit of the pan-European end boundary) ([Higham et al. 2014](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature13621)).

**D. Dating method(s):** **AMS radiocarbon with Bayesian modelling** for the terminal chronology ([Higham et al. 2014](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature13621); [Marín-Arroyo et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0194708)); **AMS ¹⁴C on short-lived marine and terrestrial molluscs and charcoal plus thermoluminescence on heat-altered lithics**, calibrated with OxCal 4.3 and IntCal13/Marine13 at Bajondillo ([Cortés-Sánchez et al. 2019](http://www.ugr.es/~grupo179/pdf/CortesSanchez%202019.pdf)); **typological** definition for the industry itself ([Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/pub/173/oa_monograph/chapter/230633)).

**E. Geographic range:** Mostly Europe, parts of western Asia and North Africa, with the most characteristic form in western Europe ([Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/pub/173/oa_monograph/chapter/230633); [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Mousterian-industry)).

**F. Type site / key site:** **Le Moustier, Dordogne, France** — the term was applied by Gabriel de Mortillet in 1869 and 1872 to collections made by Edouard Lartet in 1864 ([Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/pub/173/oa_monograph/chapter/230633)).

**G. Dispute status:** **definitional**, with a secondary layer of **rival chronologies** at the end.
- Definitional: the Mousterian's boundaries are blurred at both ends — early assemblages intergrade with the latest Acheulean and the "proto-Quina Tayacian", late ones with the Châtelperronian — and Bordes's four "facies" (Charentian with Quina and Ferrassie subtypes, Denticulate, Mousterian of Acheulean Tradition A/B, Typical) are argued to be arbitrary constructs on a continuum, with the author calling for the facies concept to be abandoned ([Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/pub/173/oa_monograph/chapter/230633)).
- **Widely revised date within the last 15 years:** the end. Old position: apparently much later Mousterian survivals at individual sites. New value: **41,030–39,260 cal BP (95.4%)** across 40 sites; year **2014**; cause was improved radiocarbon pretreatment plus Bayesian modelling, which eliminated contamination-driven young ages ([Higham et al. 2014, Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature13621)). Regional models still disagree by several millennia ([Marín-Arroyo et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0194708); [Cortés-Sánchez et al. 2019](http://www.ugr.es/~grupo179/pdf/CortesSanchez%202019.pdf)).
- Also disputed: who made it. Authorship is "almost all assumed" to be Neanderthal, but the chapter argues stone tools cannot settle questions of biological identity ([Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/pub/173/oa_monograph/chapter/230633)).

**H. One-sentence summary:** The flake-tool tradition of Neanderthals in Europe, western Asia and North Africa, ending about 40,000 years ago.

## 16. Aterian

**A. First appearance / start:** **145 ± 9 ka**, the weighted-mean thermoluminescence age of burnt stones from the Aterian assemblage containing stemmed points at Ifri n'Ammar, Morocco ([Jacobs et al. 2012, *Journal of Human Evolution* — PDF](http://in-africa.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/12/Jacobs-et-al-2012-JHE-Aterian-OSL-dates.pdf)). Cited independently as the earliest Aterian: "dating from at least **145 ka**" ([University of Southampton thesis record, "The Aterian and its place in the North African Middle Stone Age"](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/367294/)). A recent synthesis gives the Aterian as a whole as **~150 ka to 40 ka** ([Ben Arous et al. 2022, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0261282)).

**B. Last appearance / end:** **~40 ka** on the synthesis figure ([PLOS ONE 2022](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0261282)). The youngest directly dated Aterian occurrences reported in the OSL study are **60–35 ka** (bracketing ESR ages at Mugharet el 'Aliya) and **~59 ± 10 ka to 70 ± 4 ka** at Dar es-Soltan 1 and Contrebandiers ([Jacobs et al. 2012 PDF](http://in-africa.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/12/Jacobs-et-al-2012-JHE-Aterian-OSL-dates.pdf)).

**C. Uncertainty bounds:** **~150/145 ka at the old end to ~35–40 ka at the young end** ([PLOS ONE 2022](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0261282); [Jacobs et al. 2012 PDF](http://in-africa.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/12/Jacobs-et-al-2012-JHE-Aterian-OSL-dates.pdf)). The best-dated single-grain OSL sequences cluster much more tightly:
- El Mnasra, main Aterian sequence (Levels 11 and 7–4): weighted mean **108.9 ± 2.9 ka** (ten samples, P = 0.95); combining all Aterian ages gives **106.2 ± 3.0 ka** (P = 0.92); individual ages **117 ± 6 ka to 104 ± 8 ka** ([Jacobs et al. 2012 PDF](http://in-africa.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/12/Jacobs-et-al-2012-JHE-Aterian-OSL-dates.pdf)).
- El Harhoura 2: earliest Aterian level (Level 8) **106.7 ± 6.6 ka**; Level 6 **116.4 ± 6.6 ka**; Level 4b **99.9 ± 5.8 ka**; Level 4a **73.7 ± 4.1 ka**; Level 3 weighted mean **56.9 ± 2.2 ka** (range 51.6 ± 3.6 to 61.9 ± 4.1 ka) ([Jacobs et al. 2012 PDF](http://in-africa.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/12/Jacobs-et-al-2012-JHE-Aterian-OSL-dates.pdf)).
- El Mnasra Level 3: **75.3 ± 5.6 ka** and **71.6 ± 5.3 ka**, weighted mean **73.3 ± 4.2 ka** ([Jacobs et al. 2012 PDF](http://in-africa.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/12/Jacobs-et-al-2012-JHE-Aterian-OSL-dates.pdf)).
- The Ifri n'Ammar TL ages come in two discrete pulses separated by layers without stemmed points: **145 ± 9 ka** and **83 ± 6 ka** ([Jacobs et al. 2012 PDF](http://in-africa.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/12/Jacobs-et-al-2012-JHE-Aterian-OSL-dates.pdf)).
- A revised Bayesian model at El Mnasra confirms human presence at **124–104 ka**, earlier than the previous OSL and US-ESR data indicated ([PLOS ONE 2022](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0261282)).

**D. Dating method(s):** **Single-grain OSL on quartz** (180–212 µm, 31,400 grains measured, 9,424 usable; Risø TL/OSL-DA-15 with focused 532 nm laser; finite mixture modelling) ([Jacobs et al. 2012 PDF](http://in-africa.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/12/Jacobs-et-al-2012-JHE-Aterian-OSL-dates.pdf)); also **TL on burnt stones**, **ESR on tooth enamel**, and **U-series on flowstone** in the broader North African record ([Jacobs et al. 2012 PDF](http://in-africa.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/12/Jacobs-et-al-2012-JHE-Aterian-OSL-dates.pdf)); and **Bayesian modelling of single-aliquot and single-grain OSL plus US-ESR** in the 2022 revision ([PLOS ONE 2022](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0261282)).

**E. Geographic range:** North Africa, best documented across Morocco from the Atlantic coast (Témara region, ~9 km strip between Rabat and Casablanca, the westernmost known extent) to northeastern Morocco inland of the Mediterranean ([Jacobs et al. 2012 PDF](http://in-africa.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/12/Jacobs-et-al-2012-JHE-Aterian-OSL-dates.pdf)).

**F. Type site / key site:** Type site **n.a.** (not stated in the sources fetched). Key sites: **Ifri n'Ammar, Morocco** (oldest tanged assemblage) and **El Mnasra and El Harhoura 2, Témara, Morocco** (best-dated sequences) ([Jacobs et al. 2012 PDF](http://in-africa.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/12/Jacobs-et-al-2012-JHE-Aterian-OSL-dates.pdf)).

**G. Dispute status:** **definitional.** The Aterian is defined by the presence of tanged and foliate tools ([PLOS ONE 2022](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0261282)), but at Ifri n'Ammar stemmed-point assemblages occur in two separate pulses with non-tanged Middle Palaeolithic layers between them, so the Aterian cannot function as a chronostratigraphic marker ([Jacobs et al. 2012 PDF](http://in-africa.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/12/Jacobs-et-al-2012-JHE-Aterian-OSL-dates.pdf)). The 2012 study explicitly rejects treating the Aterian as a single short-lived culture ([Jacobs et al. 2012 PDF](http://in-africa.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/12/Jacobs-et-al-2012-JHE-Aterian-OSL-dates.pdf)).
- **Widely revised date within the last 15 years:** yes. Old position: the Aterian was thought to be a late, relatively short-lived industry, with earlier multi-grain OSL and ESR estimates that were less precise. New position: a long-lived industry starting by **145 ± 9 ka** and, in the Témara caves, tightly dated to **~117–104 ka** by single-grain OSL; revision published **2012**, with further Bayesian refinement to **124–104 ka** in **2022**. Cause: the shift from multi-grain to single-grain OSL, which removed mixing effects and produced more precise, reproducible and stratigraphically coherent ages ([Jacobs et al. 2012 PDF](http://in-africa.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/12/Jacobs-et-al-2012-JHE-Aterian-OSL-dates.pdf); [PLOS ONE 2022](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0261282)).

**H. One-sentence summary:** A North African toolkit with distinctive stemmed points, made from about 145,000 to 40,000 years ago, mostly known from Morocco.

## 17. Châtelperronian

**A. First appearance / start:** **43,760 BP** as the old limit of the southwest European span, **43,760–39,220 BP** ([Rios-Garaizar et al. 2022, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0265219)). In Cantabrian Spain specifically it lasts **42.6–41.5 ka cal BP** ([Marín-Arroyo et al. 2018, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0194708)).

**B. Last appearance / end:** End boundary **42.4–41.4 ka cal BP (95.4%)** in Cantabria ([Marín-Arroyo et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0194708)); **39,220 BP** as the young limit of the southwest European span ([Rios-Garaizar et al. 2022](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0265219)).

**C. Uncertainty bounds:** **43,760 BP to 39,220 BP** overall in southwest Europe ([Rios-Garaizar et al. 2022](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0265219)). Individual anchors: **Aranbaltza II, Basque coast, Spain — single-grain OSL 43.5 ± 2.9 ka (1σ)**; **Labeko Koba level IXinf — 43,850–40,950 cal BP**; **Ekain level Xa — 34,350 ± 550 BP = 40,700–37,700 cal BP** ([Rios-Garaizar et al. 2022](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0265219)).

**D. Dating method(s):** **AMS radiocarbon plus single-grain OSL plus TL**, modelled in **OxCal v4.4.4** ([Rios-Garaizar et al. 2022](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0265219)); **AMS ¹⁴C with Bayesian modelling** in the Cantabrian study ([Marín-Arroyo et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0194708)).

**E. Geographic range:** Southwestern Europe — from the Paris Basin of France south to northern Iberia ([Rios-Garaizar et al. 2022](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0265219)).

**F. Type site / key site:** Type site **n.a.** (not stated in fetched sources). Key sites: **Aranbaltza II, Labeko Koba and Ekain, Basque Country, Spain** ([Rios-Garaizar et al. 2022](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0265219)).

**G. Dispute status:** **contested-undecided.** In the Cantabrian region the Châtelperronian shows **no chronological overlap with the local Mousterian** — there is an interval of **2,700–5,800 years (95.4%, median 4,400 years)** between the end of the Mousterian and the start of the Châtelperronian — which implies the Châtelperronian is either **intrusive** in the region or an offshoot of a Mousterian tradition from elsewhere ([Marín-Arroyo et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0194708); [Rios-Garaizar et al. 2022](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0265219)). It also overlaps in time with the earliest Aurignacian, which appears at **43.3–40.5 ka cal BP** ([Marín-Arroyo et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0194708)). Typologically it intergrades with the Mousterian, since Châtelperronian levels contain Mousterian tool types ([Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/pub/173/oa_monograph/chapter/230633)).

**H. One-sentence summary:** A short-lived transitional toolkit in France and northern Spain around 43,000 to 39,000 years ago, whose makers are still debated.

## 18. Aurignacian

**A. First appearance / start:** **43.3–40.5 ka cal BP (95.4%)** for the regional start boundary in Cantabrian Spain ([Marín-Arroyo et al. 2018, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0194708)). In southern Iberia, the Aurignacian level Bj/13 at Bajondillo Cave has a Bayesian range of **43.4–40.0 cal ka BP**, with the level modelled at **~43.0–40.8 cal ka BP** and short-lived samples giving Bayesian medians of **42.5–41.2 cal ka BP** — an Early Aurignacian presence at **~43 cal ka BP** ([Cortés-Sánchez et al. 2019, PDF](http://www.ugr.es/~grupo179/pdf/CortesSanchez%202019.pdf)). A France-wide model reports Proto-Aurignacian at **41.5–39.9 k cal BP** and Early Aurignacian at **39.8–37.9 k cal BP** ([Banks et al. 2013 — PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23245623/)).

**B. Last appearance / end:** **34.6–33.1 ka cal BP (95.4%)** in Cantabria ([Marín-Arroyo et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0194708)). The terminal phase, Aurignacian V / Protosolutrean, is dated in France to **26,173–25,371 cal BP** ([Banks et al. 2024, *Quaternary*](https://www.mdpi.com/2571-550X/7/2/26)). At Bajondillo the Evolved Aurignacian level Bj/11 dates to **~37.6–32.4 cal ka BP** ([Cortés-Sánchez et al. 2019](http://www.ugr.es/~grupo179/pdf/CortesSanchez%202019.pdf)).

**C. Uncertainty bounds:** Oldest credible **43.4 ka cal BP** ([Cortés-Sánchez et al. 2019](http://www.ugr.es/~grupo179/pdf/CortesSanchez%202019.pdf)); youngest credible **25,371 cal BP** if the Aurignacian V/Protosolutrean is counted as Aurignacian ([Banks et al. 2024](https://www.mdpi.com/2571-550X/7/2/26)), or **33.1 ka cal BP** on the conventional Cantabrian end boundary ([Marín-Arroyo et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0194708)). Note that the Gravettian starts before the Aurignacian ends in Cantabria — the Gravettian starts at **36.8–35.0 ka cal BP**, giving an interval between the Aurignacian end and Gravettian start of **-3,300 to -800 years**, i.e. a long overlap ([Marín-Arroyo et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0194708)).

**D. Dating method(s):** **AMS ¹⁴C with Bayesian modelling** ([Marín-Arroyo et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0194708)); at Bajondillo, **¹⁴C/AMS on marine shells, terrestrial snail shells and charcoal plus thermoluminescence on thermo-altered lithics**, 26 dates including 17 new, OxCal 4.3 with IntCal13/Marine13, Markov chain Monte Carlo Bayesian model (Amodel 91.4%, Aoverall 93.4%) ([Cortés-Sánchez et al. 2019](http://www.ugr.es/~grupo179/pdf/CortesSanchez%202019.pdf)); **Bayesian modelling of ¹⁴C dates with IntCal20** in the French sequence ([Banks et al. 2024](https://www.mdpi.com/2571-550X/7/2/26)).

**E. Geographic range:** Upper Palaeolithic Europe, from southern Iberia to central Europe ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Aurignacian-culture); [Cortés-Sánchez et al. 2019](http://www.ugr.es/~grupo179/pdf/CortesSanchez%202019.pdf)).

**F. Type site / key site:** Type site **n.a.** (not stated in fetched sources). Key sites: **Bajondillo Cave, Torremolinos, Bay of Málaga, Spain** ([Cortés-Sánchez et al. 2019](http://www.ugr.es/~grupo179/pdf/CortesSanchez%202019.pdf)) and **Cueva del Castillo (Aurignacian Level 18), Cantabria, Spain** ([Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/pub/173/oa_monograph/chapter/230633)). Britannica confirms the Aurignacian follows the Mousterian and is succeeded by the Solutrean but gives no dates or type site ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Aurignacian-culture)).

**G. Dispute status:** **rival chronologies.** The date of first arrival differs sharply between models: an Early Aurignacian at **~43 cal ka BP** in southern Iberia ([Cortés-Sánchez et al. 2019](http://www.ugr.es/~grupo179/pdf/CortesSanchez%202019.pdf)) versus Proto-Aurignacian at **41.5–39.9 k cal BP** and Early Aurignacian at **39.8–37.9 k cal BP** in France ([Banks et al. 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23245623/)) — i.e. the southern Iberian Early Aurignacian would predate the French Early Aurignacian by several millennia. Whether Neanderthals and Aurignacians coexisted is also contested: the Bajondillo dates "do not indicate coexistence", with a possible ~2 kyr gap between Bj/14 and Bj/13 ([Cortés-Sánchez et al. 2019](http://www.ugr.es/~grupo179/pdf/CortesSanchez%202019.pdf)), whereas the Cantabrian model has the Aurignacian overlapping the Châtelperronian ([Marín-Arroyo et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0194708)).
- **Widely revised date within the last 15 years:** yes, for southern Iberia. Previously the region was thought to have received the Aurignacian late; the 2019 Bajondillo study placed an Early Aurignacian there at **~43 cal ka BP**, as early as anywhere in Europe, on the strength of 17 new AMS dates on short-lived samples ([Cortés-Sánchez et al. 2019](http://www.ugr.es/~grupo179/pdf/CortesSanchez%202019.pdf)).

**H. One-sentence summary:** The first clearly modern-human toolkit across Europe, appearing about 43,000 years ago and lasting until roughly 33,000 years ago.

## 19. Gravettian

**A. First appearance / start:** **35,340–33,595 cal BP (95.4%)**, or **34,520–33,825 cal BP (68.2%)** — the Bayesian start boundary for the Adriatic basin, described as similar to the date proposed for France and statistically indistinguishable from the oldest Gravettian in Europe at Hohle Fels ([Vukosavljević/Peresani et al., "Mid and Late Upper Palaeolithic in the Adriatic Basin", University of Southampton PDF](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/452843/1/1_s2.0_S0277379121005266_main.pdf)). In Cantabrian Spain the regional start is later, **36.8–35.0 ka cal BP** ([Marín-Arroyo et al. 2018, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0194708)).

**B. Last appearance / end:** **26,390–24,230 cal BP (95.4%)**, or **25,975–25,040 cal BP (68.2%)** in the Adriatic; in France the Gravettian ends at about **25.8 ka cal BP** and in central Europe at about **25–24 ka cal BP** ([Southampton PDF](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/452843/1/1_s2.0_S0277379121005266_main.pdf)).

**C. Uncertainty bounds:** Overall **~35–34 ka cal BP to ~26–25 ka cal BP**, a timespan of around **8,000–10,000 years** ([Southampton PDF](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/452843/1/1_s2.0_S0277379121005266_main.pdf)). Regional phase boundaries in France, 95% credible intervals in cal BP with IntCal20 and outliers removed ([Banks et al. 2024, *Quaternary*](https://www.mdpi.com/2571-550X/7/2/26)):
- Middle Gravettian, Pyrenees: **32,116–28,821**
- Middle Gravettian, north of the Pyrenees: **31,688–28,788**
- Northern Noaillian: **31,205–29,995**
- Rayssian: **30,519–28,518**
- Recent Gravettian: **29,165–26,670**
- Final Gravettian: **27,140–25,836**

**D. Dating method(s):** **Bayesian modelling of AMS and conventional radiocarbon dates** — 278 dates from 66 sites reduced to 188 dates from 52 sites on reliability criteria, modelled in **OxCal 4.3** and calibrated with **IntCal20**; an AMS-only model gave a start of **35,360–33,650 cal BP (95.4%)**, and the IntCal13 model gave **34,910–33,320 cal BP (95.4%)**, a three-to-four-century offset ([Southampton PDF](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/452843/1/1_s2.0_S0277379121005266_main.pdf)). The French phase model likewise uses Bayesian ¹⁴C modelling with IntCal20 ([Banks et al. 2024](https://www.mdpi.com/2571-550X/7/2/26)).

**E. Geographic range:** Continent-wide in Europe, from Iberia and France through central Europe to the Adriatic basin and eastern Europe ([Southampton PDF](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/452843/1/1_s2.0_S0277379121005266_main.pdf); [Banks et al. 2024](https://www.mdpi.com/2571-550X/7/2/26); [Marín-Arroyo et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0194708)).

**F. Type site / key site:** Type site **n.a.** (not stated in fetched sources). Key sites: **Hohle Fels, Germany** (layers IIc, IId, IIe — oldest Gravettian in Europe) ([Southampton PDF](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/452843/1/1_s2.0_S0277379121005266_main.pdf)) and **Aitzbitarte III, Gipuzkoa, Spain** (earliest Cantabrian Gravettian at 35.6–34.8 ka cal BP) ([Marín-Arroyo et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0194708)).

**G. Dispute status:** **largely settled** on the overall span, with **rival chronologies** regionally. Bayesian models from the Adriatic, France and central Europe now converge on **~35–34 ka to ~26–25 ka cal BP** ([Southampton PDF](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/452843/1/1_s2.0_S0277379121005266_main.pdf)). But regional starts differ by millennia — Cantabria at **36.8–35.0 ka cal BP** with the earliest site at **35.6–34.8 ka cal BP** in the east and the latest at **33.7–32.4 ka cal BP** at La Viña in Asturias ([Marín-Arroyo et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0194708)). A calibration-curve effect is documented: only the Gravettian *start* differs between IntCal13 and IntCal20, by three to four centuries ([Southampton PDF](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/452843/1/1_s2.0_S0277379121005266_main.pdf)). No taxon dispute applies.

**H. One-sentence summary:** A Europe-wide culture of ivory art and small pointed blades, from about 34,000 to 25,000 years ago.

## 20. Solutrean

**A. First appearance / start:** **c. 25 ka cal BP** overall ([Cascalheira & Bicho 2015, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0137308)). Phase start boundaries in southern Iberia (95.4%): Lower Solutrean **27,095–25,228 cal BP**; Middle **25,477–24,150**; Upper **26,035–24,497**; Solutreo-Gravettian **25,867–23,650 cal BP** ([Cascalheira & Bicho 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0137308)). In France, Lower Solutrean is modelled at **25,387–23,951 cal BP** ([Banks et al. 2024, *Quaternary*](https://www.mdpi.com/2571-550X/7/2/26)).

**B. Last appearance / end:** **c. 19 ka cal BP** overall ([Cascalheira & Bicho 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0137308)). Phase end boundaries in southern Iberia (95.4%): Lower **25,421–23,153**; Middle **24,352–22,234**; Upper **21,400–18,375**; Solutreo-Gravettian **19,684–14,701 cal BP** ([Cascalheira & Bicho 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0137308)). In France, Middle & Upper Solutrean is modelled at **24,440–22,836 cal BP** ([Banks et al. 2024](https://www.mdpi.com/2571-550X/7/2/26)).

**C. Uncertainty bounds:** Oldest credible **27,095 cal BP** (old limit of the Lower Solutrean start boundary) ([Cascalheira & Bicho 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0137308)); youngest credible **14,701 cal BP** (young limit of the Solutreo-Gravettian end boundary), though the conventional overall end is **~19 ka cal BP** ([Cascalheira & Bicho 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0137308)). The industry coincides with the Last Glacial Maximum ([Cascalheira & Bicho 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0137308)).

**D. Dating method(s):** **Bayesian modelling of radiocarbon dates in OxCal 4.2.4 with IntCal13 and Marine13**, across 103 southern Iberian sites ([Cascalheira & Bicho 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0137308)); **Bayesian ¹⁴C modelling with IntCal20** for the French phases ([Banks et al. 2024](https://www.mdpi.com/2571-550X/7/2/26)).

**E. Geographic range:** Confined to southwestern France and the Iberian Peninsula ([Cascalheira & Bicho 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0137308)).

**F. Type site / key site:** Type site **n.a.** (not stated in fetched sources). Key site for the southern Iberian classification: **Parpalló cave, Valencia, Spain** ([Cascalheira & Bicho 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0137308)).

**G. Dispute status:** **method conflict / contested-undecided on internal structure.** The Bayesian analysis **does not confirm the traditional ordering of the Solutrean phases**: the Upper Solutrean start boundary (26,035–24,497 cal BP) is older than the Middle Solutrean start boundary (25,477–24,150 cal BP), which contradicts the assumed Lower → Middle → Upper succession ([Cascalheira & Bicho 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0137308)). The overall span **~25–19 ka cal BP** is not in serious dispute ([Cascalheira & Bicho 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0137308)). No taxon dispute applies.
- **Widely revised interpretation within the last 15 years:** yes, in **2015** — not the overall span but the internal phase sequence, which Bayesian modelling of 103 sites failed to reproduce ([Cascalheira & Bicho 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0137308)).

**H. One-sentence summary:** An Ice Age toolkit of finely shaped leaf points, made in Spain, Portugal and southwest France roughly 25,000 to 19,000 years ago.

## 21. Magdalenian

**A. First appearance / start:** **21,211–17,988 cal BP** for the Lower Magdalenian in France, preceded by the Badegoulian at **23,092–20,792 cal BP** ([Banks et al. 2024, *Quaternary*](https://www.mdpi.com/2571-550X/7/2/26)). The post-LGM Magdalenian expansion is described as occurring around **17,000–15,000 BP** ([Reade et al. 2020, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7746568/)).

**B. Last appearance / end:** At Kůlna Cave, Moravia, the Magdalenian dates to **c. 15,630–14,610 cal BP**, and the overlying Epimagdalenian to **c. 14,140–12,680 cal BP**, with a hiatus of **400–1,000 years** between them ([Reade et al. 2020, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7746568/); [Reade & Stevens 2020, UCL Discovery PDF](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10116183/1/Stevens_Reade2020_Article_MagdalenianAndEpimagdalenianCh.pdf)). More broadly, the Late Upper Palaeolithic begins with the late Magdalenian around **14,000 cal BP** and ends at the Pleistocene–Holocene boundary around **11,600 cal BP** ([Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B, 2018](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rstb.2017.0264)).

**C. Uncertainty bounds:** Oldest credible **~21,211 cal BP** (old limit of the Lower Magdalenian in France) ([Banks et al. 2024](https://www.mdpi.com/2571-550X/7/2/26)); youngest credible **~12,680 cal BP** if the Epimagdalenian is included ([Reade et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7746568/)), or **~14,000 cal BP** for the Magdalenian proper ([Royal Society 2018](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rstb.2017.0264)). The earliest confirmed Magdalenian site in Moravia, Balcarka Cave, dates to **c. 17,000 cal BP** ([UCL Discovery PDF](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10116183/1/Stevens_Reade2020_Article_MagdalenianAndEpimagdalenianCh.pdf)).

**D. Dating method(s):** **AMS radiocarbon on bone collagen with Bayesian modelling in OxCal 4.4 and IntCal20** at Kůlna Cave ([Reade et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7746568/); [UCL Discovery PDF](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10116183/1/Stevens_Reade2020_Article_MagdalenianAndEpimagdalenianCh.pdf)); **Bayesian ¹⁴C modelling with IntCal20** for the French phase boundaries ([Banks et al. 2024](https://www.mdpi.com/2571-550X/7/2/26)).

**E. Geographic range:** Western and central Europe — France, Iberia, Germany, the Czech Republic and, at its northern maximum, as far as Britain and Poland ([UCL Discovery PDF](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10116183/1/Stevens_Reade2020_Article_MagdalenianAndEpimagdalenianCh.pdf); [Banks et al. 2024](https://www.mdpi.com/2571-550X/7/2/26)).

**F. Type site / key site:** Type site **n.a.** (not stated in fetched sources). Key sites: **Kůlna Cave, Moravian Karst, Czech Republic** and **Balcarka Cave, Czech Republic** ([Reade et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7746568/); [UCL Discovery PDF](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10116183/1/Stevens_Reade2020_Article_MagdalenianAndEpimagdalenianCh.pdf)).

**G. Dispute status:** **rival chronologies**, mostly regional and definitional. The French phase model starts the Lower Magdalenian at **21,211–17,988 cal BP** ([Banks et al. 2024](https://www.mdpi.com/2571-550X/7/2/26)), while central European sequences begin around **17,000 cal BP** ([UCL Discovery PDF](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10116183/1/Stevens_Reade2020_Article_MagdalenianAndEpimagdalenianCh.pdf)); the end depends on whether the Epimagdalenian/Azilian is counted, which shifts the terminal date from ~14,600 to ~12,700 cal BP ([Reade et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7746568/)). The Kůlna study establishes that the Magdalenian and Epimagdalenian deposits are **discrete units**, not a continuum ([Reade et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7746568/)). No taxon dispute applies.

**H. One-sentence summary:** The reindeer hunters and cave painters of late Ice Age Europe, roughly 21,000 to 14,000 years ago.

## 22. Howiesons Poort

**A. First appearance / start:** **c. 64,800 years ago**, the old end of the single-grain OSL range for eight sites, **c. 64,800–59,500 years ago** ([Wurz 2013, *Current Anthropology*](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/673283), citing Jacobs et al. 2008). A competing thermoluminescence chronology at Diepkloof puts the Howiesons Poort within **~109,000–~52,000 years ago** ([Wurz 2013](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/673283)).

**B. Last appearance / end:** **c. 59,500 years ago** on the single-grain OSL chronology ([Wurz 2013](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/673283)). Klasies River gives ESR and TL dates of **50,000–60,000 years ago** ([Wurz 2013](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/673283)). At Border Cave the Howiesons Poort layers are dated to around **75,000 years ago** ([Wurz 2013](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/673283)).

**C. Uncertainty bounds:** Narrow OSL view: **~64,800–59,500 years ago** ([Wurz 2013](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/673283)). Wide TL view: **~109,000–52,000 years ago** ([Wurz 2013](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/673283)). The OSL study concluded that both the Howiesons Poort and the Still Bay were **short-lived, 5,000 years or less each, separated by about 7,000 years**, coeval with genetic estimates of population expansion ([Jacobs et al. 2008, Science — PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18974351/)).

**D. Dating method(s):** Methods disagree, and each answer is reported with its method:
- **Single-grain OSL** across eight/nine southern African sites → **64,800–59,500 years ago** ([Wurz 2013](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/673283); [Jacobs et al. 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18974351/)).
- **ESR and TL** at Klasies River → **50,000–60,000 years ago** ([Wurz 2013](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/673283)).
- **TL** at Diepkloof → **~109,000–52,000 years ago** ([Wurz 2013](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/673283)).

**E. Geographic range:** Southern Africa, across multiple ecological zones of South Africa and Lesotho ([Wurz 2013](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/673283)).

**F. Type site / key site:** **Howiesons Poort, South Africa** — the name site ([Wurz 2013](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/673283)). Other key sites: Boomplaas, Border Cave, Diepkloof, Klasies River, Klein Kliphuis, Rose Cottage Cave, Sibudu, Umhlatuzana, Melikane, Ntloana Tsoana and Apollo 11 ([Wurz 2013](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/673283)).

**G. Dispute status:** **method conflict.** Single-grain OSL makes the Howiesons Poort a short, tightly dated horizon marker at **64,800–59,500 years ago**; TL at Diepkloof would make it span **~109,000–52,000 years ago** and therefore not a horizon marker at all. The review states explicitly that whether the Diepkloof TL results demonstrate this **remains open to debate**, and separately that the single-grain OSL dates for the early Howiesons Poort — previously interpreted as Still Bay — are argued to be erroneous because of methodological problems ([Wurz 2013](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/673283)). No taxon dispute applies.
- **Widely revised date within the last 15 years:** yes, and in both directions. The 2008 single-grain OSL programme compressed the industry into **~5,000 years** at **64.8–59.5 ka** ([Jacobs et al. 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18974351/)); the subsequent Diepkloof TL work reopened the question with a span extending to **~109 ka** ([Wurz 2013](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/673283)). The cause is a genuine disagreement between two luminescence techniques applied to different materials at different sites.

**H. One-sentence summary:** A South African toolkit of small backed blades, dated by one method to about 65,000–60,000 years ago and by another to a much longer span.

## 23. Still Bay

**A. First appearance / start:** **75,500 years ago** on the OSL chronology ([Wurz 2013, *Current Anthropology*](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/673283)). A conflicting thermoluminescence result at Diepkloof gives a **mean age of 109,000 years ago** ([Wurz 2013](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/673283)).

**B. Last appearance / end:** **67,800 years ago** on the OSL chronology ([Wurz 2013](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/673283)).

**C. Uncertainty bounds:** OSL: **75,500–67,800 years ago**, a duration of about **7,700 years**, with the industry spanning the end of MIS 5 and the beginning of MIS 4, i.e. **72–60 ka** on the broad view ([Wurz 2013](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/673283)). Preliminary OSL dates of **~72,000 and ~80,000 BP** have also been reported ([Wurz 2013](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/673283)). TL at Diepkloof pushes the old bound to **~109,000 years ago**, which the review says implies a much longer Still Bay duration than previously thought ([Wurz 2013](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/673283)). The 2008 OSL study characterised the Still Bay as **short-lived, 5,000 years or less**, and separated from the Howiesons Poort by **about 7,000 years** ([Jacobs et al. 2008, Science — PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18974351/)).

**D. Dating method(s):** Methods disagree, reported separately:
- **OSL** at Blombos Cave, Sibudu, Apollo 11 and Diepkloof → **75,500–67,800 years ago** ([Wurz 2013](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/673283)).
- **TL** at Diepkloof → **mean 109,000 years ago** ([Wurz 2013](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/673283)).

**E. Geographic range:** Southern Africa — recognised across various ecological zones of South Africa ([Wurz 2013](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/673283)).

**F. Type site / key site:** Type site **n.a.** (the eponymous locality is not named in the fetched sources). Key OSL-dated sites: **Blombos Cave, Sibudu, Apollo 11 and Diepkloof, South Africa** ([Wurz 2013](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/673283)).

**G. Dispute status:** **method conflict.** OSL makes the Still Bay a brief episode at **75.5–67.8 ka**; TL at Diepkloof puts it at **~109 ka**, roughly 35,000 years older and implying a far longer duration ([Wurz 2013](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/673283)). Some assemblages previously assigned to the Still Bay have been reinterpreted as early Howiesons Poort, and the single-grain OSL ages for those assemblages are argued to be erroneous ([Wurz 2013](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/673283)). No taxon dispute applies.
- **Widely revised date within the last 15 years:** yes. The Diepkloof TL chronology, postdating the 2008 OSL synthesis, raised the Still Bay mean age to **109,000 years** against the OSL figure of **75.5–67.8 ka**, and the review treats the resulting conflict as unresolved ([Wurz 2013](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/673283)).

**H. One-sentence summary:** A South African toolkit of finely worked stone points, dated to about 75,000–68,000 years ago, though some results suggest it is much older.

## 24. Microlithic / Later Stone Age (sub-Saharan Africa) — first appearance

**A. First appearance / start:** **44.2–43.0 ka cal BP** for layer 1WA at Border Cave, with layer 1BS Lower B+C at **43.0–42.5 ka cal BP**; the **44–42 ka cal BP** assemblages are identified as marking the beginning of the Later Stone Age in South Africa ([Villa et al. 2012, PNAS — PDF](http://in-africa.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/12/Villa-et-al-2012-PNAS-Border-Cave-and-the-beginning-of-the-LSA-in-S-Africa.pdf)). A companion study places the emergence of modern hunter-gatherer adaptation at **~44,000 years ago**, with the layer sequence dated to 2WA at 60 ka, 2BS Lower A-B at >49 ka, 2BS UP at 49–45 ka, 1WA at 44–43 ka, 1BS Lower B-C at 43–42 ka and 1BS Lower A at 41–22 ka ([d'Errico et al. 2012, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1204213109)). An independent review reports the Border Cave layers as **unequivocally dated to 44–42 ka cal BP** by multiple AMS determinations, with the underlying post-Howiesons Poort MSA at 60–45 ka ([*South African Journal of Science*, 2012](https://scielo.org.za/scielo.php?script=sci_arttext&pid=S0038-23532012000600003)).

**B. Last appearance / end:** Not applicable in the usual sense — the Later Stone Age continues into the historical period; the Villa study frames its material as directly ancestral to San material culture ([Villa et al. 2012 PDF](http://in-africa.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/12/Villa-et-al-2012-PNAS-Border-Cave-and-the-beginning-of-the-LSA-in-S-Africa.pdf)).

**C. Uncertainty bounds:** Oldest credible start of the *process*: **after 56 ka**, the date of layer 2BS Lower C, when simplification of stone-tool production began ([Villa et al. 2012 PDF](http://in-africa.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/12/Villa-et-al-2012-PNAS-Border-Cave-and-the-beginning-of-the-LSA-in-S-Africa.pdf)). Youngest credible start on the older definition: **~22–20 ka**, because the LSA was defined in 1999 as including all assemblages within the last **20,000 years**, and the earliest technological expression was taken to be the **Robberg Industry at c. 22–21 to 12 ka BP** ([Villa et al. 2012 PDF](http://in-africa.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/12/Villa-et-al-2012-PNAS-Border-Cave-and-the-beginning-of-the-LSA-in-S-Africa.pdf)). Typical MSA assemblages continued until **22,000 years ago**, which prevents a clean MSA–LSA boundary ([Wurz 2013, *Current Anthropology*](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/673283)).

**D. Dating method(s):** **Bayesian analysis of calibrated radiocarbon dates**, including conventional dates from the Pretoria and ANU laboratories on charcoal and **AMS dates from Oxford and Kiel on digging-stick wood, an ostrich-eggshell bead, hafting pitch and beeswax**, which confirmed the association of the organic artefacts with the lithics; plus **electron spin resonance**, which dated layer 2WA to **60 ± 3 ka** ([Villa et al. 2012 PDF](http://in-africa.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/12/Villa-et-al-2012-PNAS-Border-Cave-and-the-beginning-of-the-LSA-in-S-Africa.pdf)).

**E. Geographic range:** Sub-Saharan Africa; the earliest securely dated occurrence is in KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa, and is described as the only occurrence of its kind in South Africa at that date ([Villa et al. 2012 PDF](http://in-africa.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/12/Villa-et-al-2012-PNAS-Border-Cave-and-the-beginning-of-the-LSA-in-S-Africa.pdf); [Wurz 2013](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/673283)).

**F. Type site / key site:** **Border Cave, KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa** ([Villa et al. 2012 PDF](http://in-africa.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/12/Villa-et-al-2012-PNAS-Border-Cave-and-the-beginning-of-the-LSA-in-S-Africa.pdf)).

**G. Dispute status:** **definitional**, with an important recent revision.
- **Widely revised date within the last 15 years — a flagship case.** Old value: the LSA was defined in 1999 as everything within the last **20,000 years**, with the Robberg Industry at **c. 22–21 ka BP** as its earliest expression. New value: the beginning of the LSA at **44–42 ka cal BP** at Border Cave. Approximate year of revision: **2012**. Cause: Bayesian reappraisal of the radiocarbon sequence combined with direct AMS dating of organic artefacts (digging-stick wood, ostrich-eggshell bead, hafting pitch, beeswax) and a full technological reanalysis showing bipolar knapping, hafted microliths, bone arrowheads and bored stones ([Villa et al. 2012 PDF](http://in-africa.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/12/Villa-et-al-2012-PNAS-Border-Cave-and-the-beginning-of-the-LSA-in-S-Africa.pdf); [d'Errico et al. 2012, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1204213109)).
- The dispute is definitional: the LSA is a culture-stratigraphic unit whose boundary depends on which traits are treated as diagnostic, and typical MSA assemblages persisted to 22 ka, so a single date for "the start of the LSA" is inherently contestable ([Wurz 2013](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/673283); [Villa et al. 2012 PDF](http://in-africa.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/12/Villa-et-al-2012-PNAS-Border-Cave-and-the-beginning-of-the-LSA-in-S-Africa.pdf)). The Villa study also argues the LSA emerged **by internal evolution**, not by population replacement ([Villa et al. 2012 PDF](http://in-africa.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/12/Villa-et-al-2012-PNAS-Border-Cave-and-the-beginning-of-the-LSA-in-S-Africa.pdf)).

**H. One-sentence summary:** Small hafted stone tools, bows and bone arrowheads appear in South Africa by about 44,000 years ago, far earlier than once thought.
