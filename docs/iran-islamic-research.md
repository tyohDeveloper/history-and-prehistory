# Islamic-Era Iranian Dynasties: 651–1501 Research Dossier

**Purpose:** Fill the 850-year gap in the Iran branch of the historical-period dataset between the fall of the Sasanian Empire (651) and the rise of the Safavid Empire (1501). Covers the Arab conquest, the "Iranian Intermezzo" native dynasties, the Turkic and Mongol imperial periods, and the Turkoman confederations that immediately preceded the Safavids.

**Method note:** Every factual claim below is tied to a fetched/searched source with a full URL. Preferred sources are Encyclopaedia Iranica, the Cambridge History of Iran, and Encyclopaedia Britannica; Wikipedia is used as a supplementary cross-check, especially for infobox-style capital/date data, and is flagged as such. Disputed points are called out explicitly. See "What I could not verify" at the end.

## 1. Arab Conquest of Iran / End of the Sasanian Empire (633–651)

- **Start:** 633 (first Arab-Muslim raids into Sasanian Mesopotamia/Iran); **decisive collapse:** Battle of Qadisiyyah (636) and Battle of Nahavand (642); **end:** 651.
- **Founding/ending event:** The conquest is a process, not a single founding act. The accepted end marker is the death of **Yazdegerd III**, the last Sasanian King of Kings (r. 632–651), who was murdered near Merv in 651, reportedly by a local miller, while fleeing the Arab advance. His death is treated by all three tiers of sources as the definitive end of the 424-year Sasanian Empire (224–651).
- The **Battle of Nahavand (642)** — sometimes called the "Victory of Victories" (Fath al-Futuh) in Arabic sources — is the decisive military engagement after which organized Sasanian state resistance collapsed and the conquest of the Iranian plateau became largely a mopping-up operation over the following decade.
- Full pacification of the plateau (particularly Khorasan and the northeast) took until roughly 651–654, and pockets of Zoroastrian/Sasanian-aligned resistance (e.g., in Tabaristan) persisted much later, but 651 is the standard end date for "the Sasanian Empire" as a political entity.
- **Capital:** Ctesiphon (Sasanian capital, fell to Arab forces in 637).
- **Name:** Sasanian Empire — Middle Persian: 𐭠𐭩𐭥𐭠𐭭𐭱𐭲𐭥𐭩 (Ērānšahr, "Empire of the Iranians"); the dynasty name in Persian is ساسانیان (Sāsāniyān).
- **Sources:**
  - [Britannica — Yazdegerd III](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Yazdegerd-III)
  - [Livius.org — Yazdgard III](https://www.livius.org/articles/person/yazdgard-iii/) (states explicitly: "End of reign: 651 (end of the Sasanian Empire)")
  - [Wikipedia — Battle of Nahavand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Nahavand)
  - Cambridge History of Iran, Vol. 4 chapter: [The Arab Conquest of Iran and Its Aftermath](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-history-of-iran/arab-conquest-of-iran-and-its-aftermath/6AB6C254CCD1E6D299449CD836500498)

## 2. Tahirid Dynasty (821–873)

- **Start:** 821, when the caliph al-Ma'mun appointed **Tahir ibn al-Husayn** (a general of Iranian origin, descended from the *mawali* of an Arab leader in eastern Khorasan) as governor of Khorasan, effectively founding a hereditary line of governors nominally loyal to Baghdad.
- **End:** 873, when **Ya'qub ibn al-Layth al-Saffar** (founder of the Saffarids) captured Nishapur and ousted the last Tahirid governor; the family then retired to Baghdad, where they retained lesser status.
- **Capital:** Nishapur (نیشابور), made Tahir's capital following his appointment.
- **Name:** Persian: طاهریان (Tāheriyān) / سلسله طاهریان; the family were nominal Abbasid governors, not a fully independent dynasty, but are treated as the first native Iranian Islamic dynasty of the Intermezzo era.
- **Sources:**
  - [Encyclopaedia Iranica — TAHERIDS](https://iranicaonline.org/articles/taherid-dynasty)
  - [Britannica — Iran: The Iranian Intermezzo, 821–1055](https://www.britannica.com/place/Iran/The-Iranian-intermezzo-821-1055) (confirms founding in 821, capital Neyshabur, ouster by Ya'qub in 873, retirement to Baghdad)
  - [Cambridge History of Iran — The Tahirids and Saffarids](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-history-of-iran/tahirids-and-saffarids/783E1CE86EF98641A309766547250374)

## 3. Saffarid Dynasty (861–1003)

- **Start:** 861 (some sources give 867), founded by **Ya'qub ibn al-Layth al-Saffar** ("the coppersmith"), a military commander who built an army through freebooting/conquest in Sistan and expanded across the Iranian plateau, seizing Fars and Khuzestan and driving the Tahirids from Nishapur in 873.
- **Ending event:** Political power of the main line ended in 1002/1003 (sources split on the exact year) when the **Ghaznavids** under Mahmud of Ghazna defeated the last independent Saffarid ruler, Khalaf ibn Ahmad, capturing his fort on 15 October 1003; Khalaf reportedly died in 1009 at Gardez. A minor, much-reduced Saffarid line survived as vassals in Sistan for centuries afterward (see dispute note below).
- **Capital:** Zaranj (in modern Sistan, Afghanistan).
- **Name:** Persian: صفاریان (Saffāriyān) / سلسله صفاریان. Ya'qub's own preference for having verses of praise rendered in Persian rather than Arabic (he reportedly did not understand Arabic) is cited by Britannica as an early marker of the Persian cultural revival of this period.
- **Territorial note:** At their height the Saffarids held much of the Iranian plateau (Fars, Khuzestan, parts of Khorasan) plus Sistan and eastern territories now in Afghanistan/Pakistan.
- **Dispute to document:** Encyclopedia.com/Facts and Details material states nominal nominal Saffarid governors persisted in Sistan under Samanid/later suzerainty until as late as the 15th–16th century, but this refers to a much-diminished local vassal line, not the imperial-era dynasty; the political/military power that "counts" as the Saffarid Empire ended in 1002/1003.
- **Sources:**
  - [Britannica — Iran: The Iranian Intermezzo, 821–1055](https://www.britannica.com/place/Iran/The-Iranian-intermezzo-821-1055) (detailed narrative of Ya'qub's founding, expansion, death in 879, and 'Amr's later reign and 902 execution)
  - [Wikipedia — Saffarid dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saffarid_dynasty)
  - [Encyclopaedia Iranica — YA'QUB b. LAYTH](https://iranicaonline.org/articles/yaqub-b-lay-b-moaddal)

## 4. Samanid Empire (819–999) — Central Asia or Iran?

- **Start:** 819 (investiture of Saman-Khoda's four grandsons by caliph al-Ma'mun over areas including Samarkand and Herat); some sources cite 875 for when Samanid rule became effectively unified/independent.
- **End:** 999, when Bukhara fell to combined Ghaznavid and Qarakhanid forces.
- **Capital:** Samarkand (819–892), then Bukhara (892–999) — both in present-day Uzbekistan, i.e., Transoxania/Central Asia proper.
- **Name:** Persian: سامانیان (Sāmāniyān) / سلسلهٔ سامانیان. Claimed descent from the Sasanian general Bahram Chubin.
- **VERDICT — does it belong under Iran? YES, it should be cross-listed.** The Samanids' core administrative territory was Transoxania and eastern Khorasan, but their realm substantially incorporated Iranian-plateau territory:
  - Britannica's Iranian Intermezzo article lists their territory as including Samarkand, **Herat**, Bukhara, and Khujand, with eastern Khorasan under their control.
  - Independent cross-check (Archnet, Wikipedia) extends this at their height to **Ray** (near Tehran), **Tabaristan**, **Gorgan** (Caspian Iran), **Kerman**, and west to **Isfahan** — all squarely within the Iranian plateau, not Central Asia.
  - Encyclopaedia Iranica and the Oxford Handbook of Iranian History both classify the Samanids as one of the core "Iranian Intermezzo" dynasties alongside the Tahirids, Saffarids, and Buyids — a periodization explicitly about *Iranian* history, not Central Asian history.
  - The [Oxford Handbook of Iranian History treatment](https://academic.oup.com/reference/62384/reference-article-abstract/555074455?redirectedFrom=fulltext) explicitly frames the Samanids (819–999) as part of the "Persianate Dynastic Period" spanning Iraq to Central Asia, underscoring that the Iran/Central Asia line is a modern administrative convenience, not a period-appropriate boundary.
  - **Recommendation:** List Samanids under both Central Asia (capital location) and Iran (substantial territorial control of Khorasan, Ray, Tabaristan, Gorgan, Kerman, Isfahan; core participant in the "Iranian Intermezzo").
- **Sources:**
  - [Britannica — Iran: The Iranian Intermezzo, 821–1055](https://www.britannica.com/place/Iran/The-Iranian-intermezzo-821-1055)
  - [Britannica — Samanid dynasty](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Samanid-dynasty)
  - [Wikipedia — Samanid Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samanid_Empire)
  - [Archnet — Samanid timeline](https://www.archnet.org/collections/2371)
  - [Oxford Handbook of Iranian History — Persianate Dynastic Period](https://academic.oup.com/reference/62384/reference-article-abstract/555074455?redirectedFrom=fulltext)

## 5. Buyid / Buwayhid Dynasty (934–1062)

- **Start:** 934, when **Ali ibn Buya** (Imad al-Dawla), eldest of three Daylamite (northern Iranian, from Daylaman in Gilan) brothers who had served the Ziyarid ruler Mardavij, conquered Fars and established himself at Shiraz.
- **Key founding/expansion event:** In December 945, the youngest brother, **Ahmad ibn Buya (Mu'izz al-Dawla)**, occupied **Baghdad** and was given the title *amir al-umara* ("commander of commanders"), reducing the Abbasid caliphs to figurehead status under Buyid military control — this is the event that made the Buyids the de facto rulers behind the Abbasid caliphate.
- **End:** Baghdad fell to the Seljuk **Tughril Beg** in 1055, ending the Iraq branch; the last Buyid ruler (Abu Mansur Fulad Sutun, Jibal/Fars branch) was deposed by 1062.
- **Capitals:** Multiple regional branches — Shiraz (Fars branch, 934–1062), Ray (Jibal branch, 943–1029), Baghdad (Iraq branch, 945–1055).
- **Religion:** Shia (initially Zaydi, with Twelver Shia elements later); notable as the dynasty that "controlled Baghdad and the Abbasid caliphs" per the task brief.
- **Name:** Persian: آل بویه (Āl-e Buye); Arabic: بنو بویه / البویهیون.
- **Sources:**
  - [Encyclopaedia Iranica — BUYIDS](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/buyids/)
  - [Britannica — Buyid dynasty](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Buyid-dynasty)
  - [Britannica — Iran: The Iranian Intermezzo, 821–1055](https://www.britannica.com/place/Iran/The-Iranian-intermezzo-821-1055) (situates Buyids as the culmination of the Intermezzo)

## 6. Ghaznavid Empire (977–1186) — How Much of Iran Did It Hold?

- **Start:** 977, founded by **Sabuktigin**, a Turkic ex-slave-general in Samanid service, at Ghazni.
- **End:** 1186, when the Ghurids captured Lahore, the last Ghaznavid stronghold.
- **Capitals:** Ghazni (977–1163), later Lahore (1163–1186) — both outside the Iranian plateau proper (modern Afghanistan and Pakistan).
- **VERDICT — partial, time-limited YES.** Under **Mahmud of Ghazni** (r. 998–1030) the empire held **Khorasan** (northeastern Iran, taken from the Samanids) and briefly captured **Ray** and **Hamadan** in western Iran from the Buyids — genuine control of substantial Iranian territory.
  - This control ended decisively at the **Battle of Dandanaqan (1040)**, where the Seljuks defeated Sultan Mas'ud I; Khorasan was annexed by the Seljuks, and the Ghaznavids were permanently confined to Afghanistan, Punjab, and northern India for the remainder of their existence (1040–1186).
  - **Net assessment:** Ghaznavid rule over Iranian territory was real but limited to roughly 977–1040 (about 63 of the dynasty's 209 years), concentrated in Khorasan with a brief extension into western Iran. It should be cross-listed under Iran but with an explicit caveat that this was an early, time-limited phase, not a defining or lasting feature of the empire.
- **Name:** Persian: غزنویان (Ghaznaviyān).
- **Sources:**
  - [Britannica — Ghaznavid dynasty](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Ghaznavid-dynasty)
  - [Wikipedia — Ghaznavid Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghaznavid_Empire)
  - [Britannica — Battle of Dandanqan](https://www.britannica.com/event/Battle-of-Dandanqan)
  - [Wikipedia — Battle of Dandanaqan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Dandanaqan)

## 7. Great Seljuk Empire (1037–1194) — Iran Question

- **Start:** 1037, founded by **Tughril Beg** and his brother Chaghri Beg (Oghuz Turkic leaders); Tughril was proclaimed sultan at Nishapur in 1038.
- **Key event:** Tughril captured Baghdad in 1055, ending Buyid control of the Abbasid caliphate.
- **End:** 1194, when Tughril III — the last Seljuk sultan ruling from Iran — was killed in battle by the Khwarazmshah Tekish, who annexed western Persia.
- **Capitals — ALL located in Iran:** Nishapur (1037–1043) → Ray (1043–1051) → Isfahan (1051–1118) → then split into Merv (eastern branch, 1118–1153) and Hamadan (western branch, 1118–1194).
- **VERDICT — unambiguously YES, an Iran-centered empire.** Every one of the Great Seljuk Empire's successive capitals was on Iranian soil; describing it purely as a "Central Asia" entity understates its character. It should be cross-listed under Iran, arguably as a primary rather than secondary region.
- **Name:** Persian: سلجوقیان (Saljuqiyān).
- **Sources:**
  - [Britannica — Seljuq](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Seljuq)
  - [Britannica — Toghril Beg](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Toghril-Beg)
  - [Wikipedia — Seljuk Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seljuk_Empire)

## 8. Khwarazmian Empire (1077–1231) — Iran Question

- **Start:** 1077, founded by **Anush Tigin Gharchai**, a Turkic mamluk/governor of Khwarazm serving the Seljuks; the dynasty were Seljuk/Qara Khitai vassals initially, becoming independent from roughly 1190.
- **Territorial expansion:** By 1217, Khwarazmian conquests in western Persia extended the empire from the Syr Darya (Central Asia) to the Zagros Mountains — i.e., across the entire Iranian plateau.
- **Capitals:** Gurganj (1077–1212, in Khwarazm, on the modern Turkmenistan/Uzbekistan border), then Samarkand (1212–1220), then Ghazni (1220–1221), and finally **Tabriz (1225–1231)** — the last capital was deep in Iranian Azerbaijan.
- **End:** 1231, when the last ruler, **Jalal al-Din Mingburnu**, was assassinated by Kurdish bandits near Mayyafariqin, after the Mongol invasion (1219–1221) had already destroyed the empire's core territory.
- **VERDICT — YES, especially in the final phase.** The Khwarazmian Empire held substantial and, by its final years, central Iranian territory — its last capital (Tabriz) and much of its late territory (western Persia to the Zagros) were core Iranian lands. It should be cross-listed under Iran, with a note that its origin and early base (Khwarazm, Transoxania) were Central Asian.
- **Name:** Persian: خوارزمشاهیان (Khwārazmshāhiyān).
- **Sources:**
  - [Wikipedia — Khwarazmian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khwarazmian_Empire)
  - [Britannica — Jalal al-Din Mingburnu](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Jalal-al-Din-Mingburnu)
  - [Wikipedia — Anushtegin dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khwarazmian_dynasty)

## 9. Ilkhanate (1256–1335) — Confirmed Centered on Iran

- **Start:** 1256, founded by **Hulagu Khan** (grandson of Genghis Khan); captured Baghdad in 1258, ending the Abbasid Caliphate as a political entity, and completed the conquest of Iran.
- **End:** 1335, with the death of **Abu Sa'id Bahadur Khan** (r. 1316–1335) without an heir; the state disintegrated into successor states (Jalayirids, Chobanids, Muzaffarids, Sarbadars).
- **Capitals — all in Iran:** Maragheh (1256–1265), then Tabriz (1265–1306/1307), then Soltaniyeh (1306–1335).
- **VERDICT — CONFIRMED, unambiguously centered on Iran.** All capitals were Iranian cities; the Ilkhanate is widely treated by scholarship as reconstituting a unified Iranian state after the Mongol conquest. One academic source (Oxford Academic) explicitly frames it as producing "a Persian renaissance" and reestablishing Iranians as key regional players.
- **Name:** Persian: ایلخانان (Ilkhānān) / سلسله ایلخانی.
- **Sources:**
  - [Britannica — Il-Khanid dynasty](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Il-Khanid-dynasty)
  - [Wikipedia — Ilkhanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ilkhanate)
  - [Oxford Academic — The Ilkhanate: Mongol Rule in Medieval Western Asia, 1256–1335](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61799/chapter/546313656)

## 10. Timurid Empire (1370–1507) — Qualified/Partial Iranian Control

- **Start:** 1370, founded by **Timur (Tamerlane)**, capital Samarkand.
- **Capital shift:** Herat became the primary Timurid administrative and cultural capital from 1405, under Timur's son **Shah Rokh** (r. 1405–1447). Herat is in modern Afghanistan, historically part of Khorasan.
- **VERDICT — partial/qualified YES**, best documented by Britannica's own nuanced framing:
  > "The nearest a Timurid state came to being an integrated Iranian empire was under Timur's son Shah Rokh (reigned 1405–47), who endeavored to weld Azerbaijan and western Persia to Khorasan and eastern Persia to form a united Timurid state... He succeeded only in loosely controlling western and southern Iran from his beautiful capital at Herat." — [Britannica, Iran: The Timurids and Turkmen](https://www.britannica.com/place/Iran/The-Timurids-and-Turkmen)
  - This indicates Timurid authority over Iran proper was **loose and contested**, at its strongest under Shah Rokh (1405–1447), and that western Iran was frequently under the rival Turkoman confederations — the **Qara Qoyunlu** and later **Aq Qoyunlu** (see items 11 below) — for large parts of the Timurid period, rather than under direct Timurid rule.
  - By contrast, the Cambridge History of Iran (Vol. 6) states flatly that "the period of Timurid government in Iran extends from 807/1405 to 913/1507," treating 1405–1507 as the Timurid era over Iran in a general historiographical sense, while acknowledging shared/contested control with the Turkoman states in the west.
- **End:** 1507, when the Uzbek **Muhammad Shaybani Khan** captured Herat, the last Timurid stronghold; the dynasty's Indian branch, founded by **Babur** in 1526, became the Mughal Empire.
- **Name:** Persian: تیموریان (Timūriyān); self-designation: گورکانیان (Gūrkāniyān, "sons-in-law," referencing marriage ties to Genghisid lines).
- **Recommendation:** Cross-list under Iran with an explicit caveat — Timurid control of Iran proper was partial, contested with the Qara Qoyunlu/Aq Qoyunlu, and strongest (though still "loose" per Britannica) under Shah Rokh from the Herat capital.
- **Sources:**
  - [Britannica — Iran: The Timurids and Turkmen](https://www.britannica.com/place/Iran/The-Timurids-and-Turkmen) (critical nuanced source)
  - [Wikipedia — Timurid Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timurid_Empire)
  - [Britannica — Timurid dynasty](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Timurid-dynasty)
  - Cambridge History of Iran, Vol. 6 (Jackson & Lockhart, *The Timurid and Safavid Periods*), excerpted via [Scribd copy](https://www.scribd.com/document/781626906/the-Cambridge-History-of-Iran-6-Peter-Jackson-Lawrence-Lockhart-The-Cambridge-History-of-Iran-Volume-6-the-Timurid-and-Safavid-Periods-Cambridg) — states Timurid government in Iran ran 1405–1507; also confirms Isma'il's conquest of Tabriz and coronation as shah "in the summer of 906–7/1501."

## 11. Qara Qoyunlu (Black Sheep, c. 1374/1375–1468) and Aq Qoyunlu (White Sheep, 1378–1501/1508)

These two rival Turkoman (Oghuz Turkic, culturally Persianate) tribal confederations directly preceded and were destroyed by the rising Safavids, and together with the Timurids they contested control of western and central Iran through most of the 15th century.

### Qara Qoyunlu ("Black Sheep")

- **Dates:** c. 1374/1375–1468.
- **Founding:** Emerged as a Turkoman confederation in eastern Anatolia/Armenia, initially vassals of the Jalayirids; established Tabriz as their seat by 1375, ruling there under successive leaders including **Qara Yusuf** (1390–1420) and, at their territorial height, **Jahan Shah** (r. 1438–1467).
- **Peak territorial extent under Jahan Shah:** Conquered much of Iran from the Timurids, including Iraq, Fars, and Isfahan (1453); briefly took **Herat** from the Timurid Abu Sa'id in 1458 before withdrawing; per Simple Wikipedia's summary (cross-checked against Britannica's Jahan Shah biography), his territory "included Western Anatolia, most of present-day Iraq, central Iran, and even Kerman."
- **Ending event:** Jahan Shah was defeated and killed by **Uzun Hasan** of the rival Aq Qoyunlu at the Battle of Chapakchur (30 October/11 November 1467) after attempting to seize Diyarbakir from the Aq Qoyunlu; the Qara Qoyunlu state collapsed within about two years, formally ending in 1468 when Jahan Shah's son Hasan Ali was assassinated.
- **Capital:** Tabriz (from c. 1375/1436, sources vary on exact year of primary establishment, until 1467/68); earlier/seasonal bases at Erciş (summer) and Mosul (winter).
- **Religion:** Shia-leaning Turkoman confederation (noted as such in secondary sources; Encyclopaedia Iranica's Aq Qoyunlu article treats the Qara Qoyunlu as the immediate predecessor power at Tabriz).
- **Name:** Persian: قره قویونلو (Qara Qoyunlu, "Black Sheep").
- **Sources:**
  - [Britannica — Kara Koyunlu](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Kara-Koyunlu)
  - [Britannica — Jahan Shah](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Jahan-Shah)
  - [Wikipedia — Qara Qoyunlu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qara_Qoyunlu)
  - [Encyclopaedia Iranica — AQ QOYUNLU (confederation)](https://iranicaonline.org/articles/aq-qoyunlu-confederation) (discusses Qara Qoyunlu as predecessor/rival throughout)

### Aq Qoyunlu ("White Sheep")

- **Dates:** 1378–1501 (collapse of rule in Iran) / 1501–03 (Safavid conquest of core territory per Iranica) / 1508 (final remnants conquered).
- **Founding:** Real founder of the state was **Qara Osman (Qara 'Othman)**, of the Bayandor clan, who consolidated Aq Qoyunlu power in Diyarbakir under nominal Timurid overlordship after 1402; the confederation's capital was Amid/Diyarbakir for nearly 70 years.
- **Peak territorial extent under Uzun Hasan (r. 1452–1478):** After defeating the Qara Qoyunlu in 1467 and capturing the Timurid ruler Abu Sa'id in January 1469, Uzun Hasan "annexed the Iranian provinces up to the borders of Khorasan" and moved the capital from Amid to **Tabriz** — the empire's zenith, controlling Iraq, Azerbaijan, Fars, Kerman, and much of the Iranian plateau, per Encyclopaedia Iranica.
- **Ending event:** The Aq Qoyunlu were defeated by **Ismail Safavi** at the Battle of Sharur (17 July 1501); Ismail took Tabriz in 1501 and proclaimed himself Shahanshah of Iran, founding the Safavid state. The Safavid conquest of remaining Aq Qoyunlu territory (Fars, Persian Iraq) was largely complete by 1503, with the last Aq Qoyunlu sultan, Murad, defeated and killed near Urfa; remaining territory (Diyarbakir, Mesopotamia) fell to the Safavids by 1507–1508.
- **Capitals:** Amid/Diyarbakir (c. 1402–1468), then Tabriz (1468/1469–1501).
- **Name:** Persian: آق قویونلو (Āq Qoyunlū, "White Sheep"); ruling clan: Bayandor (بایندر).
- **Direct link to Safavid founding:** This is the dynasty whose capital (Tabriz) and territory were directly and immediately absorbed into the new Safavid state in 1501 — making the Aq Qoyunlu the true immediate predecessor polity in Iran proper before the Safavids.
- **Sources:**
  - [Encyclopaedia Iranica — AQ QOYUNLU (confederation)](https://iranicaonline.org/articles/aq-qoyunlu-confederation) (primary, most detailed source; gives full chronology of founding, territorial expansion, capitals, and end)
  - [Britannica — Safavid dynasty](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Safavid-dynasty) (confirms Ismail's capture of Tabriz from the Ak Koyunlu and 1501 enthronement)
  - [Wikipedia — Campaigns of Ismail I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Campaigns_of_Ismail_I) (Battle of Sharur, 17 July 1501; Safavid conquest of Fars and Persian Iraq by 1503; Baghdad by 1508)

## 12. Scholarly Framing of 651–1501 and the "Iranian Intermezzo"

- **Is there a standard name for the whole 651–1501 span?** There is no single universally standardized name for the *entire* 850-year period as one bloc. Scholarship instead breaks it into recognized sub-periods:
  1. **The Arab conquest and early Islamic period** (633–821) — sometimes called early Islamic Iran.
  2. **The "Iranian Intermezzo"** (821–1055, per Britannica's own section title) or "Persian Renaissance" — covering the native Iranian Muslim dynasties (see below).
  3. **The Seljuk/Turkic and Mongol periods** (1037–1335) — sometimes grouped as the era of "Turko-Mongol rule."
  4. **The post-Ilkhanid/Timurid-Turkoman period** (1335–1501) — sometimes called the "Timurid and Turkmen" era (Britannica's own section title) or, more broadly, part of "Medieval Islamic Iran."
  - The broad label **"Islamic Iran"** or **"medieval Iran"** is used informally and in course/reference titles (e.g., Wikipedia's "History of Islamic Iran" article) to describe the whole span from 651 to 1501/1502, but this is a convenience label rather than a single scholarly period-name with fixed boundaries. [Wikipedia — History of Islamic Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Islamic_Iran)
  - The academic monograph *Social Change in Medieval Iran, 132–628 AH (750–1231 AD)* explicitly periodizes the medieval span into three phases: (1) the Abbasid Caliphate's rise, (2) "the first Iranian local dynasties" (Tahirids, Samanids, Saffarids, Buyids), and (3) "the rise of the sultanates" (Ghaznavids, Seljuks, Khwarazmshahs) — a useful scholarly three-part model for organizing this era. [dokumen.pub — Social Change in Medieval Iran](https://dokumen.pub/social-change-in-medieval-iran-132-628-ah-750-1231-ad-the-perspectives-of-persian-historiography-9789048556304.html)

- **What is the "Iranian Intermezzo"?**
  - Definition (Wikipedia, cross-checked against Britannica's section title and content): "The Iranian Intermezzo, also called the Persian Renaissance, was a period in Iranian history marked by the rise to power of the first Iranian Muslim dynasties... an interlude between the decay of Arab power under the Abbasid Caliphate and the rise of Turkic power under the Seljuk Empire." [Wikipedia — Iranian Intermezzo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_Intermezzo)
  - **Britannica uses this exact term as an official section title**: "The 'Iranian intermezzo' (821–1055)" in its Iran country-history article, covering the Tahirids, Saffarids, Samanids, and the "Iranian renaissance" culminating in the Buyids. This is strong confirmation the term (or at least the 821–1055 periodization it names) has mainstream encyclopedic acceptance, not just Wikipedia currency. [Britannica — Iran: The Iranian intermezzo, 821–1055](https://www.britannica.com/place/Iran/The-Iranian-intermezzo-821-1055)
  - **Date range:** Most sources give **821–1055** (Tahir's appointment to the Seljuk capture of Baghdad from the Buyids). Wikipedia's "List of monarchs of Iran" article gives a broader **821–1090/1091** range in one place (extending to the end of the Ziyarids), showing some elasticity in the endpoint depending on which minor dynasty is used to close the period.
  - **Which dynasties does it cover?** Sources agree on a core list, with minor variation on inclusion of borderline/minor houses:
    - Consistently included: **Tahirids** (821–873), **Saffarids** (861–1003), **Samanids** (819–999), **Buyids** (934–1062).
    - Also included by Wikipedia's full list: Ziyarids, Sajids, Sallarids/Musafirids, Rawadids, Marwanids, Shaddadids, Kakuyids, Annazids, Hasanwayhids (mostly minor regional Iranian/Kurdish dynasties in the Caucasus and western Iran).
    - Per Wikipedia, citing two named modern scholars — **Daryaee (2012)** and **Mahendrarajah (2019)** — the standard *major*-dynasty list is: **Tahirids, Saffarids, Ziyarids, Buyids, and Samanids**; Daryaee additionally includes the **Ghaznavids**, while Mahendrarajah omits them. [Wikipedia — List of monarchs of Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_monarchs_of_Iran)
    - This directly supports the treatment above: the Ghaznavids' inclusion in "Iranian" historiography is itself a live scholarly point of disagreement, consistent with the item 6 finding that their hold on Iranian territory was real but partial/time-limited.
  - **What ends it?** The Seljuk conquest of Baghdad in 1055 (ending Buyid dominance) and the wider "Sunni Revival" associated with Seljuk rule are cited as marking the close of the Intermezzo.

## What I Could Not Verify

- **Persian-script names for several dynasties could not be independently confirmed on Encyclopaedia Iranica pages fetched in this session** — for the Aq Qoyunlu and the Iranian Intermezzo (Tahirid/Saffarid/Samanid) Britannica page, the fetched Iranica/Britannica page text rendered names only in scholarly Latin transliteration (e.g., Āq Qoyunlū, Ṭāhirids, Ṣaffārids), not in native Persian or Arabic script. Persian-script forms given in this document for those cases (e.g., آق قویونلو) are drawn from Wikipedia infoboxes, not from Iranica/Britannica directly, and should be treated as secondary-source confirmations rather than primary-source-verified.
- **Exact Saffarid end date (1002 vs. 1003)** is genuinely disputed across otherwise reliable sources (Wikipedia/Britannica narrative implies late 1002/1003; the Ghaznavid-Saffarid war article cites 15 October 1003 for the fort's capture). Both years appear in circulation; I could not find an Iranica or Cambridge History source that resolves this to a single definitive date within this research session.
- **Exact founding date for the Qara Qoyunlu** varies by source: 1374 (Wikipedia infobox), c. 1375 (Britannica, "Kara Koyunlu"), and the Encyclopaedia Iranica Aq Qoyunlu article does not give a precise Qara Qoyunlu founding date, only describing them as an established rival power by the mid-14th century. I did not locate a dedicated Encyclopaedia Iranica article specifically titled "Qara Qoyunlu" in this session (only indirect coverage within the Aq Qoyunlu and Esmail I Safavi Iranica articles) — a direct Iranica Qara Qoyunlu article may exist but was not surfaced or fetched.
- **A single, universally standardized scholarly name for the entire 651–1501 span** does not appear to exist as a fixed academic term (unlike "Iranian Intermezzo," which is well-attested as a name for the 821–1055 sub-period). "Islamic Iran" and "medieval Iran" are used as informal/organizational labels (e.g., in Wikipedia article titles) rather than as terms with a citable formal definition in Britannica or Iranica covering the full 850-year range as one unit. If the dataset needs a single label for the full span, this should be treated as an editorial choice rather than a directly sourced scholarly consensus term.
- **Precise territorial-control percentages or map-based quantification** ("how much of Iran" in area or population terms) were not available for the Ghaznavids, Timurids, or Khwarazmians from the sources consulted; the assessments above are qualitative (based on named cities/regions held) rather than quantitative, because no source in this session provided a quantified territorial metric.
- **The claimed Ilkhanate self-designation "Iranzamin" (ایران‌زمین)** was noted from an initial pass but not independently re-confirmed against a dedicated Encyclopaedia Iranica or Britannica passage in this session; it should be treated as unverified pending a direct Iranica citation and is not used as a load-bearing claim above.
