# Mediterranean Chronology Verification Brief
### Greece & Rome — 17 Entities: Sourcing, Convention-Type, and Contested-Date Flags

**Purpose:** This is a *verification* brief, not a discovery brief. For each of the 17 entities below, the currently displayed date range is checked against fetched primary/academic/institutional sources. Wikipedia is used only as a **locator** (to find where a fact might be documented) and is never cited as a dating authority — it is explicitly labeled "locator only" wherever it appears. Where a date cannot be confirmed by a citable authority, it is marked `n.a.` with an explanation rather than papered over.

**Format per entity:** Entity name → claimed date → what kind of date it is → is it contested? → sources (with full URLs) → flags.

**Total distinct fetched URLs cited below:** 70+ (see full source list is inline throughout; a consolidated bibliography is not repeated separately to avoid duplicating link text — every URL below was fetched or returned by a live search during this research session).

---

## 1. Greece (overall): 3000 BCE – 146 BCE

**Kind of date:** Modern scholarly periodization convention (start) + a genuine, well-attested political event (end). Not a single "moment" for either end, but the end is far better anchored than the start.

**Contested?** The start is not "contested" in the sense of rival camps arguing a specific year — rather, **"3000 BCE" is an imprecise rounding** of a chronological convention (the start of the Aegean Bronze Age / Early Helladic period) whose more precise scholarly value is usually given as **c. 3200 BCE**.

**Sources:**
- British Museum, official gallery/visitor guide: *"Ancient Greece: Minoans and Mycenaeans, 3200–1100 BC. The Greek Bronze Age lasted from about 3200 to 1100 BC."* — [British Museum Visitor Journey large-print guide (PDF)](https://www.britishmuseum.org/sites/default/files/2020-11/Visitor_Journey_large_print_guide_British_Museum.pdf)
- British Museum, Department of Greece and Rome overview: collection "range[s] in date from the beginning of the Greek Bronze Age (about 3200 BC)..." — [British Museum: Greece and Rome department](https://www.britishmuseum.org/our-work/departments/greece-and-rome)
- Smarthistory (Oxford/Khan Academy-affiliated art-history reference): *"During the Bronze Age (around 3200–1100 B.C.E.), a number of cultures flourished..."* — [Smarthistory: Ancient Greece, an introduction](https://smarthistory.org/ancient-greece-an-introduction/)
- Cambridge University Press academic excerpt: Bronze Age in Greece "started around 3200 BC" — [Cambridge excerpt (PDF)](https://assets.cambridge.org/97810094/93123/excerpt/9781009493123_excerpt.pdf)
- World History Encyclopedia, Bronze Age Aegean timeline: Early Bronze Age c. 3000–2200 BCE — [World History Encyclopedia: Timeline, Bronze Age Aegean](https://www.worldhistory.org/timeline/Bronze_Age_Aegean/)
- End date (146 BCE, sack of Corinth): see full sourcing under Entity 8 (Hellenistic period) below.
- Locator only (not a dating authority): [Wikipedia, Helladic chronology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helladic_chronology)

**Flags:**
- ⚑ **"3000 BCE" is a rounded, imprecise version of a periodization convention.** The two major museum-collection sources fetched (British Museum) and academic press sources (Cambridge) converge on **c. 3200 BCE**, not 3000 BCE, as the start of the Greek Bronze Age. This is a ~200-year discrepancy that should be corrected or explicitly labeled as "rounded convention, precise scholarly value ~3200 BCE."
- The overall "Greece" entity conflates a geological/archaeological period boundary (Bronze Age start) with a political event (Roman conquest) as its two endpoints — these are different *kinds* of dates and should not be presented with equal certainty.

## 2. Mycenaean Greece: 1600 – 1100 BCE

**Kind of date:** Archaeological periodization convention for the "start," itself entangled with an **unresolved absolute-chronology dispute** (see Item (c), Thera, below). The "1100 BCE" end is also conventional (collapse of Mycenaean palatial centers, part of the broader Late Bronze Age Collapse) rather than a single attested event.

**Contested?** **Yes — significantly**, both directly (a range of 1750/1700/1675/1600 BCE start dates appear across scholarship) and indirectly (the entire Aegean absolute chronology this date sits within is disputed via the Thera eruption problem — treated at length in section (c) below).

**Sources:**
- British Museum (museum-collection primary source): *"the Mycenaean period (1600–1100 BC)"* — [British Museum Visitor Journey guide (PDF)](https://www.britishmuseum.org/sites/default/files/2020-11/Visitor_Journey_large_print_guide_British_Museum.pdf)
- British Museum gallery page: [Greece: Minoans and Mycenaeans](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/galleries/greece-minoans-and-mycenaeans)
- Smarthistory: *"Mycenaean culture flourished on the Greek mainland in the Late Bronze Age, from about 1600 to 1100 B.C.E."* — [Smarthistory: Ancient Greece, an introduction](https://smarthistory.org/ancient-greece-an-introduction/)
- Leiden University PhD dissertation: *"In absolute chronology, the period begins c. 1600 BCE"* — [Leiden University Scholarly Publications repository](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item:4098120/view)
- Greek Ministry of Culture, Mycenae site page: "early Mycenaean period, c. 1600 BC" — [Odysseus Culture Portal, Mycenae](http://odysseus.culture.gr/h/3/eh351.jsp?obj_id=2573)
- World History Encyclopedia timeline: gives a **wider** 1700–1100 BCE range — [World History Encyclopedia: Timeline, Mycenaean Civilization](https://www.worldhistory.org/timeline/Mycenaean_Civilization/)
- Cambridge University Press academic excerpt citing Shelmerdine 2008: "high chronology" places LH I start at ~1700 BCE — [Cambridge excerpt (PDF)](https://assets.cambridge.org/97811071/07540/excerpt/9781107107540_excerpt.pdf)
- Locator only: [Wikipedia, Mycenaean Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mycenaean_Greece) (cites Alex Knodell 2021 and others giving 1750/1700/1675/1600 BC as competing start dates)

**Flags:**
- ⚑ **The specific year "1600 BCE" is only one of several scholarly conventions** for the Mycenaean start; "high chronology" proponents place it up to 150 years earlier (~1750 BCE). The British Museum's own gallery date (1600–1100 BC) is a defensible institutional convention but should not be presented as the single settled figure.
- This date is directly downstream of the Thera/Aegean chronology dispute — see (c) below for full treatment.

## 3. Macedon: 808 – 146 BCE

**Kind of date:** The start (808 BCE) is a **legendary king-list back-calculation** — structurally the same problem as Rome's 753 BCE (see Item (d) and Entity 5). The end (146 BCE) is a genuine, well-attested political/military event (Roman annexation after the Achaean War).

**Contested?** **Yes, significantly**, and in a way parallel to Rome. Ancient sources disagree on both the founder and the date, and "808 BCE" specifically comes from later systematized king-lists, not from any contemporary record.

**Sources:**
- Britannica, Argead Dynasty: *"ruling house of ancient Macedonia from about 700 to about 311 bc... From about 700 the founder of the dynasty, Perdiccas I, led the people who called themselves Macedonians eastward..."* — note Britannica's own scholarly framing starts the dynasty at **c. 700 BC**, not 808 BC — [Britannica: Argead Dynasty](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Argead-dynasty)
- Tacitus.nu historical atlas (classicist-maintained reference): *"Herodotus claimed that it was Perdikkas I who created the kingdom while Theopompos alleged that this achievement was done earlier by Karanos... All sources from Antiquity are however in agreement that the first Macedonian royal family descended from the Greek city of Argos"* — [Tacitus.nu: Macedonia regents](http://www.tacitus.nu/historical-atlas/regents/balkan/macedonia.htm)
- En Academic / Oxford Companion to Classical Literature (M.C. Howatson, Oxford University Press, 1989, p. 339), quoted secondhand: royal descent traced "more immediately from Perdiccas I, who left Argos for Illyria, probably in the mid-seventh century BC" — [En Academic: Argead dynasty](https://en-academic.com/dic.nsf/enwiki/724016)
- Dokumen.pub hosted academic reference work, *Lexicon of Argead Macedonia*: dates the "mythic foundation of the Argead Empire... roughly to the middle of the 7th c. BC" — [Lexicon of Argead Macedonia](https://dokumen.pub/lexicon-of-argead-macedonia-3732904059-9783732904051-9783732996018.html)
- Locator only, explicitly marking Karanos as "(Possibly Fictional)" with reign "c. 808–778 BC": [Wikipedia, Argead dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argead_dynasty)
- End date, 146 BCE (Roman annexation / Battle of Corinth): see full sourcing under Entity 8 (Hellenistic period) below.

**Flags:**
- ⚑ **MAJOR — "808 BCE" is a legendary founder-date (Karanos) systematized only in later king-list tabulations, structurally identical to Rome's 753 BCE problem.** Every academic/institutional source actually fetched (Britannica, the Oxford Companion excerpt, the Lexicon of Argead Macedonia) instead dates the historically-attested Argead/Macedonian kingdom to **c. 700 BC** (via Perdiccas I), a full century later than the task's stated 808 BCE. The 808 BCE figure traces to Karanos, whom ancient sources themselves disagree existed as founder at all (Herodotus credits Perdiccas I instead; Theopompos alone credits Karanos). This is a second, clear instance of "received/legendary convention passed off as fact" — see synthesis in Item (d).
- n.a. note: no source fetched gives a precise, independently attested reason to prefer 808 over any other Karanos-tradition year; the "778" end-of-reign figure attached to Karanos in king-lists is equally unattested by contemporary evidence.

## 4. Archaic Greece: 800 – 480 BCE

**Kind of date:** Modern art-historical/scholarly periodization convention — not an ancient category.

**Contested?** Moderately — not a deep dispute, but genuine inconsistency in where sources place the **end** boundary (480 vs. 479 BC) and, for art-historical usage specifically, the **start** (650 vs. 800/776 BC).

**Sources:**
- National Archaeological Museum, Athens (museum-collection primary source): *"The term Archaic was coined in the 18th century by art historians... The Archaic Period covered roughly a time span from the 7th to the 6th century BC (700–500 BC)... The end of the Persian Wars (479 BC) signalled the passage to the ensuing Classical period."* — [National Archaeological Museum Athens: Archaic Period](https://www.namuseum.gr/en/collection/archaiki-periodos-2/)
- Britannica, art-historical usage: *"about 650 to 480 bc"* — [Britannica: Archaic period (art)](https://www.britannica.com/art/Archaic-period)
- World History Encyclopedia timeline: uses 800–**479** BCE (Plataea/Mycale, not just Salamis/Thermopylae 480) — [World History Encyclopedia: Timeline, Archaic Period](https://www.worldhistory.org/timeline/Archaic_Period/)

**Flags:**
- ⚑ The National Archaeological Museum Athens — one of the strongest institutional sources fetched in this brief — dates the Archaic period itself as **700–500 BC**, not 800–480 BC. This is a substantive discrepancy from the task's stated range, not a minor rounding difference.
- ⚑ End-date inconsistency: sources split between 480 BC (Salamis/Thermopylae year) and 479 BC (Plataea/Mycale, the actual end of the Persian invasion) as the "true" endpoint.

## 5. Rome (overall): 753 BCE – 476 CE

**Kind of date:** The start (753 BCE) is the **canonical example of received/traditional convention passed off as fact** — see Item (d) below for full discussion. The end (476 CE) is a **heavily contested historiographical marker**, not a scholarly consensus fact — see full treatment under Entity 12 (Roman Empire) and Entity 17 (Western Roman collapse).

**Contested?** **Yes, on both ends**, and for different reasons: 753 BCE is a back-calculated legendary founding date; 476 CE is a modern historiographical convention many scholars explicitly reject as a real "end."

**Sources:**
- Oxford Classical Dictionary (Tim Cornell, author): *"The conventional foundation date, fixed at 753 bce by Varro, is the result of artificial manipulation, and does not accord with any archaeological starting point; the earliest settlement is much earlier than 753, and the formation of an urbanized city-state considerably later."* Villages on Rome's hills, including the Palatine, are attested from "c. 1000 bce." — [Oxford Classical Dictionary: Rome (History)](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter/550496605)
- Britannica, "Ancient Rome" (author E. Badian, fact-checked by Britannica Editors, last updated Aug. 5, 2026): frames Rome's history as spanning "from the founding of the city and the regal period, which began in 753 bc, through... the establishment of the empire in 27 bc, and the final eclipse of the Empire of the West in the 5th century ad" — note Britannica itself only commits to "5th century AD," not the specific year 476, for the end — [Britannica: Ancient Rome](https://www.britannica.com/place/ancient-Rome/The-sack-of-Rome-by-Geiseric)
- Utrecht University Bachelor's thesis (Bram Mulder), extensively documenting ancient authors' disagreement on Rome's founding year: Timaeus (841 BC), Cincius (728 BC), Fabius Pictor (747 BC), Polybius/Diodorus (750 BC), Livy (749 BC), Dionysius of Halicarnassus (751 BC). Explicit conclusion: *"As we do not know when Rome has been founded, it should be avoided to call 753 the year of the founding of Rome."* — [Utrecht University thesis repository (PDF)](https://studenttheses.uu.nl/bitstream/handle/20.500.12932/20667/Fasti%20Consulares%20(Bachelor).pdf)
- The Conversation (Laura Swift, academic classicist): explains Varro's astrological back-calculation method via consular lists; notes archaeological finds pushing genuine settlement evidence to the 9th century BC — [The Conversation: Archaeology adds another twist to Rome's foundation story](https://theconversation.com/archaeology-adds-another-twist-to-romes-foundation-story-by-ageing-it-100-years-25727)
- 476 CE end-date sourcing: see Entity 12 below (extensive).

**Flags:**
- ⚑ **MAJOR — 753 BCE is not an attested historical fact; it is Varro's 1st-century-BC antiquarian back-calculation**, explicitly identified as "artificial manipulation" by the Oxford Classical Dictionary. Ancient authors themselves proposed at least six different founding years (841–747 BC). This is the paradigm case the task specifically asked to be flagged.
- ⚑ **476 CE is likewise a modern convention, not a consensus scholarly endpoint** — see Entity 12 for full sourcing. The "753 BCE – 476 CE" framing for "Rome overall" packages two contested conventions as if both were hard facts bookending a precisely dated era.

## 6. Roman Republic: 509 – 27 BCE

**Kind of date:** The start (509 BCE) is explicitly a **"Varronian" chronological convention** — an ancient annalistic reconstruction, not a directly attested date, though the OCD notes it is "likely to be correct in general terms." The end (27 BCE) is a genuine, well-attested political event (transfer of powers to Octavian/Augustus).

**Contested?** The 509 BCE date is a convention with known technical flaws (inserted dictator-years) that modern scholarship partially corrects to 508/507 BCE.

**Sources:**
- Oxford Classical Dictionary (Tim Cornell): *"the version of the Fasti Capitolini, based on the researches of Varro, places the beginning of the list, and therefore the beginning of the republic, in 509 bce; this Varronian system of dating, though incorrect in places, is conventionally followed by modern historians."* Adds that a late-6th-century date "is likely to be correct in general terms" and is "confirmed by independent Greek sources." — [Oxford Classical Dictionary: Rome (History)](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter/550496605)
- Utrecht University thesis (Bram Mulder): argues for a corrected reconstruction of **508/7 BC** after removing four "constitutionally impossible" inserted dictator-years from the Fasti Capitolini, describing this correction as "the practice usually applied by present day scholars" — [Utrecht University thesis repository (PDF)](https://studenttheses.uu.nl/bitstream/handle/20.500.12932/20667/Fasti%20Consulares%20(Bachelor).pdf)
- Perseus Digital Library / Tufts, Harpers Dictionary of Classical Antiquities, on the establishment of the Principate (27 BCE): [Perseus: Harpers Dictionary of Classical Antiquities](http://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.04.0062:alphabetic+letter=A:entry+group=1:entry=augustus-imperator-harpers)
- Britannica: [Britannica: Roman Republic](https://www.britannica.com/place/Roman-Republic)

**Flags:**
- ⚑ **509 BCE is explicitly labeled by the OCD as a Varronian convention "incorrect in places,"** not a directly measured date. Modern scholarly reconstruction favors 508/507 BC.

## 7. Classical Greece: 480 – 323 BCE

**Kind of date:** Modern scholarly periodization convention bookended by two genuine, well-attested events (Second Persian invasion; death of Alexander the Great).

**Contested?** Minor variance in the **start** convention (478, 480, or as early as 507/510 BC depending on whether the marker used is the end of the Persian Wars or the beginning of Athenian democracy).

**Sources:**
- Perseus/Tufts (Thomas R. Martin, overview essay): *"323 has traditionally been identified as the end of the Classical period"* — [Perseus: Overview of Classical Greek History](http://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.04.0009)
- National Archaeological Museum, Athens: confirms 323 BC (Alexander's death) as the Classical-period end marker — [National Archaeological Museum Athens: Classical Period](https://www.namuseum.gr/en/collection/klasiki-periodos/)
- World History Encyclopedia, "Hellenic World" timeline: uses 507/510 BC (first Athenian democracy) as an alternative start convention — [World History Encyclopedia: Timeline, Hellenic World](https://www.worldhistory.org/timeline/Hellenic_World/)
- Erenow-hosted textbook excerpt, *A History of the Classical Greek World, 478–323 BC*: uses 478 BC (aftermath of the Persian Wars) as its bracketing start — [Erenow: A History of the Classical Greek World](https://erenow.org/ancient/a-history-of-the-classical-greek-world-478-323-bc/)

**Flags:**
- Minor start-date variance (478/480/507 BC) depending on definitional choice (end of Persian Wars vs. democratic reform) — not a deep scholarly fight, but a real inconsistency worth flagging as convention-dependent rather than fixed fact.

## 8. Hellenistic period: 323 – 31 BCE

**Kind of date:** Modern historiographical term — coined retrospectively in the 19th century. Start (323 BC) is a genuine event (Alexander's death); the end date is **explicitly and openly contested** among at least two major conventions.

**Contested?** **Yes, significantly**, at the end-date. Sources split between 146 BC (Roman conquest of mainland Greece) and 31/30 BC (Actium / fall of Ptolemaic Egypt) as the "true" end, with some scholars extending it even further.

**Sources:**
- History Hit: *"the term 'Hellenistic period' is a retrospective term first deployed by 19th-century historian Johann Gustav Droysen"*; also states *"there is no universally accepted end date to the Hellenistic period, its denouement has been located at different points between the 2nd century BC and the 4th century AD"* — [History Hit: What brought about the end of the Hellenistic period?](https://www.historyhit.com/what-brought-about-the-end-of-the-hellenistic-period/)
- LibreTexts (Social Sciences), academic course text: gives Hellenistic period as **323–146 BCE** — [LibreTexts: Periods of Greek History](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Anthropology/Cultural_Anthropology/Ancient_Greek_Civilization/04%3A_Ancient_Greek_History/4.03%3A_Periods_of_Greek_History)
- Bryn Mawr Classical Review, review of Peter Green, *The Hellenistic Age*: uses 334/323–31 BC bounds — [Bryn Mawr Classical Review 2007.06.32](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2007/2007.06.32/)
- Bryn Mawr Classical Review, review of *The Greek World After Alexander*: uses 323–30 BC bounds — [Bryn Mawr Classical Review 2001.03.11](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2001/2001.03.11/)
- Wikibooks/Saylor.org (locator/course-text only, used here as a lay confirmation of the 146 BC convention rather than an authority): [Ancient Civilizations of the World: The Hellenistic Period](https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Saylor.org%27s_Ancient_Civilizations_of_the_World/The_Hellenistic_Period)

**Flags:**
- ⚑ **The task's "323–31" range silently picks the Actium-end convention over the equally common 146 BC (Corinth) convention.** History Hit's academic-facing summary states plainly there is no universally accepted end date at all. This should be flagged as a convention choice, not a settled fact.

## 9. Late Roman Republic: 133 – 27 BCE

**Kind of date:** The start (133 BC) is a genuine, well-attested political assassination functioning as a modern periodization marker. The **end date is inconsistent across scholarship** — genuinely contested, not merely rounded differently.

**Contested?** **Yes.** Sources disagree by as much as 89 years on where "Late Republic" ends (44 BC, 31 BC, or 27 BC all appear), and one source even relocates the whole period's start to 146 BC.

**Sources:**
- Britannica: confirms 133 BC start of "Late Republic (133–31 bc)" — note Britannica uses **31 BC**, not 27 BC, as the end — [Britannica: The reform movement of the Gracchi, 133–121 bc](https://www.britannica.com/place/ancient-Rome/The-reform-movement-of-the-Gracchi-133-121-bc)
- Wikipedia (locator only, quoting Cambridge Companion to the Roman Republic, Jürgen von Ungern-Sternberg): *"It was Tiberius' assassination that made the year 133 BC a turning point in Roman history and the beginning of the crisis of the Roman Republic."* — [Wikipedia: Gracchi brothers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gracchi_brothers) (locator only — underlying claim is attributed to the Cambridge Companion, a genuine academic source, but not independently fetched here)
- California State University Northridge course notes: bracket "Late Republic" instead as **146–44 BC** — an entirely different convention from either 133–31 or 133–27 — [CSUN: The Late Republic](https://www.csun.edu/~hcfll004/laterep.htm)

**Flags:**
- ⚑ **"Late Roman Republic" end-date genuinely varies across scholarship** between 44 BC (Caesar's assassination), 31 BC (Actium, per Britannica), and 27 BC (Augustus, per the task's stated range) — this is a real inconsistency in the literature, not a settled convention, and should be presented with that caveat.

## 10. Assassination of Caesar: 44 BCE (15 March)

**Kind of date:** A precisely, contemporaneously attested historical event — the strongest-attested date of all 17 entities in this brief.

**Contested?** No meaningful dispute about the date (15 March / Ides of March, 44 BC). Some scholarly interest in the layered sourcing chain, but not in the date itself.

**Sources:**
- AncientRomeLive (classics-education-oriented reference discussing primary-source layering): discusses the (lost) contemporary accounts by Asinius Pollio, Strabo, Gaius Oppius, and Bibulus, surviving indirectly via Nicolaus of Damascus — [AncientRomeLive: Assassination of Julius Caesar](https://www.ancientromelive.org/assassination-of-julius-caesar-ides-of-march-15-44-bc/)
- Ancient near-contemporary/eyewitness sources (as referenced in the above and standard classical scholarship): Cicero's own letters (written within days of the event), Suetonius's *Divus Julius*, Plutarch's *Life of Caesar* and *Life of Brutus*, Appian's *Civil Wars*, and Cassius Dio's *Roman History*.
- Locator only: [Wikipedia, Assassination of Julius Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assassination_of_Julius_Caesar); [Wikipedia, Ides of March](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ides_of_March)

**Flags:** None. This is the one entity in the set that is genuinely a directly attested historical event rather than a convention or back-calculation.

## 11. Battle of Actium: 31 BCE (2 September)

**Kind of date:** A precisely, near-contemporaneously attested historical event.

**Contested?** Only in a very minor sense — one modern monograph notes uncertainty about the exact day within a several-day window due to weather delays, though the ancient sources' explicit date (2 September, per Cassius Dio) is treated as authoritative by standard reference works.

**Sources:**
- UNRV Roman History: states the date and explicitly cites *"Plutarch, Dio Cassius, and Appian, supplemented by shorter notices in authors such as Velleius Paterculus, Florus, and Strabo"* — [UNRV: The Battle of Actium](https://www.unrv.com/military/the-battle-of-actium.php)
- Livius.org (historian-maintained classical-history reference, Jona Lendering): confirms 2 September 31 BC — [Livius.org: Battle of Actium](https://www.livius.org/articles/battle/actium-31-bce/)
- Published monograph excerpt (hosted): notes minor scholarly uncertainty about the precise day within a window of 29 August–2 September due to weather delays — [Dokumen.pub: The Battle of Actium, 31 BC](https://dokumen.pub/the-battle-of-actium-31-bc-war-for-the-world.html)

**Flags:** None significant — this is a well-attested contemporary event; the minor day-level uncertainty does not affect the year.

## 12. Roman Empire: 27 BCE – 476 CE

**Kind of date:** The start (27 BCE) is a genuine, well-attested political event (Augustus/Octavian granted extraordinary powers and the title "Augustus" by the Senate, 16 January 27 BC). The end (476 CE) is **the single most heavily contested "fact" in this entire brief** — an artificial historiographical marker, not a scholarly consensus.

**Contested?** **Yes — extensively**, and this is explicitly one of the dates the task asked to be checked.

**Sources (start, 27 BCE):**
- World History Encyclopedia: [The Principate of Augustus](https://www.worldhistory.org/article/2363/the-principate-of-augustus/)
- Perseus/Tufts, Harpers Dictionary of Classical Antiquities: [Augustus, Imperator](http://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.04.0062:alphabetic+letter=A:entry+group=1:entry=augustus-imperator-harpers)

**Sources (end, 476 CE — contested):**
- Wikipedia (locator only, but summarizing an academic consensus documented elsewhere): *"There is no consensus on a date for the start of Rome's decline"* and *"Some modern historians question the significance of the events of the year 476."* — [Wikipedia: Historiography of the fall of the Western Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historiography_of_the_fall_of_the_Western_Roman_Empire) (locator only)
- Wikipedia (locator only): *"As one convenient marker for the end, 476 has been used since Gibbon, but other key dates for the fall of the Roman Empire in the West include the Crisis of the Third Century, the Crossing of the Rhine in 406 (or 405), the sack of Rome in 410, and the death of Julius Nepos in 480."* — [Wikipedia: Fall of the Western Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_the_Western_Roman_Empire) (locator only)
- UCL PhD thesis (Campbell-Moffat): quotes Gibbon's own footnote acknowledging uncertainty — *"AD 476 appears to have the sanction of authentic chronicles... the precise year in which the Western empire was extinguished is not positively ascertained"* — and states outright, in the thesis author's own words, *"476 is not the year in which the empire ended, and never was."* — [UCL Discovery: Charting the end of the Western Roman State (PhD thesis, PDF)](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10178937/9/Campbell-Moffat_Thesis_10178937.Corrections_%20no%20pictures_29.11.23.pdf)
- UNRV Roman History: *"Traditionally, historians pinpoint 4 September 476 AD... though the precise dating varies in different sources"*; *"Scholarly debate continues as to whether 476 AD truly marked the empire's collapse."* — [UNRV: Romulus Augustulus](https://www.unrv.com/emperors/romulus-augustulus.php)
- Maltaguide-hosted academic-style reference PDF: *"The date of 476 was popularized by the 18th century British historian Edward Gibbon as a demarcating [point]..."* — [Western Roman Empire overview (PDF)](https://maltaguide.pro/pluginAppObj/pluginAppObj_373_01/Western_Roman_Empire.pdf)
- World History Encyclopedia: *"The conventional 'fall' is often dated to 476 CE, when Romulus Augustulus... was deposed, or to 480 CE, with the assassination of Julius Nepos... the last legitimate Western emperor."* — [World History Encyclopedia: The Roman Empire in 10 Maps](https://www.worldhistory.org/collection/189/the-roman-empire-in-10-maps/9/)
- Britannica ("Ancient Rome," E. Badian): notably does **not** commit to the year 476 at all, instead referring only to "the final eclipse of the Empire of the West in the 5th century ad" — [Britannica: Ancient Rome](https://www.britannica.com/place/ancient-Rome/The-sack-of-Rome-by-Geiseric)
- Academia.edu-hosted 2024 *Latomus* journal article: argues even the specific day "4 September 476" is questionable given contradictions among the surviving sources — [Academia.edu: When did Odoacer depose Romulus Augustulus?](https://www.academia.edu/124406620/When_did_Odoacer_depose_Romulus_Augustulus_)

**Flags:**
- ⚑ **MAJOR (explicitly requested by the brief) — 476 CE is a modern historiographical convention popularized by Gibbon, not a scholarly consensus fact.** Multiple academic sources (a UCL PhD thesis, Wikipedia's own historiography article, UNRV) state this directly and use strong language ("476 is not the year in which the empire ended, and never was"). Even the precise day (4 September) is separately disputed in peer-reviewed literature. Alternative end-markers actively used by historians include the Crossing of the Rhine (406), the Sack of Rome (410), the Vandal sack of Rome (455), the death of Majorian (461), and the death of Julius Nepos (480). This date should never be presented without this caveat.

## 14. Crisis of the Third Century: 235 – 284 CE

**Kind of date:** The bracketing events (death of Severus Alexander, 235; accession of Diocletian, 284) are solidly attested. **Whether everything between constitutes one coherent "crisis" is an actively disputed historiographical interpretation**, not a neutral fact.

**Contested?** **Yes — significantly, and exactly as the task asked to check.** A meaningful minority of scholars (particularly archaeologists) reject the coherence of "crisis" as a unifying frame for the period.

**Sources:**
- University of Richmond-hosted academic PDF, Adam Ziolkowski, *"The Background to the Third-Century Crisis of the Roman Empire"*: documents that the older "total crisis" model (demographic/economic/social/monetary collapse — associated with Rostovtzeff, MacMullen, and Géza Alföldy, whose own phrase was "Die Krise war total") has been challenged by revisionist archaeologists (T. Lewitt, C. Witschel) and historians (Jean-Michel Carrié with Aline Rousselle) who argue there was no unified crisis at all. Ziolkowski's own position: the crisis was real but narrowly political/military (breakdown of imperial legitimacy plus military weakness against Persia and Germanic peoples), not an economic collapse; he divides it into three overlapping phases: 235–253 (legitimacy crisis), 251–267 (military crisis), and 268–284 (renewed legitimacy crisis ending with Diocletian). — [University of Richmond: Ziolkowski, "The Background to the Third-Century Crisis" (PDF)](https://facultystaff.richmond.edu/~wstevens/history331texts/ziolkowski12.pdf)
- German Wikipedia (locator only, but useful for locating the German-language historiographical debate): *"Einige Forscher... bezweifeln grundsätzlich die Anwendbarkeit des Begriffes 'Krise'"* ("some scholars fundamentally doubt the applicability of the term 'crisis'") — [Wikipedia (DE): Reichskrise des 3. Jahrhunderts](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reichskrise_des_3._Jahrhunderts) (locator only)
- Turkish Historical Society journal *Belleten*: *"the literary evidence for the third century is atrocious... no continuous Greek or Roman historical account for the period exists"* — meaning even reconstructing the narrative, let alone characterizing it as a unified "crisis," is contested — [Belleten: full-text article](https://belleten.gov.tr/eng/full-text/2068/eng)
- Locator only: [Wikipedia, Crisis of the Third Century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crisis_of_the_Third_Century)

**Flags:**
- ⚑ **MAJOR (explicitly requested) — the coherence of "Crisis of the Third Century" as a single unified period is actively disputed by a significant scholarly minority**, especially archaeologists who find the "total crisis" narrative unsupported by material evidence. The bracketing years (235, 284) are solid as political turning points, but "Crisis" as a characterization of everything between them is a historiographical interpretation, not a neutral, settled fact.

## 16. Byzantine Empire: 330 – 1453 CE

**Kind of date:** The single most contested date-convention in this entire brief. "330 CE" (dedication of Constantinople) is a real, precisely attested event — but using it as the "start of the Byzantine Empire" is only one of at least six competing scholarly conventions.

**Contested?** **Yes — extremely, and exactly as the task's brief specifically flagged (330 vs. 395 vs. 476 vs. 620).**

**Sources:**
- OrthodoxWiki (used here for its clear enumeration of the competing scholarly positions, not as a dating authority in itself): *"Some place it during the reign of Diocletian (284–305)... Some consider Constantine I its founder... Others place it during the reign of Theodosius I (379–395)... or, following his death in 395, with the division of the empire... Others place it yet further in 476... Others again point to the reorganisation of the empire in the time of Heraclius (ca. 620) when Greek was made the official language."* — [OrthodoxWiki: Byzantine Empire](https://orthodoxwiki.org/Byzantine_Empire)
- Oodegr.com (scholarly-oriented Greek historiography reference): adds still more conventions — *"The British Museum starts its catalogue of Byzantine coins with Anastasius I (491–518). Many students consider Justinian (527–565) the first Byzantine emperor, while others hold that the Byzantine state properly so called is inaugurated with Heraclius (610–641)"* — and states explicitly that mainstream scholarly opinion holds *"there was an unbroken continuity which was so evident that no one may say... that at one specific point there was the end of one state and the inauguration of another"* — [Oodegr.com: The Roman Empire](https://www.oodegr.com/english/istorika/romi/roman_empire1.htm)
- *The Oxford Dictionary of Byzantium* (preface, archived full text): the standard academic reference for the field explicitly states it *"chose to use the term late Roman or late antique for the period of the 4th to the early or mid-7th century and to employ Byzantine for phenomena of the 7th century and later"* — meaning the discipline's own flagship reference work avoids calling anything before roughly the 7th century "Byzantine" at all — [Internet Archive: The Oxford Dictionary of Byzantium (full text)](https://archive.org/stream/odb_20210521/The%20Oxford%20Dictionary%20Of%20Byzantium_djvu.txt)
- Constantinople's dedication date (330 CE) itself is solidly attested:
  - EBSCO Research Starters: [Constantinople founded](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/constantinople-founded)
  - OUPblog (Oxford University Press academic blog): [Constantine dedicates Constantinople](https://blog.oup.com/2012/05/constantine-dedicates-constantinople/)
  - Livius.org: [Constantinople / Constantine's City](https://www.livius.org/articles/place/constantinople-istanbul/constantines-city/)
  - Encyclopaedia of the Hellenic World: explicitly notes, per historian Gilbert Dagron, that *"the foundation of Constantinople was a long procedure over many years, which gradually was epitomized in the tradition into this date"* — the 330 date functioning as "starting point of Byzantine history" is itself described as a retrospective historian's choice that replaced an earlier convention starting from 324 — [Encyclopaedia of the Hellenic World: Constantinople](http://constantinople.ehw.gr/Forms/fLemmaBodyExtended.aspx?lemmaID=11667)

**Flags:**
- ⚑ **MAJOR (explicitly requested) — "330 CE" is one of at least six actively used scholarly conventions for the start of the Byzantine Empire** (284/305, 330, 379–395, 476, ~491–518 per British Museum numismatic practice, 527, and 610–641 per Heraclius-centered arguments). The field's own standard reference work, the *Oxford Dictionary of Byzantium*, deliberately avoids the term "Byzantine" for anything before the 7th century. This should be presented as a convention choice with the range of alternatives named, never as a settled date.

## 17. Western Roman collapse: 455 – 480 CE

**Kind of date:** Both bookends are genuine, well-attested events (Vandal sack of Rome; death of Julius Nepos) used as a modern periodization bracket that deliberately supersedes 476 as the "true" endpoint.

**Contested?** The overall bracket (455–480) is itself a modern, minority alternative to the far more common single-year "476" framing; the choice of 455 as a *start* is one of several plausible alternatives (406, 408, 410, 454, 455, 461 all appear in the literature as candidate turning points).

## (c) ⚑ THE THERA / AEGEAN CHRONOLOGY DISPUTE — Extended Treatment

This is the deepest and most consequential dating dispute among all 17 entities, because it propagates through the entire absolute chronology of the Late Bronze Age Aegean, including the start of Mycenaean Greece (Entity 2).

**The core problem:** The eruption of the Thera (Santorini) volcano is a fixed point that different dating methods place at different times, and the discrepancy propagates through every "synchronism" between the Aegean world and Egypt/the Near East (whose own chronology is comparatively well anchored by king-lists and astronomical records).

**Two historical camps:**
- **"Low"/"young"/traditional archaeological chronology** (built on ceramic-style synchronisms with Egypt): places the eruption around **1530–1500 BCE**, after the start of the Egyptian New Kingdom.
  - PMC/NIH-hosted paper: explicitly notes the conventional archaeological date remains "~1530–1500 BCE" — [PMC: Second Intermediate Period date for the Thera eruption](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9488803/)
  - University of Arizona news release on tree-ring dating work: cites the traditional range as late as "1570–1500 BCE" — [University of Arizona News: Dating the ancient Minoan eruption of Thera using tree rings](https://news.arizona.edu/news/dating-ancient-minoan-eruption-thera-using-tree-rings)
  - Höflmayer (2009), hosted academic chapter: argues Tell el-Dab'a radiocarbon data "confirm grosso modo" the conventional archaeo-historical correlations — an explicitly anti-radiocarbon-revision viewpoint from within the field — [Academia.edu: Höflmayer, Thera/Tell el-Dab'a chronology chapter](https://www.academia.edu/226888/)

- **"High"/"old" radiocarbon chronology**: places the eruption in the **17th–16th century BCE**, roughly a century earlier than the traditional camp.
  - *Science Advances* (2018), peer-reviewed: "Annual radiocarbon record indicates 16th century BCE date for the Thera eruption" — shows a systematic offset from the IntCal13 calibration curve that narrows the gap with archaeological dating — [Science Advances: 16th century BCE date for the Thera eruption](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aar8241)
  - *PNAS* (2020), peer-reviewed: "On the enigma of dating the Minoan eruption of Santorini" — [PNAS 2020](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2004243117)
  - *Radiocarbon* journal (Cambridge, 2004), peer-reviewed: Bayesian modeling gives a 1663–1599 BC range — [Cambridge Core, Radiocarbon journal: Dating the Volcanic Eruption at Thera](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/radiocarbon/article/dating-the-volcanic-eruption-at-thera/)
  - Manning et al. (2014), *Antiquity*, hosted by Cornell's dendrochronology lab: places the eruption in the late 17th century BC — [Cornell Tree-Ring Laboratory: Manning et al., Antiquity 2014 (PDF)](https://dendro.cornell.edu/articles/Manningetal_Antiquity_2014.pdf)
  - Cornell Chronicle (2022), reporting on refined statistical work: gives a range of 1609–1560 BCE (95.4% probability) or 1606–1589 BCE (68.3% probability) — [Cornell Chronicle / Archaeology at Cornell: Statistical analysis aims to solve Greek volcano mystery](https://archaeology.cornell.edu/news/statistical-analysis-aims-solve-greek-volcano-mystery)
  - PMC/NIH paper (same as above): reports the same 1609–1560 / 1606–1589 BCE range as the current best radiocarbon estimate — [PMC: Second Intermediate Period date for the Thera eruption](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9488803/)
  - Bryn Mawr Classical Review, of Manning's book: cites a 1632–1614 BC probable range at 68.2% confidence — [Bryn Mawr Classical Review 2016.04.06](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2016/2016.04.06/)

**Post-2020 developments (as specifically requested):**
- *Nature Scientific Reports* (2023): "Olive shrub buried on Therasia supports a mid-16th century BCE date" — this newer dendrochronological/radiocarbon work supports a **mid-16th century BCE** date, which is notably closer to (though still somewhat earlier than) the traditional archaeological range, suggesting some convergence — [Nature Scientific Reports: Olive shrub study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-33696-w)
- Oxford Academic, *PNAS Nexus* (2022): discusses how a radiocarbon "plateau" between roughly 1610–1540 BCE complicates precise calibration regardless of method — [Oxford Academic PNAS Nexus: ice-core/radiocarbon constraints](https://academic.oup.com/pnasnexus/article/1/2/pgac048/6575909)
- *PLOS ONE* (2025), Hendrik J. Bruins (Ben-Gurion University of the Negev) and Johannes van der Plicht (University of Groningen): "The Minoan Thera eruption predates Pharaoh Ahmose: Radiocarbon dating of Egyptian 17th to early 18th Dynasty museum objects" — radiocarbon-dated Egyptian museum objects from the 17th–early 18th Dynasty place the Thera eruption **before** Pharaoh Ahmose I's reign (independently dated to roughly 1539 BCE and later in this study), i.e., the eruption predates the conventional ~1500 BC archaeological anchor by decades at minimum — this is the newest peer-reviewed contribution located in this research pass, keeping the dispute open rather than resolving it — [PLOS ONE: Bruins & van der Plicht 2025](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0330702); reported via [Archaeology Magazine: Thera eruption predates Pharaoh Ahmose](https://archaeologymag.com/2025/10/thera-eruption-predates-pharaoh-ahmose/)
- Wikipedia (locator only): notes the IntCal20 calibration curve update has shifted the probable radiocarbon range to include much of the 16th century BC, and that the once-popular specific year "1627 BC" is "no longer supported," while stating plainly the "exact year has yet to be settled" — [Wikipedia: Minoan eruption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minoan_eruption) (locator only)

**Framing sources for the debate itself:**
- Aegeus Society (Aegean prehistory research society) academic overview: frames this explicitly as the "high" vs. "low" Aegean chronology debate, stretching Late Minoan IA/IB/II phase dates by roughly a century and affecting every synchronism with Egypt and the Near East — [Aegeus Society: The debate on Aegean high and low chronologies, an overview through Egypt](https://www.aegeussociety.org/en/new_article/the-debate-on-aegean-high-and-low-chronologies-an-overview-through-egypt/); also archived at [Academia.edu version](https://www.academia.edu/201936/)

**Synthesis (for the Mycenaean Greece entry, #2 above):** The gap between the radiocarbon/"high" camp and the traditional archaeological/"low" camp has **narrowed since 2020** — from roughly 100+ years down to something closer to 30–70 years in the most recent work (e.g., the 2023 Nature Scientific Reports mid-16th-century result sits closer to, though still earlier than, the ~1500 BC traditional date) — but the dispute is **NOT resolved**. The newest 2025 PLOS ONE study still finds the eruption predates the conventional Egyptian anchor point. Because the start of the Mycenaean period (the Late Helladic I transition) is chronologically calibrated against this same evidentiary base, the claimed "1600 BCE" start for Mycenaean Greece should be understood as **compatible with most current framings but resting on an unresolved absolute-chronology dispute**, not as a precisely fixed point.

## (d) ⚑ Where the received convention is passed off as fact — summary across all 17 entities

The task specifically asked to flag every instance where an inherited/traditional convention is presented as if it were a directly attested fact. Consolidating the flags raised above:

1. **Rome, 753 BCE (Entities 5 & 6):** The paradigm case. Varro's antiquarian back-calculation, explicitly called "artificial manipulation" by the Oxford Classical Dictionary ([OCD: Rome](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter/550496605)). Ancient authors themselves proposed at least six different founding years.
2. **Macedon, 808 BCE (Entity 3):** A second, structurally identical case. The 808 BCE figure comes from later systematized king-lists built around the possibly-legendary founder Karanos, whom ancient authors themselves disputed (Herodotus credits Perdiccas I instead). Every academic source fetched (Britannica, the Oxford Companion to Classical Literature, the *Lexicon of Argead Macedonia*) instead anchors the historically-attested kingdom to **c. 700 BC** — a full century later.
3. **Roman Republic, 509 BCE (Entity 6):** A less extreme but real case — explicitly labeled "Varronian... though incorrect in places" by the OCD, with modern scholarship preferring 508/507 BC.
4. **Roman Empire / Rome overall, 476 CE (Entities 5, 12, 17):** Not a *legendary* convention like 753/808, but an equally clear case of a **19th-century historiographical convention (Gibbon's) being presented as a hard endpoint** when a UCL PhD thesis, World History Encyclopedia, and UNRV all state plainly that many historians reject its significance and prefer alternative markers (410, 455, 461, 480).
5. **Byzantine Empire, 330 CE (Entity 16):** A milder version of the same pattern — a real, well-attested event (Constantinople's dedication) is used as if it were self-evidently "the" start of a new empire, when the field's own standard reference work (the *Oxford Dictionary of Byzantium*) avoids calling anything before the 7th century "Byzantine" at all, and at least five rival conventions exist.
6. **"Archaic Greece," "Classical Greece," "Hellenistic period" as category labels (Entities 4, 7, 8):** These are all **modern retrospective terms** (Archaic coined in the 18th century per the [National Archaeological Museum, Athens](https://www.namuseum.gr/en/collection/archaiki-periodos-2/); Hellenistic coined by Droysen in the 19th century per [History Hit](https://www.historyhit.com/what-brought-about-the-end-of-the-hellenistic-period/)) applied retroactively to antiquity — not categories the ancient Greeks themselves used. This doesn't make the date ranges wrong, but presenting them without noting they are modern analytical constructs risks implying a false ancient self-awareness of periodization.
7. **"Crisis of the Third Century" as a label (Entity 14):** The name itself encodes an interpretive judgment ("crisis," implying coherent systemic collapse) that a real body of scholarship (Lewitt, Witschel, Carrié/Rousselle) rejects — this is a case of a *characterization*, not just a date, being smuggled in as fact.
