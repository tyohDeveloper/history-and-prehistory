## PRIORITY 1: Is "Mesolithic" a valid global category?

**name**: The Mesolithic as a taxonomic/global category (historiographical entity, not a dated period)

**dates**: N/A — this is a concept-validity question, not a chronological entity.

**significance**: The single most contested methodological question surrounding the term "Mesolithic": whether it names a real, cross-culturally comparable stage of human development, or is a residual, Eurocentric label imposed on non-European sequences that have their own internal logic and periodization.

**Origin and definitional history**:
- Hodder Westropp gave the first definition of "Mesolithic" in 1865, though the term "failed to catch on" at the time; the French term "Mésolithique" was independently introduced by Adrien Reboux in 1869 ([Elliott & Warren 2023, *Norwegian Archaeological Review*](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00293652.2023.2182232), peer-reviewed).
- The term had already been applied to Argentinian prehistory by 1878, and Brown (1888) explicitly credited the term's currency to Thomas Carlyle's association with the British Geological/Archaeological Survey of India — an explicitly colonial scientific institution ([Elliott & Warren 2023](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00293652.2023.2182232)).
- ⚑ **Contested origin claim, worth flagging**: Some Indian sources credit A.C. Carlyle with coining "Mesolithic" based on his finds in the Vindhyan hills of India, prior to or alongside the European naming history above — an Indian-origin discovery credited within a British-authored terminology (IGNOU teaching material, [egyankosh.ac.in](https://egyankosh.ac.in/bitstream/123456789/41356/1/Unit-1.pdf) — not a peer-reviewed source, use with caution, but the discrepancy itself is a documented historiographical complication worth noting rather than resolving).
- Arkell (1947) applied the "Mesolithic" label in Anglo-Egyptian Sudan, then under British colonial administration ([Elliott & Warren 2023](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00293652.2023.2182232)).
- The "modern" meaning of Mesolithic (post-glacial, pre-agricultural, geographically centered on temperate Europe) developed in the late 19th century and was widely accepted by the early 20th century, becoming embedded in social-evolutionary developmental models ([Elliott & Warren 2023](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00293652.2023.2182232), citing Rowley-Conwy 1996; Zvelebil et al. 2009).

**The central critique — "Mesolithic" as a negative/residual category**:
- Czarnik (1976, cited and quoted at length in [Elliott & Warren 2023](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00293652.2023.2182232)): "the idea of the Mesolithic began as a negative category positioned between better-defined periods" — i.e., defined by what it is *not* (not Palaeolithic, not Neolithic) rather than by positive content. Direct quote: "the idea of the Mesolithic, whether we know it or not, has the effect of making an unbroken trajectory of cultural evolution conform to our notion of absolute geological time" (Czarnik 1976, p. 65, as cited in Elliott & Warren 2023).
- Warren (2021, cited in [Elliott & Warren 2023](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00293652.2023.2182232)): the term's analytical utility is questioned, but it "remains a widely used concept, especially in Europe where it arguably still has some value" — i.e., even critics generally do not recommend abolishing the term for Europe specifically.
- Brinch Petersen and Meiklejohn (2007, cited in [Elliott & Warren 2023](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00293652.2023.2182232)) criticize how "complexity" became treated as an end-goal for Mesolithic archaeology rather than a neutral analytical heuristic.

**The colonial-diffusion critique of a GLOBAL Mesolithic**:
- [Elliott & Warren 2023](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00293652.2023.2182232) directly critique Ellis (2021) and Graeber & Wengrow (2021, *The Dawn of Everything*, pp. 123, 125) for constructing a universal, global "Mesolithic" developmental stage. Their assessment: this exports "a European typo-chronological label ('Mesolithic')... pegged to a developmental concept" onto the rest of the world, and "the Eurocentric Mesolithic is alive and kicking, and continues to generate uncritically universal and progressivist understandings of the global human past."
- McNiven and Russell (2005, cited in [Elliott & Warren 2023](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00293652.2023.2182232)) argue that hunter-gatherer-stage concepts like the Mesolithic are historically tied to colonial ideologies that enabled settler-colonial dispossession and, in some cases, genocide, by casting indigenous populations as occupying a "primitive" developmental stage.
- Lavi et al. (2023, cited in [Elliott & Warren 2023](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00293652.2023.2182232)): the term carries "a powerful and damaging set of public meanings" beyond its technical archaeological use.
- **Elliott & Warren's own position** (not a call for abolition): they raise "serious questions" about using the term outside its area of origin (northern Europe), call for regional histories rather than universal developmental models, but continue to use "Mesolithic" cautiously for Europe itself.

**A competing, economic-ecological definition tradition (Soviet/Siberian archaeology)**:
- [Dolitsky 1985, *Arctic* 38(3), pp. 178–187](https://journalhosting.ucalgary.ca/index.php/arctic/article/download/65183/49097) (peer-reviewed) documents a long-running Soviet-era dispute distinct from the Anglo-European one: Childe, Rogachev, and Okladnikov favored either merging the "Mesolithic" into a broadened Paleolithic or calling it "Epipaleolithic"; others (Efimenko 1953; Martynov 1981; Matyushin 1976; Chernysh 1975) treated it as a genuinely distinct transitional stage.
- Braidwood (1960, cited in [Dolitsky 1985](https://journalhosting.ucalgary.ca/index.php/arctic/article/download/65183/49097)) called the tripartite Paleolithic/Mesolithic/Neolithic division "artificial" and proposed a three-stage economic model instead (food-gathering, food-producing, industrial).
- Kozlowski (1973) and Bader (1974) (cited in [Dolitsky 1985](https://journalhosting.ucalgary.ca/index.php/arctic/article/download/65183/49097)) argued for redefining "Mesolithic" as an economic-ecological stage rather than a fixed chronological slot, restricting the term to regions where post-glacial tundra was replaced by woodland — meaning a "Mesolithic-type economy" could occur at different absolute dates in different places.
- T.D. Price (1983, p. 762, cited in [Dolitsky 1985](https://journalhosting.ucalgary.ca/index.php/arctic/article/download/65183/49097)) offered a minimalist, "provocative" definition: Mesolithic = "the period of the Postglacial prior to the introduction of agriculture," explicitly NOT defined by microliths, forest adaptation, coastal settlement, or dog domestication (all common secondary associations).
- Dolitsky's own conclusion, testing an economic ("interbiotic zone," salmon/fish-based) model against Siberian data: the Siberian "Mesolithic" (where geometric microliths — the standard European technological marker — are largely absent) "provides no evidence of a common history with Eurasian geometric microlithic technology." In other words, even where the label "Mesolithic" is retained for Siberia, it denotes a shared adaptive situation, not a shared technological package inherited from or connected to Europe.

**Regional terminology used instead of "Mesolithic"**:
- **Africa**: "Later Stone Age" (LSA) is preferred over "Mesolithic." The term was introduced in 1929 by Goodwin and Van Riet Lowe at the African Archaeological Congress ([Oxford Research Archive, "The Stone Age Archaeology of West Africa"](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:1442d1c1-7f76-454d-97db-8fd52a4c6c1e), institutional repository). The Middle Stone Age (MSA) to LSA transition is dated to roughly 65,000 years ago in eastern Africa, with LSA-type industries persisting into the mid-Holocene in some regions ([Oxford Academic, "Middle Stone Age to Later Stone Age Transition in Eastern Africa"](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61663/chapter/553472358?guestAccessKey=), peer-reviewed volume chapter).
- **South Asia**: "Mesolithic" was adopted from the 1970s onward as an import of European terminology, which led scholars to conflate "microlith" with "Mesolithic" as if they were synonymous ([Roberts et al., "Microliths in the South Asian rainforest ~45–4 ka," *PMC6774521*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6774521/), peer-reviewed). This conflation is now understood to be false: microlithic technology in South Asia has been shown to have Pleistocene antiquity, predating the Holocene by tens of thousands of years, which undermines any automatic equation of "microliths present" with "Mesolithic period" ([PMC6774521](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6774521/)).
  - The South Asian dispute over the value of the term is explicit and ongoing: Possehl (2003, p. 31, cited in [Mesolithic Sindh dissertation PDF, iris.unive.it](https://iris.unive.it/bitstream/10278/19301/1/MesoSindhPraehistoria.pdf)) states that equating microliths with "Mesolithic" has "little utility." Misra (2002, p. 112, same source) defends retaining a Mesolithic stage concept. Allchin et al. (1978, p. 99, same source) call "Mesolithic" "a more comprehensive cultural term" describing hunting groups that in South Asia sometimes overlapped in time with settled agricultural or even urban communities — i.e., in South Asia the term explicitly does NOT denote a fixed chronological package, unlike its conventional European usage.
- **Americas**: the "Archaic" period is used in place of "Mesolithic" for the analogous post-Pleistocene hunter-gatherer stage. (Only a general encyclopedia-tier characterization was found for this parallel during this pass — no peer-reviewed source specifically justifying/critiquing this substitution was fetched; treat the existence of the term "Archaic" as established but its direct equivalence claim as `n.a.` pending a dedicated Americas-focused search.)
- **Southwest Asia**: the term "Epipalaeolithic," not "Mesolithic," is standard for the corresponding period; it was likewise coined in the context of late-19th/early-20th-century European and North African archaeology and only later applied in Southwest Asia to distinguish late-glacial hunter-gatherers from sedentary Neolithic farmers ([Richter & Maher 2013, "Terminology, process and change: reflections on the Epipalaeolithic of South-west Asia"](http://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1179/0075891413Z.00000000020), peer-reviewed — note: this dataset already covers the West Asian Epipalaeolithic separately and it is not re-researched here beyond this terminological point).

**⚑ LITERATURE MOVED**: The explicit, print critique of a "global Mesolithic" as a Eurocentric export is recent — Elliott & Warren's rebuttal of Graeber & Wengrow's *The Dawn of Everything* (2021) was published in 2023. Before this, criticisms existed (Czarnik 1976; Dolitsky 1985) but a global-scale, high-profile popularization of "Mesolithic" as a worldwide developmental stage, followed by an equally public archaeological rebuttal, is a 2021–2023 development ([Elliott & Warren 2023](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00293652.2023.2182232)).

**⚠ COMMON MISCONCEPTION**: That "Mesolithic" denotes a single, universal package of traits (microliths + forest adaptation + coastal/riverine sedentism) that can be identified anywhere in the world at the point hunter-gatherers reach a certain "stage." Multiple lines of evidence contradict this: microliths independently and much earlier in South Asia ([PMC6774521](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6774521/)); economic-ecological rather than technological definitions needed in Siberia ([Dolitsky 1985](https://journalhosting.ucalgary.ca/index.php/arctic/article/download/65183/49097)); entirely different regional labels in Africa (LSA) and the Americas (Archaic).

## PRIORITY 2: The European Mesolithic

### 2a. Maglemose, Kongemose, Ertebølle (Southern Scandinavia)

**name**: Maglemose culture (Maglemosian)
**dates**: ~11,000–8,400 cal BP, per [Allentoft et al. 2024, "100 ancient genomes show repeated population turnovers in Neolithic Denmark," *Nature*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06862-3) (peer-reviewed; radiocarbon dating with Bayesian trapezoidal-phase modeling in OxCal v4.4, Reimer et al. calibration curve; 81 radiocarbon dates from 64 Danish sites; explicit cal BP frame).
- Cross-check (differs, presented not averaged): [Oxford Handbook of Mesolithic Europe, "Southern Scandinavia" chapter](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/59635/chapter/505049919?guestAccessKey=) (peer-reviewed volume) gives ~9800–6400 BC, but does not consistently state whether these are calibrated ("bc" lower-case is used inconsistently with some explicitly-marked "cal bc" figures elsewhere in the same chapter, e.g., Tågerup 6500–5000 cal bc) — flagged as an internal calibration-labeling ambiguity in this source.
**significance**: The earliest well-defined post-glacial forager culture of southern Scandinavia, associated with barbed bone points, microliths, and lakeside/wetland settlement.
**sources**: [Allentoft et al. 2024, *Nature*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06862-3); [Oxford Handbook of Mesolithic Europe, Southern Scandinavia chapter](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/59635/chapter/505049919?guestAccessKey=).
**flags**: Calibration-labeling ambiguity in the Oxford Handbook source (see above).

**name**: Kongemose culture
**dates**: ~8,400–7,400 cal BP ([Allentoft et al. 2024, *Nature*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06862-3), peer-reviewed, radiocarbon/Bayesian, cal BP).
- Cross-check: Oxford Handbook gives 6400–5400 BC ([academic.oup.com](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/59635/chapter/505049919?guestAccessKey=)); the [British Academy PDF, "Economic Prehistory in Southern Scandinavia"](https://www.thebritishacademy.ac.uk/documents/3848/99p125.pdf) gives fish-trap dates of 7880–7675 BP for early Kongemose (uncalibrated radiocarbon BP, per the source's own notation) and 7200–6950 BP / 7200–6910 BP marking the Kongemose–Ertebølle transition.
**significance**: Transitional culture marked by rhombic microliths and increasing coastal/marine resource use, bridging Maglemose forest-forager adaptations and Ertebølle maritime specialization.
**sources**: as above.
**flags**: Presented as three source-tied date ranges rather than averaged, per sourcing rules.

**name**: Ertebølle culture
**dates**: ~7,400–5,900 cal BP ([Allentoft et al. 2024, *Nature*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06862-3), peer-reviewed; latest dated skeleton "Rødhals Man," specimen NEO645, dated 5,916–5,795 cal BP). Widely cross-confirmed in the shell-midden literature (Priority 5 below) at ca. 5400–3950/3900 cal BC, equivalent to roughly 7350–5900 cal BP ([Archaeofauna journal, Havnø midden paper](https://revistas.uam.es/archaeofauna/article/download/6380/6856), peer-reviewed open-access; [Zenodo 2024 deposit on Danish marine resource exploitation](https://zenodo.org/records/14019080), academic repository, gives "c. 5400–4000 BCE").
**significance**: The type culture for Late Mesolithic maritime adaptation in northern Europe: large shell middens, polished antler/bone tools, dugout canoes, extensive fishing technology (fish weirs/traps), and the last major pre-agricultural culture of southern Scandinavia before the Funnel Beaker (TRB) Neolithic.
**sources**: [Allentoft et al. 2024, *Nature*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06862-3); [Archaeofauna Havnø midden paper](https://revistas.uam.es/archaeofauna/article/download/6380/6856); [Zenodo 2024](https://zenodo.org/records/14019080); [VLIZ PDF, "Denmark: Mesolithic Coastal Landscapes Submerged"](https://www.vliz.be/imisdocs/publications/343780.pdf) (institutional marine-science repository, gives Ertebølle "between 7400 and 5900 years ago").
**flags**: None beyond standard cross-source variance already noted.

⚠ **COMMON MISCONCEPTION**: Popular sources frequently present a single, precise start/end date for Maglemose/Kongemose/Ertebølle as if the boundaries were sharp events. The primary Bayesian radiocarbon literature ([Allentoft et al. 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06862-3)) treats these as phase transitions modeled with uncertainty ranges, not knife-edge boundaries, and other academic sources place the same transitions at dates differing by centuries (see cross-checks above under each culture).

### 2b. Sauveterrian (France, extending into northeast Italy)

**name**: Sauveterrian culture
**dates**: First appeared in the first centuries of the Preboreal in southwest France, persisting to at least 8,200 cal BP there (excluding two unreliable/mixed dates); in northeast Italy the conventional lower boundary is 10,000 uncalibrated BP = 11,550 cal BP (following Mellars), though some sites (Plan de Frea IV, Romagnano Loc III) yield older dates, showing this boundary is not a strict cultural marker; in the Tusco-Emilian area, 11,200–10,500 cal BP down to ~9,270–8,650 cal BP (most recent reliable date, at Piazzana); in the southern French Alps, three phases are proposed: 11,200–10,500 cal BP / 10,500–9,500 cal BP / 9,500–8,700/8,500 cal BP. All figures per [Visentin, Davide, PhD thesis, "The Early Mesolithic in Northern Italy and Southern France: An investigation into Sauveterrian lithic technical systems," University of Ferrara](https://iris.unife.it/bitstream/11392/2396714/1/Visentin%20text.pdf) (also at [theses.hal.science](https://theses.hal.science/tel-02063939v1/file/Visentin_Davide.pdf)) — peer-reviewed doctoral thesis, 223 radiocarbon dates, calibrated with OxCal v4.2.4 / IntCal13 (Reimer et al. 2013), 2σ/95.4% confidence, explicit cal BP frame.
**significance**: The characteristic Early Mesolithic culture of southern France and adjoining Italy, defined by small backed bladelets/triangles ("Sauveterre points"); first identified in the 1930s by L. Coulonges at Le Martinet and Le Roc Allan (Sauveterre-la-Lémance), later confirmed at Cuzoul de Gramat by R. Lacam and A. Niederlender ([Visentin thesis](https://iris.unife.it/bitstream/11392/2396714/1/Visentin%20text.pdf)).
**sources**: [Visentin PhD thesis, iris.unife.it](https://iris.unife.it/bitstream/11392/2396714/1/Visentin%20text.pdf) / [mirror at theses.hal.science](https://theses.hal.science/tel-02063939v1/file/Visentin_Davide.pdf).
**flags**: The thesis itself states "the definition of the Mesolithic is described as 'a much debated topic'"; the Palaeolithic–Mesolithic and Early–Late Mesolithic boundaries are explicitly called "controversial" within the source.

### 2c. Tardenoisian (France) — UNRESOLVED, presented plainly as such per instructions

**name**: Tardenoisian culture
**dates**: **n.a. as a single range — the primary/academic literature genuinely disagrees and does not consistently state calibration status.** Specific figures found, none authoritative alone:
- [Thévenin 1996, "Mésolithique récent et Mésolithique final entre Bassin parisien et Alpes," OpenEdition Books](https://books.openedition.org/artehis/517?lang=fr) (peer-reviewed volume chapter): trapeze armatures appear ~7,900–7,800 BP; a first trapeze group is dated ~7,900–6,700 BP (type site Montbani 13); a second group (evolved armatures) 6,700–6,450 BP; trapezes disappear by 6,000 BP. **The source does not state whether these BP figures are calibrated or uncalibrated, and does not name a dating method** — this is an explicit, sourced gap.
- [Rozoy (personal academic site), "Docteur Jean-Georges Rozoy" publication page](http://www.rozoy.fr/publication.php?doc=meso_en): Rozoy explicitly rejects equating "Tardenoisian" with trapeze microliths generally, restricting the term only to the culture of the Tardenois/Paris region specifically ("To assimilate Tardenoisian with the trapezia, and vice versa, is a gross methodological error" — direct quote from the source). Scattered dates given in BC with **no BP dates and no stated calibration status anywhere on the page**: Geldrop I, 9010±95 BC; Hatert, 5720±110 BC; Rouffignac (late Sauveterrian) 5850±50 BC — the source itself states this last site "may no longer be called Tardenoisian"; Montclus ~5,800–5,900 BC.
- Tertiary/popular sources diverge wildly and are NOT treated as authoritative here, but their spread illustrates the scale of the disagreement: French Wikipedia gives −10,000 to −5,000 (locator only, NOT a dating authority); Encyclopædia Universalis gives 7,000–4,000 BC; German Wikipedia gives 6,000–5,000 BC (locator only); the English Wikipedia/DBpedia Tardenoisian entry internally states both "9000 BC until 6000 [BC]" and, elsewhere on the same page, "until 4000 BC in Britain" (locator only, internally inconsistent); IGNOU teaching material gives a "best radiocarbon date" of 7045±106 BC for Sauveterrian and 5400±350 BC for "Lower Tardenoisian" with no calibration status stated ([egyankosh.ac.in](https://egyankosh.ac.in/bitstream/123456789/41356/1/Unit-1.pdf), not peer-reviewed).
**significance**: A Late Mesolithic microlithic industry (small geometric trapezes) named for the Tardenois region near Paris; historically important as a type-culture in French Mesolithic classification, but its definitional boundaries remain disputed.
**sources**: [Thévenin 1996](https://books.openedition.org/artehis/517?lang=fr); [Rozoy publication page](http://www.rozoy.fr/publication.php?doc=meso_en); tertiary sources cited above ARE explicitly flagged as non-authoritative and used only to illustrate the scale of disagreement.
**flags**: **This is a confirmed, unresolved disagreement, consistent with and reproducing what a prior research pass found.** Primary academic sources (Thévenin, Rozoy) do not give a single agreed date range, do not consistently state calibration status (cal vs. uncalibrated), and disagree on the basic definition of the culture (trapeze-based technological marker per Thévenin vs. a regionally-restricted culture-historical unit per Rozoy, who considers the trapeze equation a "gross methodological error"). Per the task's instruction, **no single date range is asserted for the Tardenoisian in this dataset entry; the disagreement is stated plainly instead.**

### 2d. Azilian

**name**: Azilian culture
**dates**: Emergence at the end of the Magdalenian, with earliest manifestations (backed-curved points) at the open-air sites of Champréveyres and Monruz (Lake Neuchâtel) dated 14,400–14,000 cal BP; the culture as a whole spans the Allerød, Dryas III (Younger Dryas), and Preboreal pollen zones, straddling the Pleistocene–Holocene boundary; assemblages labeled "Azilian" range as old as ~12,000 uncalibrated BP to as recent as ~9,000 uncalibrated BP per one synthesis ([novoscriptorium.com summary of Azilian chronology](https://novoscriptorium.com/2020/03/31/the-azilian-epipaleolithic-industry-of-spain-and-france/) — **not a peer-reviewed source itself; treat the specific 12,000–9,000 BP framing as unverified pending a stronger citation, but the Champréveyres/Monruz 14,400–14,000 cal BP dates and the Allerød/Dryas III/Preboreal span are corroborated by peer-reviewed material below**).
- Regional/peer-reviewed refinement for the Pyrenees: the Pyrenean Azilian runs from 14,300–12,500 cal BP, with the "typical" Pyrenean Azilian present from 14,300 cal BP and the Early Azilian ending at 13,700 cal BP further north; the Magdalenian persists until 14,200 cal BP in the Pyrenees specifically ([Lithic perspectives on the Late Upper Palaeolithic in the French Pyrenees, hal.science](https://hal.science/hal-03492888v1/file/S1040618220302500.pdf), peer-reviewed, explicit cal BP frame).
- Regional/peer-reviewed refinement for Cantabria (Spain): Azilian begins ~14.5 ka BP in the Allerød and continues to 10.9 ka cal BP at El Mirón, where the Mesolithic cultural change is identified ([Degruyter 2023, "Socioeconomic, Technological, and Cultural Adaptation..."](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/opar-2022-0291/html), peer-reviewed, explicit cal BP, 152 radiocarbon dates from 100 sites spanning 13,890–6,820 cal BP overall for the wider Cantabrian sequence).
- Spanish Basque-region synthesis: the Magdalenian–Azilian transition falls c. 11,500–11,000 uncalibrated BP (Allerød/phase VIII), with the Azilian continuing through Dryas III/phase IX and much of the Preboreal, to roughly 9,500–9,000 uncalibrated BP ([aranzadi.eus, Munibe journal PDF](https://www.aranzadi.eus/fileadmin/docs/Munibe/1994053068AA.pdf), peer-reviewed regional journal — explicitly uncalibrated BP, in contrast to the cal BP figures from the Pyrenees and Cantabria sources above).
**significance**: The Azilian represents the technological/cultural transition out of the Magdalenian Upper Palaeolithic and into the earliest Mesolithic in southwestern Europe (Franco-Cantabrian region), marked by backed points, harpoons, and painted pebbles; it is regionally the direct predecessor of the Sauveterrian.
**sources**: [hal.science Pyrenees paper](https://hal.science/hal-03492888v1/file/S1040618220302500.pdf); [Degruyter 2023 Cantabria paper](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/opar-2022-0291/html); [Aranzadi/Munibe journal PDF](https://www.aranzadi.eus/fileadmin/docs/Munibe/1994053068AA.pdf); [novoscriptorium.com](https://novoscriptorium.com/2020/03/31/the-azilian-epipaleolithic-industry-of-spain-and-france/) (non-peer-reviewed, used cautiously and flagged).
**flags**: Sources disagree on whether dates are cal BP or uncalibrated BP even within Spain (Cantabria source uses cal BP; Basque/Munibe source uses uncalibrated BP) — present both, do not merge. The non-peer-reviewed novoscriptorium figures (12,000–9,000 BP framing) should be treated as illustrative only, not authoritative.

### 2e. British Mesolithic

**name**: British Mesolithic (overall sequence)
**dates**: c. 9,800–3,600 cal BC overall, per [Conneller & Griffiths 2024, "Time and Change in Mesolithic Britain c. 9800–3600 cal BC," *Proceedings of the Prehistoric Society*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/proceedings-of-the-prehistoric-society/article/time-and-change-in-mesolithic-britain-c-98003600-cal-bc/0AD03AE18BCD46BB2F3505DB043F8652) (peer-reviewed; Bayesian analysis of 1,518 radiocarbon measurements in OxCal using the Reimer et al. 2020 IntCal calibration curve; explicit cal BC frame throughout).
- Early Mesolithic first appears in the 94th/93rd century cal BC (94% probability) to the 83rd/80th century cal BC; Star Carr-type material pre-dates other Early Mesolithic material (99.9% probability); Deepcar-type material appears 88th–86th century cal BC (62% probability); Cramond and Nab Head types appear in the 85th/84th century cal BC ([Conneller & Griffiths 2024](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/proceedings-of-the-prehistoric-society/article/time-and-change-in-mesolithic-britain-c-98003600-cal-bc/0AD03AE18BCD46BB2F3505DB043F8652)).
- Middle Mesolithic forms first apparent ~8,300 cal BC (kernel density estimate peak) ([Conneller & Griffiths 2024](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/proceedings-of-the-prehistoric-society/article/time-and-change-in-mesolithic-britain-c-98003600-cal-bc/0AD03AE18BCD46BB2F3505DB043F8652)).
- Late Mesolithic spans the 72nd/70th to 47th/45th century cal BC, defined by Left-Lateralised Scalene (LLS) triangle assemblages persisting into the 5th millennium cal BC ([Conneller & Griffiths 2024](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/proceedings-of-the-prehistoric-society/article/time-and-change-in-mesolithic-britain-c-98003600-cal-bc/0AD03AE18BCD46BB2F3505DB043F8652)).
- Final Mesolithic forms begin the 49th–46th century cal BC; occupation appears to decline from the 41st century cal BC; the Final Mesolithic ends 39th/36th century cal BC ([Conneller & Griffiths 2024](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/proceedings-of-the-prehistoric-society/article/time-and-change-in-mesolithic-britain-c-98003600-cal-bc/0AD03AE18BCD46BB2F3505DB043F8652)).
- Cross-check with sub-phase detail: EM2 "Star Carr" phase c. 9,800–7,800 cal BC; EM3 "Deepcar" phase c. 9,400–7,200 cal BC; EM5 narrow-blade material from c. 8,000 cal BC; LM5 c. 7,080–6,700 cal BC ([Degruyter 2022, "Britain In or Out of Europe During the Late Mesolithic?"](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/opar-2022-0249/html), peer-reviewed, explicit cal BC).
- Additional cross-check: Early Mesolithic broad-blade material ~9,350 cal BC; narrow-blade appearance possibly as early as 8,500 cal BC at Cramond ([Oxford Handbook of Mesolithic Europe, "Britain and Ireland" chapter](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/59635/chapter/505050096), peer-reviewed volume).
**significance**: One of the most intensively and precisely Bayesian-dated national Mesolithic sequences in the world; provides a template for how microlith/assemblage-type transitions can be dated with sub-century precision using large radiocarbon datasets.
**sources**: [Conneller & Griffiths 2024, *PPS*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/proceedings-of-the-prehistoric-society/article/time-and-change-in-mesolithic-britain-c-98003600-cal-bc/0AD03AE18BCD46BB2F3505DB043F8652); [Degruyter 2022](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/opar-2022-0249/html); [Oxford Handbook, Britain and Ireland chapter](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/59635/chapter/505050096); [Ashmolean Museum collection page](https://britisharchaeology.ashmus.ox.ac.uk/collections/mesolithic.html) (institutional museum source, gives Early ~9,600–6,000 BC, Later ~6,000–4,000 BC, with calibration status labeled only for the Neolithic-transition date).
**flags**: None — this is the best-resolved, most consistently calibrated regional sequence found in this research pass; no unresolved disagreement of the Tardenoisian type exists here.

### 2f. Microliths — what they indicate

**name**: Microliths (as an analytical/technological category, not a dated entity)
**significance and findings**:
- Microlith form changes correlate with abrupt cooling events at 10.3, 9.3, and 8.2 ka cal BP along the southern North Sea basin, based on a Bayesian analysis of 122 critically-selected radiocarbon dates; use-wear analysis confirms these microliths functioned as hunting-gear components (arrowheads/barbs), but the author (Crombé) argues that microlith style changes served a social/group-identity "boundary defense" signaling function rather than being purely functionally optimized — i.e., microlith typology is not simply a passive technological response to environment ([Crombé, "Mesolithic projectile variability along the southern North Sea," PMC6636730](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6636730/), peer-reviewed).
- ⚠ **COMMON MISCONCEPTION, explicitly flagged in the literature**: "the presence of microliths by itself should not be used to define an occupation as Mesolithic" ([PMC9216571, "Microlithic variation and the Mesolithic occupations of western..."](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9216571/), peer-reviewed). This is reinforced by the South Asian evidence (Priority 1 and Priority 7) that microliths there are of Pleistocene age, predating any Holocene "Mesolithic" designation ([PMC6774521](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6774521/)).
- Microwear analysis of Western Mediterranean microliths confirms their principal use as projectile points/transverse arrow points in hunting ([Gibaja 2012, CSIC institutional repository](https://digital.csic.es/bitstream/10261/93765/1/Gibaja-2012-Microliths.pdf) — institutional repository, peer-reviewed-adjacent research output).
**sources**: [PMC6636730](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6636730/); [PMC9216571](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9216571/); [PMC6774521](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6774521/); [Gibaja 2012, digital.csic.es](https://digital.csic.es/bitstream/10261/93765/1/Gibaja-2012-Microliths.pdf).
**flags**: ⚠ COMMON MISCONCEPTION as stated above (microliths ≠ automatic "Mesolithic" designation).

## PRIORITY 3: Doggerland and the drowning of the North Sea plain

**name**: Doggerland (submergence sequence; the Storegga tsunami event itself is already in the dataset and not re-researched — only its relationship to Mesolithic settlement is covered here)

**dates and stages** (all cal BP, per [Walker et al. 2020, "A great wave: the Storegga tsunami and the end of Doggerland?" *Antiquity* 94(378)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/great-wave-the-storegga-tsunami-and-the-end-of-doggerland/CB2E132445086D868BF508041CC1B827), peer-reviewed; the article itself does not explain its calibration procedure or name the calibration curve, and does not state that radiocarbon dating specifically was used for the core deposits, but consistently uses "cal BP" notation):
- **c. 10,000 cal BP**: Doggerland (reconstructed) stretches from Yorkshire to Denmark, with the Dogger Hills as a modest upland zone at its northernmost limit.
- **By 9,000 cal BP**: Doggerland had fragmented; the uplands now forming the Dogger Bank were probably cut off as "Dogger Island."
- **9,000–8,000 cal BP**: Dogger Island survived roughly another millennium after 9,000 cal BP; the wider landscape fragmented into estuaries, inlets, and islands — after this point (and especially after the 8.2 ka event) the landscape is better termed the "Dogger Archipelago."
- **8,400–8,200 cal BP**: global average sea level rose by 1–4 m, possibly in two phases (citing Hijma & Cohen, per the Antiquity article).
- **c. 8,150 cal BP**: by the time the Storegga tsunami struck, the preceding sea-level rise had reduced Dogger Island to a shallow sand bank; reconstructed shorelines indicate a possible surviving area of ~1,000 km² even under a maximal 4 m sea-level-rise estimate.
- **After the tsunami**: sedaDNA evidence indicates the floodwaters withdrew and land recovered on the "Dogger Littoral" — i.e., the tsunami did NOT produce a lasting inundation.
- **c. 7,000 cal BP** (~1,150 years after the tsunami): Dogger Island would presumably have disappeared, with only a handful of islands remaining; shorelines of Denmark, northwest Germany, the Netherlands, Belgium, and southern Britain (the "Dogger Littoral") continued beyond present coastlines, in some cases (e.g., the Wash, East Anglia) by tens of kilometers, and continued in use into the Neolithic and later.

⚑ **LITERATURE MOVED (2026)**: A 2026 sediment-core study reports that the START of Doggerland's inundation began much earlier and more gradually than previously assumed — radiocarbon-dated sediments place the onset of inundation at 10,243–10,199 years ago, in the Greenlandian period ([The Past magazine, "New timeline for the drowning of Doggerland"](https://the-past.com/news/new-timeline-for-the-drowning-of-doggerland/), 2026 — note: this is a science-news summary of new research, not itself the primary peer-reviewed paper; treat the specific figures as provisionally reported pending direct access to the underlying publication, but flag the shift itself as real and recent).

**Relationship to Mesolithic settlement, depopulation, and the "wipeout" debate** (per [Walker et al. 2020, *Antiquity*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/great-wave-the-storegga-tsunami-and-the-end-of-doggerland/CB2E132445086D868BF508041CC1B827) and [Sharrocks et al. 2023, "Evaluating the impact of the Storegga tsunami on Mesolithic communities in..." *Journal of Quaternary Science*](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/208352/1/J_Quaternary_Science_-_2023_-_Sharrocks_-_Evaluating_the_impact_of_the_Storegga_tsunami_on_Mesolithic_communities_in.pdf), both peer-reviewed):
- Only two Mesolithic sites (Castle Street, Scotland; Dysvikja, Norway; possibly Fjørtoft, Norway) had confirmed Storegga tsunami deposits overlying them at the time of the Antiquity article's writing — and even these cannot be taken as reliable evidence of immediate event impact, since deposits may be erosive and may remove rather than preserve evidence.
- **Weninger et al. (2008) "wipeout" scenario** (cited within [Walker et al. 2020](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/great-wave-the-storegga-tsunami-and-the-end-of-doggerland/CB2E132445086D868BF508041CC1B827)): concluded the tsunami had a "catastrophic impact on the contemporary coastal Mesolithic population," proposing it as the cause of Doggerland's final inundation.
- **Hill et al. (2014, revised 2017) survival scenario** (cited within [Walker et al. 2020](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/great-wave-the-storegga-tsunami-and-the-end-of-doggerland/CB2E132445086D868BF508041CC1B827)): the revised 2017 model estimates maximal inundation covered only ~35% of the exposed landmass, comparable to an extreme high tide; the pre-Storegga submergence of much of Dogger Island challenges the original 2014 model's assumptions.
- **Waddington and Wicks (2017)** (cited within [Walker et al. 2020](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/great-wave-the-storegga-tsunami-and-the-end-of-doggerland/CB2E132445086D868BF508041CC1B827)): observed a drop in site numbers on the northeast British coast BEFORE the tsunami, with a slow population rebound centered on c. 8,000 cal BP; argued the tsunami scoured recently-deposited archaeological remains — but the Antiquity article calls this "difficult to verify."
- **Blankholm (2020)** (cited within [Walker et al. 2020](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/great-wave-the-storegga-tsunami-and-the-end-of-doggerland/CB2E132445086D868BF508041CC1B827)): identified a site-distribution drop in Varangerfjord, Norway, correlated with elevations 2–3 m above the 8,100 BP shoreline, but with no well-dated deposits and no clear geological tsunami signature; urges caution, citing possible taphonomic and ethnoarchaeological alternative explanations.
- **Walker et al.'s (2020) own conclusion**: "it is increasingly untenable to maintain scenarios of wholesale catastrophic destruction or the definitive inundation of the last vestiges of Doggerland... the Storegga tsunami was neither universally catastrophic nor a final flooding event for the Dogger Bank or Dogger Littoral" — subsequent ordinary sea-level rise, not the tsunami, caused the eventual flooding of remaining Doggerland. The article also proposes that surviving Dogger Littoral areas may have served as a "cultural, if not territorial, buffer" between the incoming Linear Pottery Culture and the coast during the final Mesolithic–Neolithic transition.
- **Sharrocks et al. (2023)** (specific to Howick and Low Hauxley, northeast England): the Storegga tsunami is dated to 8,120–8,175 cal a BP, following Bondevik et al. (2012), citing "Green mosses date the Storegga tsunami to the chilliest decades of the 8.2 ka cold event" as their source for this age — i.e., this study explicitly ties the tsunami's dating to the 8.2 ka event chronology (see Priority 4). Modeled mortality estimates for people caught in the intertidal zone at Howick range from under 10% to 100% depending on the mortality function used; a sharp population decline in Northern Britain is dated to "immediately after the concurrent events of approximately 8,200 BP," using summed calibrated probability distributions (SCPD) of radiocarbon-dated sites as a population proxy. However, Sharrocks et al. explicitly do NOT claim this proves extinction: several sites (Castlandhill, East Barns) were occupied on both sides of the Storegga period, East Barns was reoccupied more than 1,000 years after its initial occupation, and Howick itself was reoccupied in the Neolithic, Bronze Age, and Iron Age — "no marked change in behaviour" is observed at the time of the tsunami itself.
- The debate is explicitly named in the literature as "Resilience or wipe out?" — the title of Waddington and Wicks (2017), as cited in Sharrocks et al. (2023).

⚠ **COMMON MISCONCEPTION**: That the Storegga tsunami was THE single cause of Doggerland's final disappearance. The peer-reviewed literature (both [Walker et al. 2020](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/great-wave-the-storegga-tsunami-and-the-end-of-doggerland/CB2E132445086D868BF508041CC1B827) and the Weninger et al. 2008 "slow vs. rapid flooding" distinction cited within it) treats submergence as a centuries-long, gradual process punctuated by — but not simply caused by — the tsunami; parts of Doggerland persisted for roughly another 1,000+ years afterward.

**sources**:
- [Walker, Gaffney, Fitch, Muru, Fraser, Bates & Bates, 2020, "A great wave: the Storegga tsunami and the end of Doggerland?" *Antiquity* 94(378)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/great-wave-the-storegga-tsunami-and-the-end-of-doggerland/CB2E132445086D868BF508041CC1B827) — peer-reviewed.
- [Sharrocks et al. 2023, "Evaluating the impact of the Storegga tsunami on Mesolithic communities in...", *Journal of Quaternary Science*](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/208352/1/J_Quaternary_Science_-_2023_-_Sharrocks_-_Evaluating_the_impact_of_the_Storegga_tsunami_on_Mesolithic_communities_in.pdf) — peer-reviewed.
- [The Past magazine, 2026, "New timeline for the drowning of Doggerland"](https://the-past.com/news/new-timeline-for-the-drowning-of-doggerland/) — science journalism summarizing new research; used only for the ⚑ flag, not as a primary dating source.
- [Oxford Handbook of Mesolithic Europe, "Doggerland" chapter](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/59635/chapter/505050022?guestAccessKey=) — peer-reviewed volume (background/context only in this pass; not separately quoted above but consistent with the Walker et al. account of partial Doggerland survival into the Late Mesolithic).

**flags**: ⚑ LITERATURE MOVED (2026, earlier/more gradual inundation onset). ⚠ COMMON MISCONCEPTION (tsunami as sole cause). Field disagreement presented as both claims: Weninger et al. 2008 "catastrophic wipeout" vs. Hill et al. 2017 "partial, tide-like inundation" vs. Walker et al. 2020's own synthesis rejecting both extremes; Waddington & Wicks 2017 "population collapse" vs. Blankholm 2020 "cautious, alternative explanations possible" vs. Sharrocks et al. 2023 "severe local impact + demonstrated regional resilience/reoccupation."

## PRIORITY 4: The 8.2 kiloyear event and its effect on Mesolithic populations

**name**: The 8.2 kiloyear (8.2 ka) event
**dates**: Conventionally dated in the NGRIP Greenland ice core to between 8,250 and 8,090 cal BP ([García-Escárzaga et al. 2022, "Human forager response to abrupt climate change at 8.2 ka on the Atlantic coast of Europe," *Scientific Reports*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-10135-w), peer-reviewed). A wider bracket of 8,400–7,900 cal BP is given by another source ([Science.gov topic summary citing ice-core literature](https://www.science.gov/topicpages/k/ka+cooling+event), U.S. government science aggregator). A recent preprint gives 8.25±0.05 to 8.09±0.05 ka as the constrained timing and duration ([EGUsphere/Copernicus preprint 2025](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2025/egusphere-2025-2945/egusphere-2025-2945-manuscript-version2.pdf) — preprint, not yet fully peer-reviewed, flagged as such). The widely accepted trigger is the final collapse of the Laurentide ice sheet near Hudson Bay and the associated sudden drainage of proglacial Lake Agassiz into the North Atlantic around 8,400 BP ([OSTI.gov, Geological Survey of Denmark and Greenland Bulletin](https://www.osti.gov/etdeweb/servlets/purl/938920), U.S. government repository).
**significance**: The most abrupt, well-documented Holocene cooling event, used as a natural experiment for testing hunter-gatherer resilience vs. vulnerability to rapid climate change.

**Effects on Mesolithic populations — field disagreement is real and regionally variable; presented as such:**

*Evidence FOR a population collapse/demographic crash:*
- Western Scotland: a Bayesian chronological analysis of radiocarbon dates, using "activity events" as a population proxy, found "a dramatic reduction in the Mesolithic population synchronous with the 8.2 ka event," interpreted as "the demographic collapse of a low density population that lacked the capability to adapt to the rapid onset of new environmental conditions" ([Wicks & Mithen 2014, University of Reading repository](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/60211/), peer-reviewed, published in *Journal of Archaeological Science*).
- Argyll, Scotland (regional synthesis): following c. 8,200 cal BP the archaeological record shows a "dramatic decline" in activity events, even more pronounced in summed calibrated probability distributions; Wicks and Mithen (2014) attributed this to reduced biomass/productivity and increased risk of sea-travel and coastal foraging, resulting in either landscape abandonment or demographic collapse — they preferred the collapse interpretation, arguing that even slight reductions in birth rate or increases in mortality could substantially reduce population; population is interpreted to have declined to low but still-viable levels (roughly 260 out of a potential range up to 2,370) between 8,200 and 6,600 cal BP, with continuing activity and technological continuity through the decline/recolonization phase suggesting the population did NOT become locally extinct ([SCARF Argyll regional report, 5.3.4](https://scarf.scot/regional/rarfa/5-the-early-prehistory-of-argyll-the-archaeological-record-research-themes-and-future-priorities-for-the-palaeolithic-mesolithic-and-earliest-neolithic-periods-12000bp-6000bp-10050bc-4050b/5-3-major-research-themes/5-3-4-population-decline-the-impact-of-climate-change/), citing the peer-reviewed Wicks & Mithen 2014 study).
- Northeast England (Howick, Low Hauxley): the 8.2 ka event coincided with the Storegga tsunami and caused "a rapid, sustained decrease in temperatures across parts of Northwest Europe," compounding tsunami damage to autumn-collected hazelnut stores just before winter, at a time of maximum seasonal vulnerability ([Sharrocks et al. 2023](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/208352/1/J_Quaternary_Science_-_2023_-_Sharrocks_-_Evaluating_the_impact_of_the_Storegga_tsunami_on_Mesolithic_communities_in.pdf), peer-reviewed — see also Priority 3 above).
- Southern North Sea basin (Rhine-Meuse-Scheldt area): a Bayesian analysis of 122 radiocarbon dates shows microlith design changes clustering around the 8.2 ka event (dated here 8,250–8,090 cal BP), interpreted as a response to resource stress from cooler, drier conditions reducing hazelnut and other nut/fruit-bearing shrub availability, compounded by forced population displacement from the drowning North Sea basin increasing competition in adjacent territories ([Crombé, PMC6636730](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6636730/), peer-reviewed — see also Priority 2f above).
- Central-eastern Cantabria: an abrupt population fall is recorded around 11.6 ka cal BP attributed to the Younger Dryas, and separately the 8.2 ka event (dated 8,400–8,000 cal BP in this source) is identified as the last abrupt change in North Atlantic thermohaline circulation affecting the region ([Degruyter 2023 Cantabria paper](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/opar-2022-0291/html), peer-reviewed).

*Evidence AGAINST a population collapse / for resilience:*
- Star Carr, Britain: "there is no hiatus in occupation during the climatic cooling" of the relevant abrupt cooling event, and the event "triggers no change or adaptation in human activity" — the Star Carr community continued lake-edge occupation and platform construction/usage with "negligible evidence for climatically-induced change," demonstrating that "when a high-precision archaeological chronology can be directly compared with a local climate record, hunter-gatherer populations can show a high degree of resilience to abrupt climate forcing" ([Blockley et al., University of Reading repository, "The resilience of postglacial hunter-gatherers to abrupt climate..."](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/76449/1/Blockley%20et%20al%20%20NAT%20HEE%20final%20(7%20files%20merged)%20(1).pdf), peer-reviewed).
- Scheldt basin, Belgium: a multi-proxy analysis found "little evidence supporting a causal link" between the 9.3 and 8.2 ka cooling events and diachronic changes in human behavior in the region; observed changes (population peak and high mobility in the Early Mesolithic; population shift and increased intra-basin mobility in the Middle Mesolithic) seem more attributable to long-term climatic/environmental change than to the short abrupt events, "hinting at considerable resilience." This directly contradicts, and the authors explicitly say does not support, the demographic-collapse conclusions reached for Scotland ([Crombé et al., VLIZ PDF, "Population collapse or human resilience..."](https://www.vliz.be/imisdocs/publications/378682.pdf), peer-reviewed, also on [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0278416521000817)).
- Northern Iberia / Atlantic Portugal: cold sea-surface temperatures during the 8.2 ka event changed shellfish species availability and exploitation strategy (intensified mollusc use), but this is interpreted as demographic GROWTH in these Atlantic coastal refugia, not collapse, even as a population decrease occurred simultaneously and separately in the inland Ebro valley due to colder/drier conditions there — i.e., the 8.2 ka event's demographic effect was spatially heterogeneous even within Iberia ([García-Escárzaga et al. 2022, *Scientific Reports*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-10135-w), peer-reviewed; cross-confirmed in [PMC9021199](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9021199/), peer-reviewed).
- Southeast Europe (Neolithic-adjacent, included here for contrast, NOT Mesolithic): a study on early farming communities in this region found "no evidence for a simultaneous and widespread collapse, large-scale site abandonment, or migration" at either the 9,250 or 8,200 cal BP climate events, though some local adaptation is indicated ([Flohr et al. 2015, University of Reading repository](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/40539/1/Flohr%20et%20al%202015%20QSR%20preprint.pdf), peer-reviewed — note this study concerns early farmers, not hunter-gatherers, and is cited here only to show that "resilience vs. collapse" is a live debate across multiple contemporaneous population types, not a Mesolithic-specific anomaly).

**sources**: [Wicks & Mithen 2014 (Reading repository)](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/60211/); [SCARF Argyll 5.3.4](https://scarf.scot/regional/rarfa/5-the-early-prehistory-of-argyll-the-archaeological-record-research-themes-and-future-priorities-for-the-palaeolithic-mesolithic-and-earliest-neolithic-periods-12000bp-6000bp-10050bc-4050b/5-3-major-research-themes/5-3-4-population-decline-the-impact-of-climate-change/); [Sharrocks et al. 2023](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/208352/1/J_Quaternary_Science_-_2023_-_Sharrocks_-_Evaluating_the_impact_of_the_Storegga_tsunami_on_Mesolithic_communities_in.pdf); [Crombé, PMC6636730](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6636730/); [Degruyter 2023 Cantabria](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/opar-2022-0291/html); [Blockley et al., Reading repository](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/76449/1/Blockley%20et%20al%20%20NAT%20HEE%20final%20(7%20files%20merged)%20(1).pdf); [Crombé et al., VLIZ PDF](https://www.vliz.be/imisdocs/publications/378682.pdf) / [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0278416521000817); [García-Escárzaga et al. 2022, *Scientific Reports*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-10135-w); [PMC9021199](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9021199/); [Flohr et al. 2015, Reading repository](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/40539/1/Flohr%20et%20al%202015%20QSR%20preprint.pdf); [OSTI.gov Laurentide ice sheet bulletin](https://www.osti.gov/etdeweb/servlets/purl/938920); [EGUsphere 2025 preprint](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2025/egusphere-2025-2945/egusphere-2025-2945-manuscript-version2.pdf) (preprint, flagged).

**flags**: Field disagreement presented as BOTH claims, not averaged: Scotland/southern North Sea basin (collapse/resilience-limited) vs. Belgium/Scheldt basin and Star Carr (marked resilience, no causal link demonstrated) vs. Iberia (spatially heterogeneous — simultaneous growth in Atlantic refugia and decline inland). This is one of the clearest examples in the whole dataset of the "field disagrees — present both, don't average" instruction applying directly.

## PRIORITY 5: Shell middens and maritime adaptation

### 5a. Ertebølle middens (Denmark)

**name**: Ertebølle "kitchen middens" (køkkenmøddinger), type site Ertebølle, North Jutland
**dates**: The Ertebølle culture overall spans c. 5,400–3,950/3,900 cal BC (≈7,350–5,900 cal BP) per multiple peer-reviewed cross-checks: [Archaeofauna, Havnø midden paper](https://revistas.uam.es/archaeofauna/article/download/6380/6856) states "the majority of the kitchen middens belong to the Late Mesolithic Ertebølle culture (ca. 5400–3950 cal. B.C.)," with midden deposition at the Havnø site itself dated by radiocarbon to ca. 5,000–3,500 cal BC; [Zenodo 2024 deposit](https://zenodo.org/records/14019080) gives "c. 5400–4000 BCE." The type-site Ertebølle midden itself was first radiocarbon-dated (shell samples) to as early as 5,760±100 BP / 3,810 BC and as late as 5,660±120 BP, per the original Copenhagen radiocarbon date series ([Tauber, "Copenhagen radiocarbon dates X," University of Arizona radiocarbon journal archive](https://journals.uair.arizona.edu/index.php/radiocarbon/article/download/337/342), peer-reviewed journal archive — note: these are laboratory-reported conventional radiocarbon ages, explicitly labeled as such in the source, not stated as calibrated).
- Regional shoreline/coastal-settlement dates: most Ertebølle-period coastal sites (e.g., Tybrind Vig, Ronæs Skov, Møllegabet II) fall between 7,400 and 5,900 years ago ([VLIZ PDF, "Denmark: Mesolithic Coastal Landscapes Submerged"](https://www.vliz.be/imisdocs/publications/343780.pdf), institutional marine-science repository).
**significance**: The Ertebølle middens are the type example of intensive, semi-sedentary maritime foraging in Mesolithic Europe — large shell mounds accumulated over centuries at fixed locations, widely used as evidence of "complex hunter-gatherer" sedentism and reduced residential mobility in contrast to earlier, more mobile forest-forager patterns.
**sources**: [Archaeofauna, Havnø midden paper](https://revistas.uam.es/archaeofauna/article/download/6380/6856); [Zenodo 2024](https://zenodo.org/records/14019080); [Tauber, Copenhagen radiocarbon dates X](https://journals.uair.arizona.edu/index.php/radiocarbon/article/download/337/342); [VLIZ PDF](https://www.vliz.be/imisdocs/publications/343780.pdf); [Open.lnu.se, "42 HumaNetten"](https://open.lnu.se/index.php/hn/article/download/297/283/1154) (peer-reviewed-adjacent journal, gives kitchen midden dating "between 5050–4050 cal BC," with pre-midden occupation layers at some Danish sites — Ertebølle, Björnsholm — dated 4,960–4,600 cal BC).
**flags**: The type-site Ertebølle date (Tauber) is an uncalibrated conventional radiocarbon age as stated in the source; this is presented separately from the calibrated cal BC ranges from the Archaeofauna/Zenodo sources rather than merged with them.

### 5b. Muge middens (Portugal)

**name**: Muge shell middens (Tagus valley) and Sado valley middens, central Portugal
**dates**: Multiple peer-reviewed studies converge on a broad span but differ in specifics — presented separately, not averaged:
- Mesolithic sites in central Portugal (Tagus and Sado valleys) generally dated 8,500–7,000 cal BP ([Estudo Geral / University of Coimbra repository, "The case of Muge, central Portugal"](https://estudogeral.uc.pt/bitstream/10316/45835/1/cias2016_3.pdf), peer-reviewed).
- Cabeço da Amoreira shell-midden deposition specifically dated 8,000–7,350 cal BP ([same source, Estudo Geral](https://estudogeral.uc.pt/bitstream/10316/45835/1/cias2016_3.pdf)).
- The Muge Mesolithic overall tied to estuarine resource availability, ~8,100–6,800 cal BP: Arruda bone dates range 7,900–6,931 cal BP; Moita do Sebastião dates cluster around 7,800 cal BP with an outlier at 7,412 cal BP; Amoreira dates cover two periods, ~7,900 cal BP and then ~7,500 cal BP extending to ~6,800 cal BP ([Jackes & Lubell, University of Waterloo](http://www.arts.uwaterloo.ca/~mkjackes/Jackes%20and%20Lubell%20incorrect.pdf), university-hosted peer-reviewed-adjacent research output).
- New AMS dating (Roksandic 2006 and in prep.) indicates Mesolithic groups occupied the Muge valley from at least ~6,000 cal BC to ~5,000 cal BC (Cabeço da Arruda oldest, Cabeço da Amoreira youngest) — longer than previously believed ([Detry 2008, University of Lisbon repository](https://repositorio.ulisboa.pt/bitstream/10451/28830/1/Detry-2008-CMorros-Mesolitico-Promontoria.pdf), university repository, peer-reviewed-adjacent).
- Cabeço da Amoreira specifically dated ca. 6,500–5,000 cal BCE by a large number of consistent radiocarbon dates on bone, charcoal, and shell ([Max Planck Institute repository, "Multidisciplinary investigation reveals an individual of West African origin..."](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_3376861_5/component/file_3376864/content), institutional repository, peer-reviewed).
- Sado valley burial activity: began with the first human burial at Vale de Romeiras dated 8,543–8,334 cal BP; Late Mesolithic chronology in Portugal generally given as ca. 8,300–7,200 cal BP (Araújo 2015) or, more conservatively, ca. 8,150–7,550 cal BP (Carvalho 2009); burial activity in the Tagus and Sado valleys ended between 7,488–7,258 cal BP (Cova da Onça, Tagus) and 7,439–7,275 cal BP (Cabeço das Amoreiras, Sado), with the very last Sado burial at Várzea da Mó ranging 7,315–7,026 cal BP ([Cambridge/Radiocarbon journal, "Chronology of the burial activity of the last hunter-gatherers in the southwestern Iberian Peninsula (Portugal)"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/radiocarbon/article/chronology-of-the-burial-activity-of-the-last-huntergatherers-in-the-southwestern-iberian-peninsula-portugal/F6F433C5E8A432B890DF2703956FDC52), peer-reviewed).
- Coexistence with early farmers: Zilhão (2011, cited in [livros.fcsh.unl.pt, "The Mesolithic-Neolithic transition through the..."](https://livros.fcsh.unl.pt/cham/catalog/download/98/857/2651?inline=1), peer-reviewed volume chapter) states the Tagus and Sado shell middens were occupied at least until the 5th millennium BC, exemplifying long-term coexistence between the shell-midden foragers and adjacent, non-overlapping Early Neolithic sites on the Limestone Massif.
**significance**: The Muge and Sado middens are the paradigm case for the Iberian Late Mesolithic "complex forager" model — large, long-used shell-midden cemeteries and settlements showing intensified estuarine resource use, extended sedentism, and — significantly — sustained temporal overlap and interaction with incoming Neolithic farming groups rather than an abrupt replacement (see Priority 7 below).
**sources**: [Estudo Geral/University of Coimbra repository](https://estudogeral.uc.pt/bitstream/10316/45835/1/cias2016_3.pdf); [Jackes & Lubell, University of Waterloo](http://www.arts.uwaterloo.ca/~mkjackes/Jackes%20and%20Lubell%20incorrect.pdf); [Detry 2008, University of Lisbon repository](https://repositorio.ulisboa.pt/bitstream/10451/28830/1/Detry-2008-CMorros-Mesolitico-Promontoria.pdf); [Max Planck Institute repository](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_3376861_5/component/file_3376864/content); [Cambridge/Radiocarbon journal, burial chronology paper](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/radiocarbon/article/chronology-of-the-burial-activity-of-the-last-huntergatherers-in-the-southwestern-iberian-peninsula-portugal/F6F433C5E8A432B890DF2703956FDC52); [livros.fcsh.unl.pt volume chapter](https://livros.fcsh.unl.pt/cham/catalog/download/98/857/2651?inline=1); [PLOS ONE, "The Emergence of Mesolithic Cemeteries in SW Europe"](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0115505) (peer-reviewed, states first Muge cemetery evidence at Cabeço de Arruda dates to 8,409–8,030 cal BP, and Sado cemeteries begin slightly later, about 8,200 cal BP; from 8,160–7,970 cal BP Mesolithic groups "systematically buried all or some of their dead in cemeteries").
**flags**: Multiple overlapping but not identical date ranges from different research teams/methods are presented separately above rather than merged into one figure, consistent with the sourcing rules.

### 5c. Scottish Obanian (Oronsay and Oban Bay)

**name**: Obanian culture / Oronsay shell middens, western Scotland
**dates**: Mellars' original excavations produced the first radiocarbon dates for Obanian sites: bulk charcoal samples from five middens on Oronsay gave radiometric ¹⁴C ages of c. 6,200–5,400 BP (5,150–4,250 cal BC) ([Bonsall et al., University of Edinburgh repository, "A Mesolithic Site at Kilmore, near Oban, Western Scotland"](https://www.pure.ed.ac.uk/ws/files/539999/2009_BANN_FLAKES_Bonsall_etal.pdf), peer-reviewed, explicitly gives both the uncalibrated BP figure and its cal BC equivalent).
- Individual midden accumulation duration varied: at Druimvargie Rockshelter (Oban) and Caisteal nan Gillean I and Priory Midden (Oronsay), accumulation occurred over several hundred radiocarbon years; at the smaller (8 m across) Ulva Cave midden, accumulation spanned 2,000–3,000 radiocarbon years ([dokumen.pub, *The Archaeology of Argyll*](https://dokumen.pub/the-archaeology-of-argyll-9780585101095.html), citing Russell, Bonsall and Sutherland 1995).
- Detailed radiocarbon dates for Oronsay middens, given in uncalibrated BC form in a doctoral thesis: range from 4,240±80 BC to 3,200±380 BC across sites including Cnoc Coig, Caisteal nan Gillean I and II, Cnoc Sligeach, and Priory Midden ([University of Leeds/White Rose eTheses repository, "An Ecological Interpretation of Mesolithic Shellfish..."](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14735/1/257446_Vol1.pdf), doctoral thesis, university repository — explicitly labeled "Date bc," calibration status not further specified in the excerpt obtained).
- A more recent Bayesian re-analysis of a new excavation (Port Lobh, Colonsay) suggests limpet-shell midden accumulation took place broadly from the latter half of the 5th millennium to the start of the 4th millennium cal BC; median ages for two pairs of radiocarbon determinations are 4,110 and 4,215 cal BC ([Cambridge/*Proceedings of the Prehistoric Society*, "Calling Time on Oronsay: Revising Settlement Models..."](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/proceedings-of-the-prehistoric-society/article/calling-time-on-oronsay-revising-settlement-models-around-the-mesolithicneolithic-transition-in-western-scotland-new-evidence-from-port-lobh-colonsay/FEFA73101D5A06D80641763B4595167D), peer-reviewed, explicit cal bc frame, Bayesian modeling via Bronk Ramsey 2009 methodology). This paper's title itself signals a revision of older Oronsay settlement models.
- Oronsay is described as home to the highest concentration of identified Mesolithic sites in Scotland, dating to sometime between 7,000 and 5,000 years ago ([Canmore/Historic Environment Scotland, "Oronsay, 'cnoc Sligeach'"](https://canmore.org.uk/site/37801/oronsay-cnoc-sligeach") — institutional heritage-agency source, general framing figure only, not a specific excavation-report date).
- The Oronsay/Oban middens occur along the shoreline formed at the maximum of the Main Postglacial Transgression, c. 6,000–7,000 BP, with most sites post-dating or contemporaneous with that maximum ([dokumen.pub, *The Archaeology of Argyll*](https://dokumen.pub/the-archaeology-of-argyll-9780585101095.html)).
**significance**: The Obanian middens are Scotland's principal evidence for Late Mesolithic maritime/shellfish-based subsistence and (like Ertebølle and Muge) are cited as evidence for increased, if regionally variable, sedentism among coastal foragers; the 2019 Port Lobh paper explicitly revises earlier settlement-duration models, making this an active research area.
**sources**: [Bonsall et al., University of Edinburgh repository](https://www.pure.ed.ac.uk/ws/files/539999/2009_BANN_FLAKES_Bonsall_etal.pdf); [dokumen.pub, *The Archaeology of Argyll*](https://dokumen.pub/the-archaeology-of-argyll-9780585101095.html); [White Rose eTheses repository](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14735/1/257446_Vol1.pdf); [Cambridge/PPS, "Calling Time on Oronsay"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/proceedings-of-the-prehistoric-society/article/calling-time-on-oronsay-revising-settlement-models-around-the-mesolithicneolithic-transition-in-western-scotland-new-evidence-from-port-lobh-colonsay/FEFA73101D5A06D80641763B4595167D); [Canmore/Historic Environment Scotland](https://canmore.org.uk/site/37801/oronsay-cnoc-sligeach).
**flags**: ⚑ The 2019 Cambridge/PPS paper explicitly presents itself as "Revising Settlement Models" for Oronsay relative to Mellars' original 1970s dating framework — flagged as a within-region literature revision, though not dated precisely enough by the source itself to assign a single "moved in year X" flag beyond noting the 2019 publication date.

### 5d. What shell middens show about sedentism among foragers

- The scale and duration of midden accumulation (centuries at single locations for Ertebølle, Muge, and the larger Oronsay middens; the Ulva Cave midden spanning 2,000–3,000 radiocarbon years) is used across all three regional literatures as primary evidence for increased residential stability ("complex hunter-gatherer" sedentism) among Late Mesolithic maritime foragers, in contrast to the higher residential mobility inferred for earlier, inland/forest-adapted Mesolithic groups. This is a consistent interpretive thread across the Danish ([VLIZ PDF](https://www.vliz.be/imisdocs/publications/343780.pdf)), Portuguese ([Estudo Geral repository](https://estudogeral.uc.pt/bitstream/10316/45835/1/cias2016_3.pdf)), and Scottish ([dokumen.pub, *Archaeology of Argyll*](https://dokumen.pub/the-archaeology-of-argyll-9780585101095.html)) sources cited above.

## PRIORITY 6: Mesolithic burial and art — Skateholm and Vedbæk

### 6a. Skateholm (Skåne, southern Sweden)

**name**: Skateholm I and Skateholm II cemeteries
**dates**: Radiocarbon dates for the two cemeteries are presented with differing frames across sources, not merged:
- Skateholm I: 6,290±90 BP to 5,930±125 BP (conventional/uncalibrated radiocarbon BP, per the source's own notation); the youngest Late Mesolithic burial date found in this research pass, grave no. 11 at Skateholm I, is given as 6,290±90 BP ([rcin.org.pl, Polish Academy of Sciences digital repository, "Obrządek pogrzebowy" volume](https://rcin.org.pl/Content/58935/PDF/WA308_76462_PIII149_Obrzadek-pogrzebowy_I.pdf), institutional repository, peer-reviewed-adjacent).
- Skateholm II: (6,910±90 BP–) 5,900±100 BP to 5,470±125 BP, i.e., older than Skateholm I overall, per the same source ([rcin.org.pl](https://rcin.org.pl/Content/58935/PDF/WA308_76462_PIII149_Obrzadek-pogrzebowy_I.pdf)).
- Cross-check with calibrated figures: charcoal dates from Skateholm II settlement layers mainly fall between 5,700 and 4,700 cal BC (2σ, calibrated per Stuiver et al. 1998); bone samples from two Skateholm I graves are dated to roughly 5,300–5,000 cal BC, with charcoal dates suggesting an additional burial phase prior to 4,700 cal BC ([Eriksson & Lidén, Stockholm University repository](https://www.su.se/download/18.11777a0b1993704a0586b81/1758028106582/ErikssonLidenJ13_erikssonliden.pdf), peer-reviewed, explicit cal BC / 2σ frame, citing Larsson 1988c).
- A further, distinctly later popular/heritage-tier figure (Megalithic Portal) gives specific individual grave dates of 4,290±85 BC (Lu-1834) and 4,340±95 BC (Lu-1835), framing overall site occupation as "about 4000 BC" ([megalithic.co.uk](https://www.megalithic.co.uk/article.php?sid=32658) — not peer-reviewed; presented only as a specific-grave data point, flagged as lower-tier).
**significance**: Skateholm is one of the largest and most thoroughly studied Mesolithic cemetery complexes in Europe, containing both human and dog burials; it is central to debates about social differentiation, ritual complexity, and group identity among Late Mesolithic (Ertebølle-period) foragers in Scandinavia.
**sources**: [rcin.org.pl repository](https://rcin.org.pl/Content/58935/PDF/WA308_76462_PIII149_Obrzadek-pogrzebowy_I.pdf); [Eriksson & Lidén, Stockholm University repository](https://www.su.se/download/18.11777a0b1993704a0586b81/1758028106582/ErikssonLidenJ13_erikssonliden.pdf); [megalithic.co.uk](https://www.megalithic.co.uk/article.php?sid=32658) (lower-tier, flagged).
**flags**: Sources disagree substantially on whether Skateholm dates to roughly 5,700–4,700 cal BC (Eriksson & Lidén, peer-reviewed) or "about 4000 BC" (Megalithic Portal, non-peer-reviewed) — the peer-reviewed figure is preferred and the popular figure flagged as unreliable rather than merged in.

### 6b. Vedbæk (Henriksholm-Bøgebakken and Vedbæk Boldbaner, Zealand, Denmark)

**name**: Vedbæk-Bøgebakken (Henriksholm-Bøgebakken) cemetery
**dates**: 19 individuals across 23 graves, radiocarbon dated 6,290±75 BP to 5,810±105 BP (conventional/uncalibrated radiocarbon BP, per source notation) ([rcin.org.pl repository](https://rcin.org.pl/Content/58935/PDF/WA308_76462_PIII149_Obrzadek-pogrzebowy_I.pdf), institutional repository).
- Individual burial dates from the original excavation report: Grave 3 (K-2781), 4,100±75 BC; Grave 5 (K-2782), 4,340±75 BC; Grave 14 (K-2784), 3,860±105 BC — all explicitly labeled "uncalibrated" in the source ([academia.edu, "New Information on the Stone Age Graves at Dragsholm, Denmark"](https://www.academia.edu/2589923/NEW_INFORMATION_ON_THE_STONE_AGE_GRAVES_AT_DRAGSHOLM_DENMARK), citing Albrethsen & Brinch Petersen 1977).
- A separate Danish-language technical report gives 12 dates for 17 Vedbæk graves distributed between ca. 5,500 and ca. 4,900 BC (calendar/calibrated years, "f.Kr." = BC, per the source's own framing) and additional feature dates: a hearth/fire feature to 5,710–5,570 BC; a cremation grave (brandgrav N) to 5,600–5,390 BC; the Vedbæk Boldbaner settlement layer radiocarbon-dated 7,210±160 BP (calibrated to 6,240–5,910 BC) ([natmus.dk, National Museum of Denmark technical report on Vedbæk sea-level changes](https://natmus.dk/fileadmin/user_upload/Editor/natmus/bevaringsafdelingen/billeder/M_M/Arkaeobotanisk_rapportoversigt/2014/2014__NNU_15__Havniveauaendringer_6000-3000_f.Kr._i_Vedbaek-omraadet__NOE-Sjaelland_-_fortsatte_geobotaniske_undersoegelser_i_aarene_1982-1990..pdf), institutional/museum source, peer-reviewed-adjacent).
- A directly-dated human bone from Vedbæk-Boldbaner grave 1 (Uppsala lab code Ua-23792) gives 7,115±55 BP, calibrated to 6,050–5,920 BC ([same natmus.dk source](https://natmus.dk/fileadmin/user_upload/Editor/natmus/bevaringsafdelingen/billeder/M_M/Arkaeobotanisk_rapportoversigt/2014/2014__NNU_15__Havniveauaendringer_6000-3000_f.Kr._i_Vedbaek-omraadet__NOE-Sjaelland_-_fortsatte_geobotaniske_undersoegelser_i_aarene_1982-1990..pdf)).
- A comparative Scandinavian skeletal-material study notes that bone preservation prevented direct radiocarbon dating of most Vedbæk-Bøgebakken individuals; a separate diagnostic-lithics date of c. 6,650 BP is given for associated Ertebølle material ([Cambridge/*Proceedings of the Prehistoric Society*, "Strøby Egede, Vedbæk-Bøgebakken and Relationships among Scandinavian Mesolithic Skeletal Material"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/proceedings-of-the-prehistoric-society/article/stroby-egede-vedbaekbogebakken-and-relationships-among-scandinavian-mesolithic-skeletal-material/C9DE161B90C64672BD84E24900686AC2), peer-reviewed).
**significance**: Vedbæk-Bøgebakken is one of the best-known Mesolithic cemeteries in Europe, notable for a grave containing a mother buried with an infant laid on a swan's wing, widely cited as evidence for symbolic/ritual behavior and emotionally invested mortuary practice among Ertebølle-period foragers.
**sources**: [rcin.org.pl repository](https://rcin.org.pl/Content/58935/PDF/WA308_76462_PIII149_Obrzadek-pogrzebowy_I.pdf); [academia.edu, Dragsholm graves paper citing Albrethsen & Brinch Petersen 1977](https://www.academia.edu/2589923/NEW_INFORMATION_ON_THE_STONE_AGE_GRAVES_AT_DRAGSHOLM_DENMARK); [natmus.dk technical report](https://natmus.dk/fileadmin/user_upload/Editor/natmus/bevaringsafdelingen/billeder/M_M/Arkaeobotanisk_rapportoversigt/2014/2014__NNU_15__Havniveauaendringer_6000-3000_f.Kr._i_Vedbaek-omraadet__NOE-Sjaelland_-_fortsatte_geobotaniske_undersoegelser_i_aarene_1982-1990..pdf); [Cambridge/PPS comparative skeletal study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/proceedings-of-the-prehistoric-society/article/stroby-egede-vedbaekbogebakken-and-relationships-among-scandinavian-mesolithic-skeletal-material/C9DE161B90C64672BD84E24900686AC2).
**flags**: Uncalibrated (K-lab conventional BP/BC) and calibrated (cal BC) figures are both present in the source record for Vedbæk and are kept explicitly separate above rather than merged, per sourcing rules — note the ~600–1,000 year gap between raw uncalibrated BC lab dates (e.g., 4,100 BC uncalibrated) and calibrated calendar dates (e.g., 5,500–4,900 BC calibrated) for broadly the same cemetery, illustrating why calibration status must always be stated.

### 6c. Social organization implications

- Both Skateholm and Vedbæk are used in the literature as primary evidence for social differentiation among Late Mesolithic (Ertebølle-period) foragers — formal cemeteries with grave goods, evidence of status/role differences, and (at Skateholm) associated dog burials are cited as markers of increased social complexity accompanying the sedentism documented at contemporary shell middens (Priority 5). A broader Scandinavian comparative framework situates Skateholm and Vedbæk alongside other Ertebølle-period cemeteries (e.g., Strøby Egede) as part of a shared regional mortuary tradition ([Cambridge/PPS comparative skeletal study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/proceedings-of-the-prehistoric-society/article/stroby-egede-vedbaekbogebakken-and-relationships-among-scandinavian-mesolithic-skeletal-material/C9DE161B90C64672BD84E24900686AC2), peer-reviewed).
- A broader synthesis notes that radiocarbon dating of prehistoric hunter-gatherer cemeteries, including Vedbæk and Skateholm, has historically been "rather limited in scope and primarily focused on confirming broad placement within a period" rather than fine-grained chronological resolution — flagged here as a methodological limitation acknowledged in the recent literature ([Bronk Ramsey et al., "Spatio-temporal patterns of cemetery use among Middle Holocene hunter-gatherers of Cis-Baikal, Eastern Siberia"](https://baikalproject.artsrn.ualberta.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/Bronk-Ramsey-et-al_2021_ARA_Spatio-temporal-patterns-of-cemetery-use.pdf), peer-reviewed, cited for this methodological point though its primary subject is Siberian, not European).
- El Collado (Spain), cited for comparative context: over 300 Mesolithic individuals are known from southwestern European Mesolithic cemeteries collectively, dated to the period 8,500–7,110 cal BP ([PLOS ONE, "The Emergence of Mesolithic Cemeteries in SW Europe"](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0115505), peer-reviewed) — included here to show that Skateholm/Vedbæk are part of a wider pan-European Mesolithic cemetery phenomenon rather than isolated northern anomalies.

**flags**: None beyond the calibration-status flag already noted under 6b.

## PRIORITY 7: South Asia term usage and Mesolithic–Neolithic persistence vs. replacement (time-permitting priority)

### 7a. South Asia — already substantially covered under Priority 1; key additional point

- The core South Asian terminological problem (conflation of "microlith" with "Mesolithic," now undermined by Pleistocene-age South Asian microliths) is documented at [PMC6774521](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6774521/) and the [Mesolithic Sindh dissertation](https://iris.unive.it/bitstream/10278/19301/1/MesoSindhPraehistoria.pdf) — see Priority 1 above for full detail; not re-derived here to avoid duplication.

### 7b. Mesolithic–Neolithic relationship: replaced vs. persisted, by region

**name**: Regional variation in Mesolithic hunter-gatherer replacement vs. persistence following the arrival of farming
**findings, presented as explicit regional contrasts (field position varies by region — not a single global answer)**:

- **Western/Central Europe (core Linear Pottery Culture, LBK, zone)**: genome-wide ancient-DNA studies show that in Western and Central Europe, hunter-gatherers were "largely replaced" by incoming farmers of Anatolian origin, with changes occurring "rapidly and synchronously" after farmers' arrival ([PubMed, "Paleogenomic Evidence for Multi-generational Mixing between Neolithic Farmers and Mesolithic Hunter-Gatherers in the Lower Danube Basin"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28552360/?dopt=Abstract), peer-reviewed, referencing prior studies 1–6 within its own citation list). A separate ancient-DNA study of Poland similarly reports an "overall population turnover pattern in which migrating farmers... largely replaced autochthonous Mesolithic hunter-gatherers," while also documenting "the co-existence of unadmixed hunter-gatherers as late as 4300 BCE" and "recurrent hunter-farmer admixture over three millennia" — i.e., replacement was the dominant pattern but was neither instantaneous nor total ([Nature Scientific Reports, "A genomic Neolithic time transect of hunter-farmer admixture in central Poland"](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-33067-w), peer-reviewed).
- **Blätterhöhle, Germany (a specific, well-documented persistence case)**: combined ancient DNA and isotopic data show two occupation phases — Mesolithic, 9,210–8,340 cal BCE, and Neolithic, 3,986–2,918 cal BCE — with "persuasive evidence for the prolonged coexistence of genetically distinct hunter-gatherer and farming groups over the course of the Neolithic in Central Europe," indicating "descendants of Mesolithic people maintained a foraging lifestyle in Central Europe for more than 2,000 years after the arrival of farming societies," in a "well-defined cultural boundary" alongside contemporary Neolithic farmers ([Science.org, peer-reviewed report PDF](https://www.science.org/cms/asset/6627f101-f92f-4c45-97d2-9e106bdd77fd/pap.pdf)).
- **Baltic region**: genomic data show hunter-gatherer populations here adopted farming "without a significant genetic input from farming populations," i.e., a gradual, largely indigenous cultural transition rather than population replacement ([PubMed, Lower Danube Basin paper](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28552360/?dopt=Abstract), peer-reviewed, and cross-confirmed in [Nature Scientific Reports, Poland transect paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-33067-w)).
- **Portugal (Tagus/Sado, Muge middens — see Priority 5b)**: explicit "resilience, replacement, and acculturation" framing — the Tagus Valley saw "an important drastic dynamic process with the replacement of hunter-gatherers by farmers" around 7,500 years ago, but there was also "an overlap in the region... of a few hundred years between the Muge Mesolithic and the early Neolithic populations," which is characterized in this source as a case of successful Mesolithic "resilience" via absorption into Neolithic farming groups rather than simple extinction ([Estudo Geral/University of Coimbra repository, "Resilience, replacement and acculturation in the Mesolithic/Neolithic"](https://estudogeral.sib.uc.pt/bitstream/10316/45835/1/cias2016_3.pdf), peer-reviewed).
- **Atlantic France**: a similar forager–farmer overlap to the Portuguese case is documented, but "nearly a millennium earlier" ([PMC10927556, "Hunter-gatherers and earliest farmers in western Europe"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10927556/), peer-reviewed, discussing Simões et al. dating evidence).
- **Northwest Africa/general outlying-Europe pattern**: a broad genomic study concludes that the initial transition to agriculture in central Europe resulted from migrating Near-Eastern/Anatolian farmers who "largely replaced or assimilated" indigenous hunter-gatherers in the "core region" of southeast and Central Europe, but that "hunter-gatherer populations survived in outlying regions [Scandinavia, the Baltic, European Russia, Ukraine] and adopted some of the cultural practices from neighbouring farming communities" instead of being replaced — a direct core-vs-periphery contrast ([ScienceDaily summary of a peer-reviewed genomic study, "Origins of farming in Europe result of human migration and cultural change"](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/02/110222192828.htm) — this is a science-news summary of a peer-reviewed paper rather than the primary paper itself; the core empirical claim is corroborated independently by the Poland and Blätterhöhle papers above, so it is retained but flagged as a secondary summary).
- A very recent (2025) study finds that increases in hunter-gatherer admixture with Neolithic farmers across continental Europe took place over "a long time period (~1,750 years)," "consistent with the prolonged coexistence estimated in some parts of central and northern Europe" — reinforcing that gradual, prolonged coexistence (rather than instantaneous replacement) is now the more commonly documented pattern across much of Europe, even where eventual genetic replacement did occur ([Science.org, "Local increases in admixture with hunter-gatherers followed the initial expansion of Neolithic farmers across continental Europe"](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adq9976), peer-reviewed, 2025).
- **South Asia** (returning briefly to this priority's other component): as documented under Priority 1, the Allchin et al. (1978) source explicitly states that in South Asia, Mesolithic-type hunting groups sometimes overlapped in time with settled agricultural or even urban communities, meaning "Mesolithic" cannot be treated as a period that was cleanly "replaced" by a subsequent "Neolithic" there in the same chronologically bounded way as in much of Europe ([Mesolithic Sindh dissertation](https://iris.unive.it/bitstream/10278/19301/1/MesoSindhPraehistoria.pdf)).

**significance**: The "replaced vs. persisted" question does not have a single answer across the Mesolithic world (or even across Europe) — the ancient-DNA/genomic revolution of the past decade has moved the field from an older, simpler "replacement" narrative toward a much more regionally variable picture with well-documented pockets of multi-century to multi-millennium coexistence.

**⚑ LITERATURE MOVED**: The shift from a dominant "replacement" narrative toward the current emphasis on regionally variable coexistence, admixture timing, and forager persistence is largely a product of ancient-DNA studies from the 2010s through 2025 (Blätterhöhle 2014-era paper republished/summarized via Science.org; Poland 2018; the 2025 Science Advances admixture-timing paper). This should be flagged as an ongoing, active literature shift rather than a settled consensus.
