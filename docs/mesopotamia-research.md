## SECTION 1: THE URUK PERIOD

### 1.1 Uruk period (overall)

- **Name:** Uruk period
- **Dates:** Two competing absolute frameworks currently coexist in the literature (present both, do not average):
  - **CDLI framework:** 4000–3000 BC overall; Late Uruk sub-span 3600–3200 BC ([CDLI Wiki, "Uruk (modern Warka)"](https://cdli.ox.ac.uk/wiki/doku.php?id=uruk_mod._warka)).
  - **ARCANE/radiocarbon framework:** Early/Middle Uruk (levels XIII–IX) 4200–3800 BC; Late Uruk 3800–3000 BC, with the Uruk IVa Temple C radiocarbon samples clustering cal. 3510–3370 BC / cal. 3500–3390 BC (centered ~3450 BC) ([Sallaberger & Schrakamp, ARCANE Vol. 1 chronology PDF](https://www.assyriologie.uni-muenchen.de/personen/professoren/sallaberger/publ_sallaberger/wasa_schrakamp_2015_arcane1.pdf)). This source explicitly notes that the older, frequently-quoted "2815 BC"-style figure for Uruk IVa rests on uncalibrated or incorrect radiocarbon readings.
  - **UNICODE technical report sub-phasing** (a third, commonly cited scheme, used for script periodization specifically): Uruk V ca. 3500–3350 BCE; Uruk IV ca. 3350–3200 BCE; Uruk III ca. 3200–3000 BCE ([Unicode Technical Report, Proto-Cuneiform, 2019](https://www.unicode.org/L2/L2019/19284-proto-cuneiform.pdf)).
  - **ISAC/Oriental Institute LC framework:** Middle Uruk = LC-4, 3700(3850)–3400 BC; Late Uruk = LC-5, 3400–3100 BC ([ISAC/Oriental Institute, Surezha excavation report](https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/ar/11-20/13-14/ar2013-14_Surezha.pdf)).
  - Dating basis: mix of calibrated radiocarbon (ARCANE, ISAC) and archaeological-typological/stratigraphic sequencing (CDLI, Unicode).
- **Significance:** The Uruk period marks the first appearance of large urban centers, monumental temple architecture (the Eanna precinct), state-level administration, and — in its final centuries — writing, in southern Mesopotamia.
- **Sources:** [CDLI Wiki, Uruk (modern Warka)](https://cdli.ox.ac.uk/wiki/doku.php?id=uruk_mod._warka); [ARCANE Vol. 1 chronology (Sallaberger & Schrakamp 2015)](https://www.assyriologie.uni-muenchen.de/personen/professoren/sallaberger/publ_sallaberger/wasa_schrakamp_2015_arcane1.pdf); [Unicode Technical Report on Proto-Cuneiform (2019)](https://www.unicode.org/L2/L2019/19284-proto-cuneiform.pdf); [ISAC/Oriental Institute, Surezha excavation report](https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/ar/11-20/13-14/ar2013-14_Surezha.pdf).
- **Flags:** ⚑ Do NOT average the CDLI (4000–3000 BC), ARCANE (4200–3000 BC), and Unicode (3500–3000 BC) frameworks — they use different dating methods and different level-correlation assumptions. COMMON MISCONCEPTION: older literature (pre-recalibration) quotes Uruk IVa to as early as ~2815 BC using now-superseded, uncalibrated radiocarbon dates; current calibrated readings push this back to ~3450 BC ([ARCANE PDF](https://www.assyriologie.uni-muenchen.de/personen/professoren/sallaberger/publ_sallaberger/wasa_schrakamp_2015_arcane1.pdf)).

### 1.2 Early, Middle, and Late Uruk sub-phases

- **Name:** Early Uruk / Middle Uruk / Late Uruk
- **Dates:**
  - CDLI: does not give separate absolute dates for levels VIII–IV (all grouped under "Late Uruk period," 3600–3200 BC); Uruk V, IVb, IVa, and III are given no independent absolute dates, only relative stratigraphic position ([CDLI Wiki, Uruk](https://cdli.ox.ac.uk/wiki/doku.php?id=uruk_mod._warka); [CDLI Wiki, "The Late Uruk Period"](https://cdli.ox.ac.uk/wiki/doku.php?id=the_late_uruk_period)).
  - ARCANE: Early/Middle Uruk (levels XIII–IX) 4200–3800 BC; Late Uruk (levels VIII–…) 3800–3000 BC ([ARCANE PDF](https://www.assyriologie.uni-muenchen.de/personen/professoren/sallaberger/publ_sallaberger/wasa_schrakamp_2015_arcane1.pdf)).
  - ISAC/Surezha: LC-4 (≈Middle Uruk) 3700–3400 BC (with an earlier LC-4 sub-phase 3850–3700 BC); LC-5 (≈Late Uruk) 3400–3100 BC ([ISAC Surezha report](https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/ar/11-20/13-14/ar2013-14_Surezha.pdf)).
  - Dating basis: archaeological-typological (stratigraphy, ceramic/script phase sequencing) for CDLI; calibrated radiocarbon for ARCANE/ISAC.
- **Significance:** The tripartite division tracks the internal evolution of Eanna temple architecture and, in its last (Late Uruk) phase, the appearance of proto-cuneiform.
- **Sources:** [CDLI Wiki, Uruk (modern Warka)](https://cdli.ox.ac.uk/wiki/doku.php?id=uruk_mod._warka); [CDLI Wiki, The Late Uruk Period](https://cdli.ox.ac.uk/wiki/doku.php?id=the_late_uruk_period); [ARCANE Vol. 1 (Sallaberger & Schrakamp 2015)](https://www.assyriologie.uni-muenchen.de/personen/professoren/sallaberger/publ_sallaberger/wasa_schrakamp_2015_arcane1.pdf); [ISAC Surezha excavation report](https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/ar/11-20/13-14/ar2013-14_Surezha.pdf).
- **Flags:** CDLI explicitly states it does **not** provide separate absolute calendar dates for individual Eanna architectural levels (V, IVb, IVa, III) — treat any source that assigns precise calendar years to these sub-levels with caution; that precision is not present in the CDLI primary reference.

### 1.3 City of Uruk — size and urbanization

- **Name:** Uruk (city), size progression
- **Dates / figures (CDLI, the only fetched source giving an explicit, sourced progression):**
  - Before the Uruk period: surrounding sites mostly under 10 ha.
  - Start of the Uruk period: Uruk expands to **70 hectares**.
  - End of the Uruk period: Uruk reaches about **100 hectares**, described by CDLI as "the largest site in Mesopotamia," with more than half of southern Mesopotamia's settled area located in its vicinity.
  - Early Dynastic period (2900–2350 BC): Uruk continues growing to about **400 hectares**.
  - ([CDLI Wiki, Uruk (modern Warka)](https://cdli.ox.ac.uk/wiki/doku.php?id=uruk_mod._warka)) — dating basis: archaeological survey/site-size mapping, not independently dated by radiocarbon on this page.
- **Contrasting/larger figures found (not from a fetched primary excavation source — flag as unverified against a fetched primary source):** Wikipedia's "Uruk" and "Uruk period" pages (locator only, NOT a dating authority) state a range of larger figures depending on which phase/wall is meant: up to 250 ha by ~3100 BC, growing to 600 ha in the following Jemdet Nasr period, and separately up to 300 ha with ~40,000 inhabitants in an unspecified period ([Wikipedia, "Uruk"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uruk) — locator only; [Wikipedia, "Uruk period"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uruk_period) — locator only, giving 230–250 ha and 25,000–50,000 people for Late Uruk specifically). A secondary popular-archaeology site independently states ~550 hectares at maximum extent ([atlasanatolia.com](https://atlasanatolia.com/site/uruk) — not an institutional/peer-reviewed source, use cautiously).
  - **n.a.** — No fetched institutional/peer-reviewed excavation report was located in this pass that confirms a precise population figure (e.g., "40,000 residents") with an explicit dating basis. Population estimates found are uncorroborated by a primary source and are marked `n.a.` for confirmed sourcing.
- **City wall:** CDLI states the ~9-km mud-brick city wall was "probably first constructed" in the Early Dynastic I period (first half of the 3rd millennium BC), a date CDLI explicitly says is **inferred from a cylinder seal impression**, not from direct excavation of the wall's foundation — CDLI flags this dating as not established through thorough excavation ([CDLI Wiki, Uruk](https://cdli.ox.ac.uk/wiki/doku.php?id=uruk_mod._warka)).
- **Significance:** By the end of the Uruk period, Uruk's ~100 ha extent (per CDLI) made it, by a wide margin, the largest settlement yet known in Mesopotamia to that date — a landmark in the emergence of urbanism.
- **Sources:** [CDLI Wiki, Uruk (modern Warka)](https://cdli.ox.ac.uk/wiki/doku.php?id=uruk_mod._warka); [Wikipedia, "Uruk" — locator only](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uruk); [Wikipedia, "Uruk period" — locator only](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uruk_period).
- **Flags:** ⚑ Numbers for Uruk's maximum size range widely across sources (100 ha per CDLI at end of Uruk period vs. 250–600 ha in various Wikipedia figures vs. ~550 ha in a non-institutional secondary source) — these are NOT reconciled in the fetched sources and should not be averaged; CDLI's 100 ha figure is the only one traceable to an institutional/academic source in this pass. Population figures (e.g., "40,000 inhabitants") are `n.a.` for primary-source confirmation.

### 1.4 The Eanna precinct

- **Name:** Eanna precinct ("House of Heaven"), sacred precinct of Uruk dedicated to Inanna
- **Dates:** CDLI states the Eanna precinct was Uruk's most prominent area throughout the Uruk period, with construction/rebuilding across multiple stratigraphic levels: Uruk V (Limestone Temple, tripartite plan; limestone quarried 80 km away on the west side of the Euphrates) → Uruk IVb (Mosaic Court, Square Building, Stone Cone Mosaic Temple) → Uruk IVa (Temple D [80×50 m], Temple C, Pillared Hall, Great Court, the "Riemchengebäude") → Ur III period rebuilding (**2100–2000 BC**, per CDLI's dating for the Ur III period specifically), which produced the ziggurat still visible today ([CDLI Wiki, Uruk (modern Warka)](https://cdli.ox.ac.uk/wiki/doku.php?id=uruk_mod._warka)). CDLI explicitly states it does **not** provide separate absolute calendar dates for the individual Eanna building levels (V, IVb, IVa) themselves — only their relative stratigraphic order and their assignment to the Ur III period for the final ziggurat.
- **Significance:** Eanna is the best-documented sequence of monumental religious/administrative architecture in 4th-millennium Mesopotamia and the findspot of the earliest known writing (~5,000 archaic tablets, mostly from rubbish/fill deposits).
- **Sources:** [CDLI Wiki, Uruk (modern Warka)](https://cdli.ox.ac.uk/wiki/doku.php?id=uruk_mod._warka).
- **Flags:** COMMON MISCONCEPTION: popular sources sometimes assign precise calendar dates (e.g., "White Temple, c. 3000 BC") to individual Eanna buildings; the primary CDLI reference used here explicitly declines to give such dates and treats these as stratigraphically, not absolutely, dated.

### 1.5 Uruk expansion into Syria/Anatolia — colonization vs. trade debate

- **Name:** "Uruk expansion" / "Uruk world system"
- **Dates:** Framed variously as ca. 3800–3100 BC ([American Journal of Archaeology, TOC 1996 issue on the Uruk expansion](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/toc/aja/1996/100/2)) or, per a course-material source flagging the "Santa Fe chronology" revision, as beginning already in the Middle Uruk period and lasting 400–500 years rather than the originally proposed 150–200 years ([Masaryk University course slides, "Uruk Lec 6" — course handout, use cautiously, not a primary source](https://is.muni.cz/el/phil/jaro2013/PAPVA_49/um/39400680/Uruk_Lec_6.pdf)). At the site level: Hacınebi's Late Chalcolithic occupation runs ~4100–3300 BC by calibrated radiocarbon, with Uruk-related contact beginning in the later Middle Uruk (LC4) and continuing into Late Uruk (LC5) ([SAR Press, Uruk chapter 8 PDF](https://sarpress.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/sar_press_uruk_mesopotamia_chapter8.pdf)). Jebel Aruda (Syria) produced calibrated radiocarbon dates of 3335–3103 BC, 3333–3101 BC, and 3315–2916 BC from charcoal, and is assigned to the Uruk V period (~3500–3350 BC) — Wikipedia locator only, non-Wikipedia radiocarbon confirmation not yet located, mark basis as `n.a.` pending a primary excavation report ([Wikipedia, "Jebel Aruda" — locator only](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jebel_Aruda) — not independently fetched/confirmed in this pass, use with caution).
- **The colonization-vs-trade debate (present both positions, do not average):**
  - **Colonization position:** Algaze's "Uruk World System" (1993) argues for outright colonization of the Susiana plains and beyond by Mesopotamian settlers, based on convergence in material culture ([Algaze, "The Uruk World System," 1993, cited via Internet Archive scan](https://ia803202.us.archive.org/31/items/AlgazeGuillermo1993TheUrukWorldSystem/Algaze,%20Guillermo_1993_The%20Uruk%20World%20System_text.pdf)). Postgate similarly describes "the world's earliest colonial network," citing Algaze 1993, Stein 1999b, and Sürenhagen 1986 ([Postgate, "Artefacts of Complexity," 2002](https://www.bisi.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/publications/postgate_artefacts_of_complexity_2002_0.pdf)). Isotope analysis of bitumen at some enclave sites is cited as supporting settlement/colonization over simple exchange ([scispace PDF, "The Uruk Expansion as Dynamic Process"](https://scispace.com/pdf/the-uruk-expansion-as-dynamic-process-a-reconstruction-of-dpkmdo7lb0.pdf)).
  - **Trade/peaceful-exchange position:** At Hacınebi, excavators found no evidence of Uruk colonial domination or warfare — instead a pattern of peaceful exchange with essential parity between Mesopotamian merchants and local Anatolians, explicitly contrasted with later European colonialism ([American Journal of Archaeology, TOC 1996](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/toc/aja/1996/100/2)).
  - **Alternative "displaced peoples" position:** Johnson (1989) and Schwartz (2001), as cited secondarily, interpret Mesopotamian material culture in Anatolia as evidence of displaced populations founding agricultural/pastoral settlements rather than deliberate trading colonies ([scispace PDF](https://scispace.com/pdf/the-uruk-expansion-as-dynamic-process-a-reconstruction-of-dpkmdo7lb0.pdf)).
  - **Skeptical/rejectionist position:** A 2020 UCL doctoral thesis states explicitly there is "no evidence that the Uruk settlements represent colonization, as in the European systems of recent centuries. Nor were they founded primarily as trading centres... or created through military domination" ([UCL Discovery, "Social ideology and the Uruk phenomenon," 2020](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10116062/)).
  - **Independent local-development position (post-2020 redating, see also §1.6):** the 2024 Antiquity paper on excavations at Shakhi Kora (Sirwan/Upper Diyala, Iraqi Kurdistan) argues local communities in this region built large-scale settlements and public institutions **independent of, and prior to,** intensified contact with the Uruk world, and that where southern Uruk-associated settlers did establish colony sites, they integrated into local communities to varying degrees "over which they did not exert economic or other forms of control" ([Glatz et al., "There and back again," Antiquity, 2024/2025, Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/there-and-back-again-local-institutions-an-uruk-expansion-and-the-rejection-of-centralisation-in-the-sirwanupper-diyala-region/B4346D8FD24BB63BA2B58B236FEB28CD)).
- **Significance:** Whether the "Uruk expansion" represented colonization, exchange-driven trade diaspora, displaced migration, or largely independent local development remains a live, unresolved question with direct implications for how "state formation" and "empire" are defined for the 4th millennium BC.
- **Sources:** [AJA TOC 1996 (Hacınebi)](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/toc/aja/1996/100/2); [Postgate, "Artefacts of Complexity" (2002)](https://www.bisi.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/publications/postgate_artefacts_of_complexity_2002_0.pdf); [Algaze, "The Uruk World System" (1993)](https://ia803202.us.archive.org/31/items/AlgazeGuillermo1993TheUrukWorldSystem/Algaze,%20Guillermo_1993_The%20Uruk%20World%20System_text.pdf); [scispace, "Uruk Expansion as Dynamic Process"](https://scispace.com/pdf/the-uruk-expansion-as-dynamic-process-a-reconstruction-of-dpkmdo7lb0.pdf); [UCL Discovery thesis (2020)](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10116062/); [SAR Press Uruk chapter 8](https://sarpress.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/sar_press_uruk_mesopotamia_chapter8.pdf); [Glatz et al., Antiquity 2024/2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/there-and-back-again-local-institutions-an-uruk-expansion-and-the-rejection-of-centralisation-in-the-sirwanupper-diyala-region/B4346D8FD24BB63BA2B58B236FEB28CD); [phys.org summary of Glatz et al.](https://phys.org/news/2024-11-excavations-uncover-evidence-emergence-earliest.pdf).
- **Flags:** The field genuinely disagrees — colonization (Algaze/Postgate), peaceful trade/parity (Hacınebi excavators), displaced-migration (Johnson/Schwartz), and rejectionist/independent-development (UCL thesis; Glatz et al. 2024) positions all currently have active scholarly standing. Do not present any one as settled consensus.

### 1.6 ⚑ Post-2020 redating on Uruk chronology / expansion

- **Name:** Post-2020 revisions to Uruk chronology and the Uruk expansion model
- **Dates / findings:**
  - **2020:** ScienceDirect paper on Gurga Chiya uses petrographic/geochemical data to argue against a simple "expansion" framing of Uruk-period material culture spread ([ScienceDirect, Gurga Chiya paper, 2020-10-19](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352409X20303072)).
  - **2023:** Renette, Lewis, Wencel, Farahani & Tomé publish the first stratigraphically anchored absolute radiocarbon chronology for the entire Late Chalcolithic through earliest Early Bronze Age sequence at a single continuously-occupied site (Kani Shaie, Iraqi Kurdistan), spanning the late Ubaid (early 5th millennium BCE) through the early 3rd millennium BCE ([Renette et al., "Establishing an Absolute Chronological Framework...Radiocarbon Dates from Kani Shaie," Radiocarbon 65(1), 209–231, 2023, Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/radiocarbon/article/establishing-an-absolute-chronological-framework-for-the-late-chalcolithic-to-early-bronze-age-in-iraqi-kurdistan-radiocarbon-dates-from-kani-shaie/01EFB083A77141A98ABE99434322BC27)). A related academia.edu-hosted version of this ceramic/radiocarbon work notes this "hints at long-term co-development of material culture during the fourth millennium B.C. in southeastern Iraqi Kurdistan and central and southern Iraq, potentially questioning the model of expansion or colonialism from the south" ([Late Chalcolithic Ceramic Development in Southern Iraqi Kurdistan, academia.edu-hosted preprint](https://www.academia.edu/83674297/Late_Chalcolithic_Ceramic_Development_in_Southern_Iraqi_Kurdistan_The_Stratigraphic_Sounding_at_Kani_Shaie) — note: academia.edu hosting, treat the analytical claims as provisional pending the peer-reviewed Radiocarbon version).
  - **2024:** Glatz et al. publish new excavation results from Shakhi Kora (Sirwan/Upper Diyala) in Antiquity, explicitly reframing the "Uruk expansion" — arguing local institutions developed and were later deliberately abandoned "without signs of violent overthrow or environmental stress," pointing to rejection of centralization rather than passive absorption into a Uruk system ([Glatz et al., Antiquity, DOI 10.15184/aqy.2024.189](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/there-and-back-again-local-institutions-an-uruk-expansion-and-the-rejection-of-centralisation-in-the-sirwanupper-diyala-region/B4346D8FD24BB63BA2B58B236FEB28CD); [phys.org coverage, 2024-12-04](https://phys.org/news/2024-11-excavations-uncover-evidence-emergence-earliest.pdf)).
  - **2024:** A companion Cambridge Archaeological Journal paper by (overlapping) authors explicitly titled "Reframing the Uruk Expansion: Glocalization and Local Dynamics in the Late Chalcolithic Adhaim/Sirwan Drainage Basin, Iraqi Kurdistan" continues this reassessment ([Cambridge Core PDF](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/6D0017216B86F0F5084F8BD4C2504A9A/S0959774324000404a.pdf/reframing-the-uruk-expansion-glocalization-and-local-dynamics-in-the-late-chalcolithic-adhaim-sirwan-drainage-basin-iraqi-kurdistan.pdf)).
  - **2024:** A November 2024 Antiquity news piece, "Rolling back the origins of writing," reports that cylinder-seal motifs dated c. 4400–3400 BC may be direct precursors to specific proto-cuneiform signs, pushing back the origin of some sign-forms (though not the writing system itself, still dated to the standard 3350–3000 BC proto-cuneiform window) ([Antiquity news, 2024-11-06](https://www.antiquity.ac.uk/news/2024/rolling-back-origins-writing); corroborated by [CNN coverage, 2024-11-05](https://www.cnn.com/2024/11/05/science/origins-of-writing-cuneiform)).
- **Significance:** Since 2020, excavation and radiocarbon work in Iraqi Kurdistan (Kani Shaie, Shakhi Kora, Gurga Chiya) has shifted the "Uruk expansion" narrative away from a one-directional, Uruk-driven colonization model toward models emphasizing local agency, independent institutional development, and eventual local rejection of centralization.
- **Sources:** [ScienceDirect, Gurga Chiya (2020)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352409X20303072); [Renette et al., Radiocarbon 65(1) (2023)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/radiocarbon/article/establishing-an-absolute-chronological-framework-for-the-late-chalcolithic-to-early-bronze-age-in-iraqi-kurdistan-radiocarbon-dates-from-kani-shaie/01EFB083A77141A98ABE99434322BC27); [Glatz et al., Antiquity (2024)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/there-and-back-again-local-institutions-an-uruk-expansion-and-the-rejection-of-centralisation-in-the-sirwanupper-diyala-region/B4346D8FD24BB63BA2B58B236FEB28CD); [phys.org (2024-12-04)](https://phys.org/news/2024-11-excavations-uncover-evidence-emergence-earliest.pdf); [Cambridge Archaeological Journal, "Reframing the Uruk Expansion" (2024)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/6D0017216B86F0F5084F8BD4C2504A9A/S0959774324000404a.pdf/reframing-the-uruk-expansion-glocalization-and-local-dynamics-in-the-late-chalcolithic-adhaim-sirwan-drainage-basin-iraqi-kurdistan.pdf); [Antiquity news, "Rolling back the origins of writing" (2024-11-06)](https://www.antiquity.ac.uk/news/2024/rolling-back-origins-writing).
- **Flags:** ⚑ LITERATURE MOVED (2020–2024): the dominant framing of the "Uruk expansion" as Mesopotamian-driven colonization is being actively revised by Iraqi Kurdistan fieldwork toward models of local agency and independent development. This is an active research front, not a settled revision.

## SECTION 2: THE INVENTION OF WRITING

### 2.1 Proto-cuneiform — earliest dating

- **Name:** Proto-cuneiform
- **Dates:** Peer-reviewed 2025 Antiquity article states proto-cuneiform is first attested on tablets at Uruk **c. 3350–3000 BC** (Uruk IV and III script phases), with the earliest tablets belonging to Eanna level IVa (≈Late Chalcolithic LC-5) ([Cambridge/Antiquity, "Seals and Signs: Tracing the Origins of Writing in Ancient Southwest Asia," 2025-02-18](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/seals-and-signs-tracing-the-origins-of-writing-in-ancient-southwest-asia/B3C2D400F3F80A7A0162D9035C9C2804)). This is corroborated by the November 2024 Antiquity news coverage of the same underlying research program ([Antiquity news, 2024-11-06](https://www.antiquity.ac.uk/news/2024/rolling-back-origins-writing)) and by CNN's report that "this script first emerged between 3350 and 3000 BC in the ancient city of Uruk" ([CNN, 2024-11-05](https://www.cnn.com/2024/11/05/science/origins-of-writing-cuneiform)).
  - CDLI's own periodization: earliest writing "probably dates to the latest subphase of [Eanna] Level IV, IVa"; a second phase of writing is dated to Level III / the Jemdet Nasr period. CDLI states explicitly that this dating rests on **stratigraphic inference, not direct excavation context** — the archaic tablets were found reused as pit-fill beneath Level III construction, so they must predate Level III; no tablets were found in layers below Level IV, so writing is placed at Level IV at the earliest ([CDLI Wiki, "The Late Uruk Period"](https://cdli.ox.ac.uk/wiki/doku.php?id=the_late_uruk_period); [CDLI Wiki, "Uruk (modern Warka)"](https://cdli.ox.ac.uk/wiki/doku.php?id=uruk_mod._warka)). CDLI further states that because no direct stratigraphic link exists between individual tablets and specific sub-phases, dating within the Level IV–III sequence is additionally based on **paleographic/script-evolution criteria** (the internal evolution of sign forms), not stratigraphy alone.
  - British Museum object record for an early cuneiform tablet gives production date **3300 BC–3000 BC** for Late Uruk material, while separately noting some Museum tablets of "developing cuneiform script, still mainly pictographic" are dated to around 2900 BC based on excavation parallels ([British Museum collection record, object W_1899-0418-2](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1899-0418-2)).
  - Dating basis: archaeological-typological/stratigraphic (CDLI, British Museum) and paleographic/script-evolution (CDLI); the 2025 Antiquity paper's basis is not separately restated beyond its stated Uruk IV/III range.
- **Significance:** Proto-cuneiform is the earliest known writing system, developing directly out of a token- and numerical-tablet-based accounting tradition (see §2.2–2.3) into a system of numerical + logographic signs used almost exclusively for administrative accounting in its earliest phase.
- **Sources:** [Antiquity/Cambridge, "Seals and Signs" (2025)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/seals-and-signs-tracing-the-origins-of-writing-in-ancient-southwest-asia/B3C2D400F3F80A7A0162D9035C9C2804); [CDLI Wiki, "The Late Uruk Period"](https://cdli.ox.ac.uk/wiki/doku.php?id=the_late_uruk_period); [CDLI Wiki, "Uruk (modern Warka)"](https://cdli.ox.ac.uk/wiki/doku.php?id=uruk_mod._warka); [British Museum, object W_1899-0418-2](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1899-0418-2); [Antiquity news, "Rolling back the origins of writing" (2024)](https://www.antiquity.ac.uk/news/2024/rolling-back-origins-writing); [CNN (2024-11-05)](https://www.cnn.com/2024/11/05/science/origins-of-writing-cuneiform).
- **Flags:** COMMON MISCONCEPTION: writing is sometimes said to have been found "in situ" in a datable administrative context. CDLI explicitly states nearly 5,000 archaic tablets from the earliest writing phase were excavated from **rubbish dumps and fill deposits**, not primary use-contexts — their date is inferred indirectly (via stratigraphic position of the fill and via script-evolution typology), not from direct excavation context.

### 2.2 Numerical and numero-ideographic tablets (precursors to proto-cuneiform)

- **Name:** Numerical tablets / numero-ideographic tablets
- **Dates:** Oldest numerical tablets (numbers only, no logographic signs) are placed in the Uruk V period, ca. 3500–3350 BC, per the Unicode technical report's sub-phasing ([Unicode Technical Report on Proto-Cuneiform, 2019](https://www.unicode.org/L2/L2019/19284-proto-cuneiform.pdf)); three such Uruk V numerical tablets are reported from Habuba Kabira in Syria (Wikipedia locator only, not independently confirmed against a fetched primary excavation report in this pass — mark as `n.a.` pending direct excavation-report confirmation). Englund (2011) places the immediately pre-writing "bullae" (sealed clay balls containing tokens) phase just before proto-cuneiform's emergence around 3300 BC ([Englund, "Accounting in Proto-Cuneiform," CDLI publication](https://cdli.earth/files-up/publications/englund2011a.pdf) — identified via search, not yet independently re-verified via direct fetch in this pass; treat the exact "3300 BC" figure as provisional pending direct confirmation).
- **Significance:** Numerical tablets represent an intermediate stage between token-based accounting and full proto-cuneiform, using impressed/incised numeral signs without accompanying word-signs.
- **Sources:** [Unicode Technical Report on Proto-Cuneiform (2019)](https://www.unicode.org/L2/L2019/19284-proto-cuneiform.pdf); [Englund, "Accounting in Proto-Cuneiform" (CDLI)](https://cdli.earth/files-up/publications/englund2011a.pdf).
- **Flags:** `n.a.` for the exact Habuba Kabira numerical-tablet count and date — only a Wikipedia-sourced (locator-only) claim was found; no primary excavation report was fetched to confirm it in this pass.

### 2.3 The clay token hypothesis (Schmandt-Besserat) and its criticism

- **Name:** Token hypothesis of the origin of writing (Denise Schmandt-Besserat, "Before Writing" I & II)
- **Dates:** Schmandt-Besserat's model proposes plain geometric clay tokens in use from as early as the 9th millennium BC across the Near East, evolving into "complex" tokens, then clay envelopes/bullae, then numerical tablets, then proto-cuneiform by ~3300 BC. Precise year figures for the theory itself are not independently at issue — the dispute is about the strength of the evidentiary chain, detailed below.
- **The criticism (well-documented, present as live scholarly dispute, not resolved):**
  - **Zimansky (1993)**, reviewing both volumes of "Before Writing" in the *Journal of Field Archaeology*, presents a detailed statistical critique: the published catalogue covers only 91% of the claimed 8,162 tokens and omits the Habuba Kabira dataset; only 18% of claimed token subtypes (93 of 508) have more than 4 members; the "sheep" token (a quartered circle) — despite livestock's known central economic importance — has only 15 total attestations across all periods; a "nails" token subtype has 60 attestations spanning from the 8th millennium, more than most livestock tokens; the 11 textile-token subtypes together total only ~27 dated examples despite textiles' known later importance. Zimansky concludes the claimed token-to-sign correlations are "virtually disprove[d]" by these numbers and that the theory as constructed "collapses" under its own evidentiary weight. He credits **Amiet & Lambert (1966)** with an earlier, more conservative alternative — that geometric objects were simple counters (one bulla = one transaction) and that pictorial signs arose only after a numerical-tablet-only phase — and states the evidence, in his assessment, remains essentially at that 1966 level of certainty. He also cites earlier skeptics **Lieberman (1980)**, "Of Clay Pebbles, Hollow Clay Balls, and Writing: A Sumerian View," *American Journal of Archaeology* 84:339–358, and **Nissen (1986)**, "The Archaic Texts from Uruk," *World Archaeology* 17:319–333 ([Zimansky 1993 review, Journal of Field Archaeology, hosted PDF](https://kieranhealy.org/files/misc/jfa93.pdf)).
  - **Bennison-Chapman (2023)**, in *Bulletin of the American Society of Overseas Research* (BASOR) 390, argues the standard "writing replaced tokens" narrative is "too simplistic" and "a gross oversimplification": tokens continued as an important administrative tool alongside clay tags, bullae, seals/sealings, and written texts well into the 1st millennium BC, rather than being cleanly superseded by writing ([Bennison-Chapman, BASOR 390 (2023), Journals.uchicago.edu](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/727776)).
  - **Bennison-Chapman (2014 PhD thesis, University of Liverpool)** notes that although Schmandt-Besserat's interpretation is "widely accepted" in popular accounts, her evidentiary base draws on sites distant in both space and time from the earliest token appearances, and there is no solid evidence that Neolithic tokens formed a single, unified, agriculture-tracking administrative framework across that whole span ([Bennison-Chapman thesis, University of Liverpool repository](https://livrepository.liverpool.ac.uk/2008477/1/Bennison-Chapman_Luc_Jul_2014_2008477.pdf)).
- **Significance:** The token hypothesis remains the most widely popularized account of writing's prehistory, but is subject to sustained, detailed, statistically grounded criticism regarding both data completeness and the strength of the proposed token-to-sign correspondence.
- **Sources:** [Zimansky, JFA 1993 review](https://kieranhealy.org/files/misc/jfa93.pdf); [Bennison-Chapman, BASOR 390 (2023)](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/727776); [Bennison-Chapman PhD thesis (2014), University of Liverpool](https://livrepository.liverpool.ac.uk/2008477/1/Bennison-Chapman_Luc_Jul_2014_2008477.pdf).
- **Flags:** The field genuinely disagrees on how much of the standard "tokens → bullae → numerical tablets → proto-cuneiform" narrative is empirically well-supported versus a plausible-but-oversimplified just-so story. Present both the hypothesis and its detailed statistical criticism; do not present the token hypothesis as settled fact.

### 2.4 Was writing invented for accounting? What is / isn't known

- **Name:** Function of the earliest writing (accounting-origin question)
- **Findings:** The earliest proto-cuneiform corpus is overwhelmingly administrative/economic in content — described by the 2025 Antiquity peer-reviewed article as "a complex accounting system with hundreds of iconographic signs" ([Cambridge/Antiquity, "Seals and Signs" (2025)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/seals-and-signs-tracing-the-origins-of-writing-in-ancient-southwest-asia/B3C2D400F3F80A7A0162D9035C9C2804)). A museum-partnered public-education source states plainly: "Writing seems to have been invented not for letters, literature or scripture, but for accountancy," referencing an early ration/beer-accounting tablet dated **3100–3000 BC** ([Google Arts & Culture / British Museum partner content, "The first writing: counting beer for the workers"](https://artsandculture.google.com/asset/the-first-writing-counting-beer-for-the-workers/fgF9ioy89DC2Uw?hl=en)).
- **Significance/nuance:** This accounting-origin narrative is broadly supported by the content of the earliest surviving tablets. However, it should be flagged as the dominant but not unchallenged framing — some scholars note that even the earliest textual corpus displays complexity beyond simple one-to-one commodity tracking, which complicates a purely reductive "invented solely to count grain" story (this nuance is noted in secondary literature discussion but was not independently confirmed against a directly fetched peer-reviewed primary source in this pass — mark the "more complex than simple tracking" claim specifically as `n.a.` pending direct confirmation, while the core "administrative/accounting function" claim is well-supported by the fetched Antiquity and museum sources above).
- **Sources:** [Antiquity/Cambridge, "Seals and Signs" (2025)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/seals-and-signs-tracing-the-origins-of-writing-in-ancient-southwest-asia/B3C2D400F3F80A7A0162D9035C9C2804); [Google Arts & Culture / British Museum, "The first writing"](https://artsandculture.google.com/asset/the-first-writing-counting-beer-for-the-workers/fgF9ioy89DC2Uw?hl=en); [British Museum, "Early writing tablet" (100 Objects)](https://sites.google.com/site/100objectsbritishmuseum/home/early-writing-tablet).
- **Flags:** `n.a.` for the specific claim that earliest texts show complexity "beyond simple commodity tracking" (nuance claim not independently re-confirmed via direct fetch in this pass). The core administrative/accounting-origin claim IS well-supported by fetched institutional sources.

## SECTION 5: ELAM — SUBDIVIDED

*Context: the existing dataset holds Elam as a single 3200–539 BCE entity with no children. The following subdivides it. Note: two different scholarly period-naming/dating schemes are in active use — a "traditional" scheme (Old/Middle/Neo-Elamite) and a "short chronology" scheme (Paleo-/Meso-/Neo-Elamite, per Gasche et al. 1998, as used by Encyclopaedia Iranica). Present both; do not average them.*

### 5.1 Proto-Elamite

- **Name:** Proto-Elamite (period and script)
- **Dates:** Cambridge Elements peer-reviewed academic source states the Proto-Elamite script was used roughly **3300–2900 BCE**, while explicitly cautioning that "precise dating remains problematic" because the relevant radiocarbon dates fall on a plateau in the radiocarbon calibration curve (a real physical limitation on precision, not just a data gap); the source notes potential refinement via Bayesian analysis given more Susa samples, citing Dahl, Petrie & Potts (2013) ([Cambridge Elements, "Proto-Elamite"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/elements/protoelamite/3684B7262E21A8B6AF8657D948A5B1A6)). An Oxford ORA academic repository paper independently states Proto-Elamite texts are dated archaeologically to **ca. 3100–2900 BC**, again citing Dahl et al. (2013) ([Oxford ORA, "Proto-Elamite and Linear Elamite, a Misunderstood..."](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:51adb1c2-61de-438c-9b29-125addf3d2a1/files/r6w924c626)).
- **Dating basis:** Radiocarbon (with an acknowledged calibration-curve plateau problem) combined with archaeological-typological placement; sub-phases (early/middle/late tablet morphology — oblong, then thin rectangular with ruled lines, then cushion-shaped) are relative-only, with **no absolute dates** assigned to these sub-phases per the Cambridge Elements source.
- **Significance:** Proto-Elamite is southwestern Iran's earliest indigenous writing system (also called Susa III archaeologically), geographically spreading from Susa to Tal-e Geser and then north to Tepe Sialk, Tepe Sofalin, and Tepe Ozbaki; it remains **undeciphered**.
- **Sources:** [Cambridge Elements, "Proto-Elamite"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/elements/protoelamite/3684B7262E21A8B6AF8657D948A5B1A6); [Oxford ORA repository paper](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:51adb1c2-61de-438c-9b29-125addf3d2a1/files/r6w924c626).
- **Flags:** ⚑ Note the explicit radiocarbon-plateau caveat from Cambridge Elements — precision beyond "c. 3300/3100–2900 BC" is not currently achievable with existing samples. Proto-Elamite remains undeciphered as of the most recent fetched sources.

### 5.2 Linear Elamite and its decipherment claim

- **Name:** Linear Elamite (script) and the 2022 decipherment claim
- **Dates:** Core Linear Elamite corpus is dated via internal evidence (royal names in the texts) to **ca. 2100–1900 BC** ([Oxford ORA repository paper](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:51adb1c2-61de-438c-9b29-125addf3d2a1/files/r6w924c626)); the original 2022 decipherment paper describes Linear Elamite as used in southern Iran in the late 3rd/early 2nd millennium BCE, specifically **ca. 2300–1880 BCE** ([Desset, Tabibzadeh, Kervran, Basello & Marchesi, "The Decipherment of Linear Elamite Writing," Zeitschrift für Assyriologie und Vorderasiatische Archäologie 112(1), 11–60, published July 2022, DOI 10.1515/za-2022-0003, hosted via ORBi repository](https://orbi.uliege.be/handle/2268/334018?wpmobileexternal=true)).
- **Decipherment claim and its status (present as unresolved, not settled — key finding of this research pass):**
  - The 2022 paper claims Linear Elamite is an alpha-syllabary (5 vowels, 12 consonants, 66 syllables), the oldest known purely phonographic writing system, with 348 total catalogued glyphs, 72 phonographic signs (plus allographs) deciphered covering over 95% of sign occurrences, only 4 undeciphered infrequent signs, and 33 hapax legomena ([ORBi repository, Desset et al. 2022](https://orbi.uliege.be/handle/2268/334018?wpmobileexternal=true)).
  - The Biblical Archaeology Society (first published 2022-08-12, updated 2023-04-19) reports that unaffiliated scholars told *Smithsonian Magazine* they were "quite convinced" by the decipherment, though "some details were still being ironed out" — indicating qualified, not unanimous, acceptance as of that update ([Biblical Archaeology Society, "Linear Elamite Deciphered"](https://www.biblicalarchaeology.org/daily/ancient-cultures/ancient-near-eastern-world/linear_elamite_deciphered/)).
  - The Oxford ORA paper presents a specific counter-model: rather than Desset's proposed direct evolutionary continuity from Proto-Elamite to Linear Elamite, Jacob Dahl argues the resemblance is better explained as deliberate "schismogenesis" — late-3rd-millennium scribes intentionally creating an archaicizing new script using recovered Proto-Elamite tablets plus knowledge of Old Akkadian cuneiform as inspiration, as a form of cultural differentiation from Mesopotamian influence, rather than a natural script-evolution lineage ([Oxford ORA repository paper](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:51adb1c2-61de-438c-9b29-125addf3d2a1/files/r6w924c626)).
- **Significance:** If accepted, the 2022 decipherment would make Linear Elamite the earliest known purely phonographic (alpha-syllabic) writing system, predating other early alphabetic/syllabic systems; the interpretive debate over its relationship to Proto-Elamite bears directly on models of script evolution versus deliberate script invention.
- **Sources:** [ORBi repository, Desset et al. 2022](https://orbi.uliege.be/handle/2268/334018?wpmobileexternal=true); [Biblical Archaeology Society (2022/2023)](https://www.biblicalarchaeology.org/daily/ancient-cultures/ancient-near-eastern-world/linear_elamite_deciphered/); [Oxford ORA repository paper (Dahl's schismogenesis counter-model)](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:51adb1c2-61de-438c-9b29-125addf3d2a1/files/r6w924c626).
- **Flags:** ⚑ LITERATURE MOVED (2022): the Linear Elamite decipherment claim is recent and, per the most recent fetched sources (through 2023), was "promising but not universally settled" — some scholars remain skeptical of the purely-phonographic claim and of the details of sign-value assignments; no fetched source in this pass reports a unanimous consensus statement. Treat "Linear Elamite has been deciphered" as a claim with a named proposing team and qualified, not universal, acceptance as of the most recent sources fetched here.

### 5.3 Old Elamite (traditional scheme) / Paleo-Elamite (short-chronology scheme)

- **Name:** Old Elamite (traditional) / Paleo-Elamite (Gasche et al. 1998 short chronology, as used by Iranica)
- **Dates (two schemes, do not average):**
  - **Encyclopaedia Iranica / short chronology:** Paleo-Elamite period, **about 2400–1450 BCE**, explicitly following "the brief chronology recently established by H. Gasche et al. - **Dating basis:** King-list/textual (the Awan/Simaški succession is derived from a paleo-Babylonian tablet found at Susa) combined with cross-synchronisms to the Mesopotamian Akkadian/Ur III/Old Babylonian sequences.

- **Sources:** [Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Susa ii. History during the Elamite period"](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/susa-ii-history-during-the-elamite-period/).

### 5.4 Middle Elamite (traditional) / Meso-Elamite (short chronology) — including Chogha Zanbil

- **Name:** Middle Elamite (traditional) / Meso-Elamite (Iranica/Gasche scheme)
- **Dates (present multiple figures, genuinely disagreeing — do not average):**
  - **UNESCO** (official international-body source): Middle Elamite period given as **1400–1100 BCE**, stated as the span for which "the ruins of Susa and of Tchogha Zanbil are the sole testimonies to the architectural development" ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre, "Tchogha Zanbil"](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/113/)).
  - **Encyclopaedia Iranica / short chronology:** Meso-Elamite period given as **about 1450–1050 BCE**, subdivided into the Kidinuids (~1450–1400 BCE), the Igihalkids (~1400–1050 BCE, within which Chogha Zanbil's builder Untash-Napirisha reigned), and the family of the Šutrukids (~1200–1050 BCE) ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Susa ii."](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/susa-ii-history-during-the-elamite-period/)).
  - **World History Encyclopedia** (secondary, moderate-quality, dated 2025-10-02): gives Middle Elamite period overall as **c. 1500–1100 BC** — a third, distinct figure ([World History Encyclopedia, "Chogha Zanbil"](https://www.worldhistory.org/Chogha_Zanbil/)) — flagged as a third, non-reconciled range.
- **Chogha Zanbil (Tchogha Zanbil / Dur-Untash):**
  - UNESCO states the city was "founded c. 1250 B.C." by Elamite king Untash-Napirisha, whose reign UNESCO dates to **1275–1240 BCE**; the city was left permanently unfinished (evidenced by thousands of unused bricks found on site) and was destroyed by the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal "about 640 BCE" ([UNESCO, "Tchogha Zanbil"](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/113/)).

  - Wikipedia (locator only, not independently confirmed here) separately notes two competing synchronism models for Untash-Napirisha's reign depending on which Babylonian king is meant by a diplomatic correspondence ("the Berlin Letter"): ca. 1340–1300 BC (if the correspondent was Burna-Buriash II) versus ca. 1275–1240 BC (if a later Kassite prince is meant) — this dispute is corroborated in substance, though not in exact wording, by the Iranica entry's own discussion of the "Berlin Letter" as the key (and disputed) evidence for reconstructing the Igihalkid dynasty's chronology ([Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/susa-ii-history-during-the-elamite-period/)).
  - Original excavations at Chogha Zanbil took place 1946–1962 ([UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/113/)).
  - Ziggurat: originally 105.2 m per side and approximately 53 m tall, built in five levels; today only 24.75 m survives, less than half its estimated original height ([UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/113/)).
- **Significance:** Chogha Zanbil is the best-preserved ziggurat in Iran and, per UNESCO, one of only two sites (with Susa) providing architectural evidence for the entire Middle Elamite period; Untash-Napirisha's reign illustrates Elam's deep dynastic intermarriage with Kassite Babylon.
- **Sources:** [UNESCO, "Tchogha Zanbil"](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/113/); [Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Susa ii."](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/susa-ii-history-during-the-elamite-period/); [World History Encyclopedia, "Chogha Zanbil"](https://www.worldhistory.org/Chogha_Zanbil/).
- **Flags:** ⚑ Genuine, unresolved disagreement on Untash-Napirisha's exact reign dates (UNESCO's 1275–1240 BCE vs. an earlier ~1340–1300 BCE synchronism implied by Iranica's Burnaburiaš II cross-date) — hinges on which Kassite king is identified as his father-in-law in "the Berlin Letter." Do not average. Middle Elamite overall date range also varies across institutional (UNESCO: 1400–1100 BCE) and Iranica short-chronology (1450–1050 BCE) sources — present both.

### 5.5 Neo-Elamite period — internal subdivision

- **Name:** Neo-Elamite period (with internal sub-phases: Neo-Elamite I, II, III)
- **Dates (multiple schemes — present all, do not average):**
  - **Cambridge University Press ("The Archaeology of Elam," ch. 8, D.T. Potts, 2015):** Neo-Elamite overall dated **c. 1000 BC to 539 BC** (the conquest of Babylonia by Cyrus the Great). Sub-phases: **Neo-Elamite I, c. 1000–744 BC** ("poorly known"); **Neo-Elamite II, 743–646 BC** ("amply documented in Assyrian sources," a period of intense Assyrian-Elamite conflict, with allied insurrectionists in southern Babylonia); **Neo-Elamite III** — this Cambridge source states the period is divided into three phases but the specific date range/characterization for Neo-Elamite III was not present in the fetched excerpt (`n.a.` for this specific sub-range from this source) ([Cambridge Core, "The Neo-Elamite period," ch. 8 in The Archaeology of Elam, 2015-11-01](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/archaeology-of-elam/neoelamite-period/054799B680BFE4F9897D0F521CAA46E8)).
  - **Encyclopaedia Iranica (following Vallat 1996):** Neo-Elamite overall dated **about 1050–539 BCE**. Sub-phases: **Neo-Elamite I, about 1050–770 BCE** ("the longest and most poorly documented phase" — for over two and a half centuries Susa produced no inscriptions, so none of its rulers from this span are known by name); **Neo-Elamite II, about 770–585 BCE** (corresponds to the Humbanuid dynasty and the last kings titled "king of Anšan and Susa"; better known via Mesopotamian Chronicles, Annals, and Letters; Susa fell to Ashurbanipal in **646 BCE**, and statues taken by the Assyrians were restored to Susa by Nabopolassar in **626 BCE**); **Neo-Elamite III, about 585–539 BCE** (Susa "appears to have lost its rank as a capital"; last three kings — Ummanunu, Šilhak-Inšušinak II, Tepti-Huban-Inšušinak — no longer bore the "king of Anšan and Susa" title; period ends with Cyrus the Great's seizure of power in 539 BCE) ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Susa ii."](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/susa-ii-history-during-the-elamite-period/)).
  - **Alvarez-Mon (2010), "The Arjan Tomb"** (found via search, academic — not independently re-fetched in this pass, treat as corroborating but provisional): presents yet another tripartite scheme — NE I (c. 1000–743 BC), NE II (743–653/647 BC), NE III (653/646–c. 539 BC), with NE III further split into NE IIIA (653/647–605/585 BC) and NE IIIB (605/585–539 BC); also cites an older bipartite division by de Miroschedji (1981): NE I (ca. 1000–725/700 BC), NE II (ca. 725/700–520 BC).
- **Key individual dates confirmed via Iranica:** Šutruk-Nahhunte II's reign, **717–699 BC** (consistent between Iranica and the Cambridge/Potts chapter, which independently gives "c. 717–699 BC" for the same king — a rare point of cross-source agreement); Sargon II of Assyria's reign given as 721–705 BC in this context; Sennacherib's installation of his son Aššur-nadin-šumi on the Babylonian throne, 699 BC; the Battle of Halule, 691 BC (outcome unclear per Iranica); Esarhaddon's reign, 680–669 BC; Ashurbanipal's reign, 668–627 BC; fall of Susa to Ashurbanipal, 646 BC (Iranica) vs. "about 640 BCE" (UNESCO, referring to Chogha Zanbil's destruction specifically — a related but not necessarily identical event) — [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/susa-ii-history-during-the-elamite-period/); [Cambridge/Potts, "The Neo-Elamite period"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/archaeology-of-elam/neoelamite-period/054799B680BFE4F9897D0F521CAA46E8); [UNESCO, "Tchogha Zanbil"](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/113/).
- **Dating basis:** Primarily king-list/textual — Iranica states explicitly that because indigenous Elamite sources are scarce for this period, the historical reconstruction is "to some extent a prisoner of the Assyrian royal inscriptions and the Babylonian Chronicle," i.e., Elamite history in this period is seen largely "through foreign eyes" (a caveat also stated independently by the Cambridge/Potts chapter).
- **Significance:** The Neo-Elamite period ends Elam's independent existence with Cyrus's 539 BC conquest, and NE II specifically documents Elam's central role in repeated anti-Assyrian coalitions with Babylonian rebels (e.g., support for Merodach-baladan against Sargon II).
- **Sources:** [Cambridge Core, "The Neo-Elamite period" (Potts, 2015)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/archaeology-of-elam/neoelamite-period/054799B680BFE4F9897D0F521CAA46E8); [Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Susa ii."](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/susa-ii-history-during-the-elamite-period/); [UNESCO, "Tchogha Zanbil"](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/113/).
- **Flags:** ⚑ Neo-Elamite I/II boundary is given as 744 BC (Cambridge/Potts) vs. 770 BC (Iranica/Vallat) vs. 743 BC (Alvarez-Mon) — a ~26-year, source-dependent discrepancy; do not average, state basis-dependence explicitly. NE II/III boundary likewise varies (646 BC per Cambridge implicit end-of-NE-II vs. 585 BC per Iranica's NE II/III split) — these reflect different scholars' (Potts vs. Vallat vs. Alvarez-Mon vs. de Miroschedji) periodization choices, not a single resolved figure.

### 5.6 Susa (site)

- **Name:** Susa (Šuš, modern Shush, Iran)
- **Dates:**
  - Encyclopaedia Iranica states buildings on the Susa Acropolis are archaeologically dated to at least **4000 BCE**, while the earliest buildings in the "Ville Royale A" excavation area date to around **1700 BCE** (with a possible slightly older occupation on the nearby Apadana Tell) ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Susa ii."](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/susa-ii-history-during-the-elamite-period/)).
  - Iranica frames Susa's "Elamite period" specifically as **about 2400–539 BCE**, beginning when Susa was probably under the Awan dynasty and ending with Cyrus the Great's seizure of power in 539 BCE; before 2400 BCE, Iranica states Susa belonged to "the proto-Elamite world" ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Susa ii."](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/susa-ii-history-during-the-elamite-period/)) — this is a narrower, historically-focused framing than the site's full occupation span.
  - A Mehrnews.com article cites a calibrated radiocarbon date of **4395 BCE** for the settlement's foundation, at which point the site was already about 15 hectares, with earlier Neolithic village traces to c. 7000 BCE and a "painted-pottery civilization" from c. 5000 BCE ([Mehr News Agency](https://en.mehrnews.com/news/128812/Susa-oldest-known-settlements-of-region) — a state-affiliated Iranian news outlet, moderate-quality secondary source, not independently cross-checked against a primary excavation report in this pass; treat the exact "4395 BCE" figure as provisional).
  - The French Ministry of Culture's official page states archaeological remains at Susa span **4200 BCE to the Islamic period**; growth of the site coincided with the emergence of proto-Elamite writing; by around **2700 BCE** Susa had become the capital city-state of the kingdom of Elam, closely connected to Mesopotamia; the site was sacked around **1100 BCE** by Nebuchadnezzar I of Babylon and destroyed by Ashurbanipal in **648 BCE** ([French Ministry of Culture, "Susa: 6,000 years of history"](https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/jacques-morgan/en/susa-6000-years-history)) — this is an official institutional source, though the exact "648 BCE" figure for Ashurbanipal's destruction of Susa differs slightly from Iranica's "646 BCE" and "647 BCE" figures for the same event (see §5.5) — a minor (1–2 year) source discrepancy, flagged rather than averaged.
  - Excavation history (Wikipedia, locator only for dates but useful for naming excavators/campaigns, not independently re-confirmed via a fetched primary excavation-history source in this pass): examined 1836 (Rawlinson); 1851 (Loftus and Fenwick Williams); 1885–86 (Marcel and Jane Dieulafoy — first French excavations, uncovering glazed bricks and Achaemenid column bases); 1897–1908/1912 (Jacques de Morgan — major citadel excavations, recovering the Hammurabi stele, the Naram-Sin stele, and the Code of Hammurabi, later confirmed by Britannica: [Britannica, "Susa"](https://www.britannica.com/place/Susa)); 1969–1979 (Jean Perrot).
- **Significance:** Susa is one of the longest continuously significant sites in the ancient Near East, serving successively as an early Proto-Elamite center, the historical capital of Elam, a major Achaemenid administrative center, and (per its French Ministry of Culture profile) the findspot of some of the most important Mesopotamian monuments ever recovered (the Hammurabi stele and the Naram-Sin stele), which had been carried to Susa as war booty by Elamite kings.
- **Sources:** [Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Susa ii."](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/susa-ii-history-during-the-elamite-period/); [Mehr News Agency](https://en.mehrnews.com/news/128812/Susa-oldest-known-settlements-of-region); [French Ministry of Culture, "Susa: 6,000 years of history"](https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/jacques-morgan/en/susa-6000-years-history); [Britannica, "Susa"](https://www.britannica.com/place/Susa).
- **Flags:** Minor (1–648 vs. 646/647 BCE) date discrepancy across sources for Ashurbanipal's destruction/sack of Susa — flagged as a small but real inconsistency, not averaged. The site's very earliest occupation date (4200–4395 BCE range depending on source) is likewise not fully reconciled across the fetched sources.

## SECTION 6: THE 2ND-MILLENNIUM BCE MESOPOTAMIAN CHRONOLOGY PROBLEM (High/Middle/Low/Ultra-Low)

- **Name:** The Mesopotamian 2nd-millennium chronology debate (High / Middle / Low / Ultra-Low chronologies)
- **What anchors the debate:** All four competing chronologies are built around the same core problem — fixing an absolute calendar date for the reign of **Hammurabi of Babylon** (and, by extension, the fall of Babylon at the end of the Old Babylonian period), since Mesopotamian king-lists and year-names give relative sequences but require an external astronomical or archaeological anchor to convert to absolute BCE dates. The main proposed astronomical anchor is the **Venus Tablet of Ammi-saduqa** (recording Venus's heliacal risings/settings), which is astronomically periodic (matching multiple possible real years), hence generating multiple viable chronologies depending on which repetition of the Venus cycle is chosen, further modulated by uncertain intervals in the king-lists between the observation and Hammurabi's reign ([Oriental Institute/ISAC, "Dating the Fall of Babylon"](https://isac.uchicago.edu/dating-fall-babylon) — cited from the earlier research phase of this session; institutional/academic source).
- **The four chronologies (present as genuinely competing, unresolved positions — do not average):**
  - **High(er) Chronology:** places Hammurabi's reign earliest among the major options (traditionally around the 1848–1806 BC range in some formulations).
  - **Middle Chronology:** the most widely used in general/textbook literature; places Hammurabi's reign at approximately 1792–1750 BC — this is the chronology most commonly assumed by default in popular and semi-popular sources when a single "Hammurabi date" or "fall of Babylon date" is quoted without further qualification.
  - **Low Chronology:** places Hammurabi's reign later, approximately 1728–1686 BC in some formulations, shifting the whole 2nd-millennium sequence down.
  - **Ultra-Low (or "New") Chronology:** places Hammurabi's reign later still, used by a minority of specialists, motivated by attempts to reconcile Mesopotamian textual chronology with independent archaeological/dendrochronological synchronisms (e.g., from Kültepe/Anatolia) that some scholars argue fit poorly with the Middle Chronology.
  - (Note: exact year-ranges for each scheme vary slightly by author; the naming and relative ordering — High > Middle > Low > Ultra-Low, all anchored to the same Venus-tablet ambiguity — is the stable, well-established part of this debate; precise year boundaries for each scheme were sourced from the earlier phase of this research session via [Oriental Institute/ISAC](https://isac.uchicago.edu/dating-fall-babylon) and [livius.org, "Mesopotamian Chronology"](https://www.livius.org/mesopotamian-chronology) — both institutional/academic-adjacent sources fetched earlier in this research pass.)
- **Where the field currently stands:** No single chronology commands universal consensus; the Middle Chronology remains the conventional default in most general reference works and textbooks (hence most commonly-quoted Hammurabi/fall-of-Babylon dates implicitly assume it), but specialists actively debate Low and Ultra-Low variants based on convergent archaeological and astronomical evidence, and the High Chronology is now a minority position mainly of historiographic interest.
- **Which commonly-quoted dates assume which scheme:** Popular/general reference sources that state "Hammurabi reigned 1792–1750 BC" (or similar) are, by default, using the **Middle Chronology**. Any source giving dates roughly 60–100 years earlier or later than this range for the same reign is implicitly using the High, Low, or Ultra-Low scheme respectively, even if it does not name the scheme explicitly — this is the single most common source of apparent date "conflicts" across different books/websites discussing Hammurabi or the Old Babylonian period.
- **Significance:** This chronology problem cascades through the entire 2nd-millennium BCE Near Eastern absolute chronology (Old Babylonian, Old Assyrian, Hittite Old Kingdom, and Kassite-period synchronisms all shift together depending on which scheme is adopted), making it one of the most consequential unresolved dating debates in ancient Near Eastern studies.
- **Sources:** [Oriental Institute/ISAC, "Dating the Fall of Babylon"](https://isac.uchicago.edu/dating-fall-babylon); [livius.org, "Mesopotamian Chronology"](https://www.livius.org/mesopotamian-chronology); cross-referenced against [Wikipedia, "Chronology of the ancient Near East" — locator only, NOT a dating authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronology_of_the_ancient_Near_East).
- **Flags:** ⚑ This is a live, unresolved scholarly debate — never present a single Hammurabi date (e.g., "1792–1750 BC") without noting it assumes the Middle Chronology specifically, and that High/Low/Ultra-Low alternatives exist and are actively argued by specialists. Do not average across schemes.
