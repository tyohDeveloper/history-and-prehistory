# Chinese Romanisation Research for Historical Book-Spelling Matching

Purpose: support a "findability" feature so that a user who types a pre-1980s
book's spelling of a Chinese dynasty, place, or emperor name can still find
the corresponding entry indexed under modern Hanyu Pinyin. This document
records verified Wade–Giles/Pinyin dynasty pairs, the history of the
Wade-Giles→Pinyin transition, Chinese postal romanisation forms, and the
temple-name ambiguity problem — each claim with its source.

---

### Notes on identical vs. divergent forms

- **Identical, no alias needed:** Shang, Han, Sui, Ming, and (with caveats
  about diacritics) the three Three-Kingdoms state names Wei, Shu, Wu are
  spelled the same in Wade-Giles and Pinyin
  ([LOC romanization table](https://www.loc.gov/catdir/cpso/romanization/chinese.pdf)).
- **Dramatically different — high priority for alias/search mapping:**
  Zhou/Chou, Qin/Ch'in, Tang/T'ang, Song/Sung, Xia/Hsia, Qing/Ch'ing, and
  both Jin/Chin dynasties. These match exactly the examples flagged in the
  task brief and are the ones most likely to trip up a search feature
  ([LOC romanization table](https://www.loc.gov/catdir/cpso/romanization/chinese.pdf)).
- **The two Jin dynasties are a special case.** They are written with
  different Chinese characters (晉 for 265–420, 金 for the Jurchen state of
  1115–1234) and different pinyin tones (Jìn vs Jīn), but **the same
  Wade-Giles spelling, "Chin," for both** — Encyclopaedia Britannica's entry
  on the Jurchen dynasty gives its Wade-Giles romanization as simply "Chin"
  ([Britannica, "Jin dynasty" 1115–1234](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Jin-dynasty-China-Mongolia-1115-1234)),
  identical to the Wade-Giles rendering of the earlier Jin (晉) dynasty
  ([German Wikipedia, Jin-Dynastie (265–420)](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jin-Dynastie_(265%E2%80%93420))
  gives "Chin Ch'ao"). This means Wade-Giles alone cannot disambiguate the two
  dynasties — the underlying character or date range must be carried along.
  Some modern sources acknowledge this collision and use "Jinn" for the
  Jurchen dynasty specifically to keep it visually distinct from the older Jin
  in Roman script
  ([Simple English Wikipedia, Jin dynasty (1115–1234)](https://simple.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jin_dynasty_(1115%E2%80%931234))).
  Other English-language sources also render the Jurchen dynasty as "Kin"
  ([Spanish Wikipedia, Dinastía Jin (1115–1234)](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dinast%C3%ADa_Jin_(1115-1234))
  notes the alternate English spellings "Kin," "Jurchen Jin," "Jinn," or
  "Chin"). For the app's search index, I recommend keying on the dynasty date
  range or the character (晉 vs 金) rather than relying on the romanised
  string to disambiguate.
- **Yuan/Yüan** is a milder case: the only difference is the umlaut on ü,
  which many pre-1980s books retained but which is easy to lose in plain-text
  search; it should still get an alias mapping.

## Task 2 — When and why Hanyu Pinyin became the international standard

**Origin of Pinyin.** Hanyu Pinyin was designed by a government committee in
China through the 1950s and formally approved by the National People's
Congress on 11 February 1958
([Wikipedia, "Pinyin"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinyin); the 1958-02-11
adoption date is also stated directly inside the ISO standard text itself,
see [ISO 7098:1991 full text](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/13683/aa7617ece3fe4864bd2381109ea6c8bb/ISO-7098-1991.pdf)).
Despite this, Pinyin initially saw little international use; Wade-Giles
remained dominant abroad through the 1960s and 1970s.

**China's own international push (1979).** On 1 January 1979 the Chinese
government itself began requiring Pinyin for foreign-language publications
and diplomatic materials, prescribing Pinyin for English-language output and
the Lessing-Othmer system for German
([Encyclopaedia Britannica, "Wade-Giles romanization"](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Wade-Giles-romanization)).
The *New York Times* reported on this shift as it was unfolding, noting in a
30 September 1979 article that "many...national newspapers, news agencies,
and television networks had already embraced" Pinyin, which "has been
officially utilized by Beijing since January 1"
([New York Times, "Controversy Arises on Chinese Spelling," 30 Sept. 1979](https://www.nytimes.com/1979/09/30/archives/controversy-arises-on-chinese-spelling-some-libraries-say-us.html)).

**United Nations (1977/1979–1986).** The Third United Nations Conference on
the Standardization of Geographical Names voted 43–1 in 1977 to adopt Hanyu
Pinyin as "the international system for the romanization of Chinese
geographical names" (the lone dissenting vote was the United States, citing
the cost of converting Library of Congress records)
([pinyin.info, "history bite: around this time in 1977"](https://pinyin.info/news/2010/history-bite-around-this-time-in-1977/)).
The United Nations itself began using Pinyin as an organisation in 1986
([Wikipedia, "Pinyin"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinyin)).

**ISO 7098.** The International Organization for Standardization adopted
Pinyin as ISO 7098:1982, "Documentation — Romanization of Chinese," published
August 1982 ([ISO, "ISO 7098:1982"](https://www.iso.org/standard/13682.html)).
This first edition was superseded by ISO 7098:1991
([ISO, "ISO 7098:1991"](https://www.iso.org/standard/13683.html)), which was
in turn superseded by ISO 7098:2015 (per the same ISO catalogue page and
[China Social Sciences Net's history of the standard](http://english.cssn.cn/skw_research/linguistics/202112/t20211201_5651117.shtml),
which also states that ISO/TC 46 issued the original 1982 text at its 19th
meeting on 1 August 1982). The IANA language-subtag registry corroborates
that Pinyin "has been the national standard of China since 1958, and an
international standard (ISO 7098:1991, 2nd ed.) since 1982"
([IANA registry entry for pinyin](https://www.iana.org/assignments/lang-subtags-templates/pinyin.txt)).

**Library of Congress and U.S./Canadian libraries.** The Library of Congress
announced on 19 November 1997 that it would convert its cataloguing from
Wade-Giles to Pinyin
([Library of Congress press release PR97-158, 19 Nov. 1997](https://www.loc.gov/catdir/pinyin/announce.html)).
Actual "Day 1" implementation did not happen immediately — the Library of
Congress, together with OCLC, the Research Libraries Group, and the Council
on East Asian Libraries, spent roughly three years planning the mechanical
conversion of catalogue and authority records
([CEAL, "Final Report on Pinyin Conversion"](http://www.white-clouds.com/iclc/cliej/cl9ceal.htm)).
The changeover finally took effect on 1 October 2000, when "American
libraries" (and other libraries in the international community) began "using
Pinyin as the standard romanization scheme for Chinese characters," and "the
Wade-Giles romanization system, followed in American libraries for the last
century, [would] no longer be used"; a one-year transition period ran through
1 October 2001
([Library of Congress, "Library of Congress, Other U.S. Libraries Join
International Community on Use of Pinyin," 2 Oct. 2000](https://www.loc.gov/item/prn-00-141/)).
A 2023 IFLA retrospective confirms that "in 2000, American and Canadian
libraries started to use Pinyin as the standard romanization scheme to
process Chinese materials in all library operations"
([IFLA repository, "One Language, Different Romanization Rules: The 20th
Anniversary of the Pinyin Conversion Project"](https://repository.ifla.org/items/699cac44-5c5c-4227-8498-fa9e399fdb86/full)).
**Practical implication for the app:** any English-language academic or
library-catalogued book published before 2000 is likely to use Wade-Giles
romanization (or, for place names specifically, postal romanization — see
Task 3); this is a good default cutoff heuristic, though many trade and
journalistic sources switched two decades earlier, around 1979–1987 (see
below).

**New York Times, other U.S. media, and Britannica.** The *New York Times*
first explained the new Pinyin system to readers in a 4 March 1979 article,
"Chinese Pronunciation: The New System"
([New York Times, 4 March 1979](https://www.nytimes.com/1979/03/04/archives/chinese-pronunciation-the-new-system.html)),
around the time of China's own January 1979 mandate. However, the *Times*
did not switch its own house style for the single highest-profile case —
"Peking" to "Beijing" — until November 1986
([Wikipedia, "Names of Beijing"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Beijing)).
The Associated Press and United Press International jointly adopted
"Beijing" starting 1 March 1987 ([same source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Beijing)),
and the BBC switched in 1990 ([same source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Beijing)).
*Time* magazine announced its own switch to Pinyin (keeping Wade-Giles in
parentheses at first, and explicitly keeping a short list of exceptions such
as "Peking," "Canton," "Tibet," and "Mao Tse-tung") in an article titled
"World: Spelling Chinese" ([Time magazine archive, "World: Spelling
Chinese"](https://time.com/archive/6850429/world-spelling-chinese/)) — I was
not able to independently confirm the exact original publication date of
this *Time* piece beyond the archive's own metadata, so treat the date as
approximate (see "What I could not verify"). I could **not** find a single,
explicitly dated Encyclopaedia Britannica editorial-policy statement
announcing its own switch to Pinyin; Britannica's biographical and topic
articles today give both Pinyin and Wade-Giles forms side by side (e.g. its
articles on [Taizong](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Taizong-emperor-of-Tang-dynasty)
and [Gaozong](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Gaozong-emperor-of-Tang-dynasty)),
but I could not source a specific year for when Britannica's main text
entries changed their primary headword system from Wade-Giles to Pinyin — see
"What I could not verify."

## Task 3 — Chinese Postal Romanization (place names)

**What it was.** Postal romanization (郵政式拼音) was a system for spelling
Chinese place names in the Latin alphabet, developed by China's postal
authorities beginning in the late 19th century, distinct from Wade-Giles
proper. A 2009 peer-reviewed article states: "Prior to the general acceptance
of Hanyu pinyin...as the governing transliteration system for Chinese in the
mid-1980s, the Wade-Giles system predominated for fifty years except in the
spelling of place names," where the postal system, established shortly after
the founding of the modern Imperial Post Office in 1896, served as "the
toponymic authority"
([Bruce Doar, "A 'Lasting Boon to All': A Note on the Postal Romanization of
Place Names, 1896–1949," Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/pub/30/article/260184/pdf)).
In other words: **Western scholars, cartographers, journalists, and
governments commonly used Wade-Giles for general Chinese terms, but kept
postal spellings specifically for place names** — this is exactly the
distinction a pre-1980s-book search tool needs to model, because a reader
searching a book index will often find place names spelled neither in
Wade-Giles nor Pinyin.

**Origin: the "Nanking syllabary."** In 1892 Herbert Giles created a
romanization system informally called the "Nanking syllabary," based on
Nanjing pronunciation rather than the Beijing-based pronunciation underlying
Wade-Giles proper. In spring 1906 an Imperial Postal Joint-Session Conference
in Shanghai formally adopted this system for the post office, with the
explicit rule of dropping accents, apostrophes, and hyphens to simplify
telegraphic transmission — while retaining a handful of pre-existing
"traditional" European spellings for already-well-known treaty-port cities
(e.g. Amoy, Canton) rather than forcing them into the new scheme
([Wikipedia, "Chinese postal romanization"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_postal_romanization)).

**Key place-name pairs.** The table below gives the 1919/1947-era postal
form (the form most likely to appear in pre-1980s English-language books),
alongside Wade-Giles and Pinyin, sourced from the comparison table in

whose data is drawn from the original postal atlases of 1907/1919/1947:

A special note on "Amoy": the spelling reflects the local Hokkien
(Southern Min) pronunciation of Xiamen, not Mandarin at all — "based on the
pronunciation of Xiamen in the neighboring Zhangzhou dialect of Hokkien"
([Wikipedia, "Chinese postal romanization"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_postal_romanization)),
which is why it looks nothing like either Wade-Giles "Hsia-mên" or Pinyin
"Xiàmén." "Canton" likewise predates the postal system as a European
merchant-era name (ultimately via Portuguese "Cantão") and was one of the
names retained rather than replaced by the 1906 conference.

**Decline and end date.** Communist China announced abolition of postal
romanization in 1964, though the forms remained in common informal and
cartographic use through the 1980s
([Wikipedia, "Chinese postal romanization" chronology table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_postal_romanization)).
*China Daily*, the PRC's official English-language paper, adopted Pinyin in
1981 ([rcmacau.org mirror of the postal-romanization article](https://rcmacau.org/wp-content/uploads/2011/05/Chinese_Postal_Map_Romanization)).
Postal-style romanization remained *officially* in use in Taiwan for city
names until 2002, when Taiwan adopted Tongyong Pinyin, itself replaced by
Hanyu Pinyin in 2009 ([Wikipedia, "Chinese postal romanization"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_postal_romanization)).
**Implication for the app:** a book published any time up to the early
1980s may use postal spellings for major cities/provinces even if it uses
Wade-Giles (or occasionally already Pinyin) for everything else — so postal
forms need their own alias layer independent of the main
Wade-Giles/Pinyin toggle.

## Task 4 — Temple names (廟號) and the reuse-and-ambiguity problem

**Confirmation that temple names were deliberately reused across dynasties.**
Chinaknowledge.de's reference article on rulers' names and titles states
plainly: "It was common that the founder of a dynasty was called Taizu 太祖,
Gaozu 高祖 or Shizu 世祖, their successors were often called Taizong 太宗 or
Shizong 世宗," and goes on: "Because virtually all dynasties have their
Gaozu, Gaozong, Taizu or Taizong (Tang Taizu 唐太祖, Song Taizu 宋太祖, Liao
Taizu 遼太祖, Jin Taizu 金太祖, Yuan Taizu 元太祖, Ming Taizu 明太祖, Qing Taizu
清太祖), it is very important to always add the name of the dynasty to avoid
confusion about which Gaozu or Taizong is meant"
([chinaknowledge.de, "Names of Persons and Titles of Rulers"](http://www.chinaknowledge.de/History/Terms/titles.html)).

Wikipedia's dedicated disambiguation page for "Taizong" confirms the name
"was the temple name of several monarchs of China," primarily but not only
referring to Emperor Taizong of Tang
([Wikipedia, "Taizong" (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taizong)).
Encyclopaedia Britannica's individual biographical entries independently
confirm at least two rulers named Gaozong from different dynasties: the third
emperor of the Tang dynasty
([Britannica, "Gaozong," Tang dynasty](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Gaozong-emperor-of-Tang-dynasty))
and the first emperor of the Southern Song dynasty
([Britannica, "Gaozong," Southern Song dynasty](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Gaozong-emperor-of-Southern-Song-dynasty)) —
demonstrating the exact ambiguity the task asks about: "Emperor Gaozong"
alone is genuinely insufficient to identify which ruler is meant.

**Why temple names were used at all, and since when.** Temple names were
first used during the Shang dynasty and continued to be bestowed until the
end of the Qing dynasty, though "from the Tang period onwards, all emperors
were given temple names" as standard practice
([chinaknowledge.de, "Names of Persons and Titles of Rulers"](http://www.chinaknowledge.de/History/Terms/titles.html)).
Endymion Wilkinson's *Chinese History: A New Manual* is cited (via a
publicly available excerpt) as giving the standard scholarly rule directly:
"Tang-Yuan: Emperors from the beginning of the Tang to the end of the Yuan
are normally referred to by the name of the dynasty plus their temple title,
[e.g.] Emperor Huizong of Song or Song Huizong 宋徽宗"
([excerpt of Wilkinson, *Chinese History: A New Manual*, hosted at ebin.pub](https://ebin.pub/chinese-history-a-new-manual-0674067150-9780674067158.html)).
The same excerpt notes that different eras use different naming systems
altogether: Shang rulers are cited by posthumous ritual names; early Zhou
rulers by dynasty name plus honorific title; later Zhou rulers by dynasty
name plus posthumous title — with temple names becoming the norm only later.
(I was not able to obtain a directly LOC- or Harvard-hosted PDF/official
excerpt of Wilkinson's book; the citation above is to a third-party-hosted
copy of the text, so treat the exact wording as reported rather than
independently verified against the print original — see "What I could not
verify.")

**The standard scholarly disambiguation convention.** The universal fix,
confirmed by multiple sources, is to prefix the temple name with the dynasty
name: "Tang Taizong" vs. "Song Taizong," "Song Taizu" vs. "Ming Taizu," etc.
([chinaknowledge.de](http://www.chinaknowledge.de/History/Terms/titles.html);
also stated by [Wilkinson excerpt via ebin.pub](https://ebin.pub/chinese-history-a-new-manual-0674067150-9780674067158.html),
which gives "Song Huizong" as the exemplar format). A detailed, sourced
discussion on r/AskHistorians (a moderated subreddit requiring
source-backed answers) makes the same point discursively: "If the state is
clear from context, the two-character temple name suffices; otherwise the
state name is added first...Temple names are disambiguated similarly with
the state-temple format, e.g., Song Taizu versus Ming Taizu"
([r/AskHistorians discussion](https://www.reddit.com/r/AskHistorians/comments/15p7ded/why_did_the_european_monarchs_of_the_same_dynasty/jvwbuve/)) —
this is included as corroborating, non-primary evidence, not as a standalone
authority.

**Practical rule for the app:** when indexing rulers by temple name, always
store the dynasty as a required qualifying field, and when displaying a bare
temple name (e.g. a search hit on "Taizong"), the UI should force
disambiguation by dynasty (e.g. show "Tang Taizong (r. 626–649)" vs. "Song
Taizong (r. 976–997)" as distinct results) rather than treating the temple
name alone as a unique key.
