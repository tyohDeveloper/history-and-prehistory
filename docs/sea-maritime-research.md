# Maritime Southeast Asian States — Research Dossier

Prepared to fill the gap around Majapahit (which currently appears with no predecessor) and to source existing unsourced entities (Srivijaya, Majapahit, Malacca, Spanish Philippines, Republic of Indonesia). Covers the full Javanese dynastic sequence, the major Sumatran/Malay thalassocracies, Brunei/Sulu, pre-Spanish Philippine polities, and the Spanish/American/Dutch colonial transitions into modern Indonesia and the Philippines.

Every entry lists: start year, end year, founding/ending events, native name/script (where verifiable), capital, and sources. Disputes are documented rather than resolved, per the research brief. A "What I could not verify" section closes the document.

## THE JAVANESE SEQUENCE

### 1. Medang / Mataram Kingdom (Central Java period, c. 732–1006/1016)

- **Start:** c. 732 CE (conventional start tied to the Canggal inscription of King Sanjaya, though the Britannica narrative account of this period does not use the name "Sanjaya" at all — see dispute below).
- **End:** Documentation "shifts abruptly" from Central Java to East Java after the early 10th century, a move Britannica calls "never satisfactorily explained." A 1016 destruction event (probably an attack by a rebellious vassal) is described in the 1041 Calcutta (Pucangan) inscription as "the destruction of the world," and is generally treated as the effective end of the Central Javanese state before its East Javanese restoration under Airlangga. Some chronologies give 1006 as the fall of Medang.
- **Founding/ending events:** Built Borobudur (Buddhist monument, late 8th–early/mid-9th century) and the Prambanan temple complex (an inscription dated 856 marks its likely foundation, associated with a rival, non-Shailendra family). Ended in a catastrophic attack on the royal capital, followed by East Javanese restoration under Erlangga/Airlangga, son-in-law of the destroyed king, who lived as an ascetic 1017–1019 and was hailed ruler of Pasuruan in 1019.
- **Native name/script:** Javanese "Mataram" = ꦩꦠꦫꦩ꧀ (Javanese script; the script itself descends from Kawi, in use in Java from the 8th century) — [Wikipedia: Mataram Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mataram_kingdom).
- **Capital:** Central Java (Kedu Plain / Mataram area) until the early 10th century; shifted to East Java (Brantas valley) thereafter, for reasons not well understood — [Britannica: Central Java from c. 700 to c. 1000](https://www.britannica.com/place/Indonesia/Central-Java-from-c-700-to-c-1000).
- **Dispute — Sailendra vs. Sanjaya, one dynasty or two:** This is a live, unresolved historiographical dispute, not settled fact:
  - Britannica's narrative account of this period (by Asvi Warman Adam and O.W. Wolters) discusses the **Shailendra dynasty and unnamed "rivals"** ruling Central Java, states an Old Malay inscription shows the Shailendras were of Indonesian (not mainland Southeast Asian) origin, and notes there is no solid evidence Central Javanese rulers controlled territory beyond Central Java and the north coast — [Britannica: Central Java from c. 700 to c. 1000](https://www.britannica.com/place/Indonesia/Central-Java-from-c-700-to-c-1000). This account does not name a "Sanjaya dynasty."
  - F.D.K. Bosch (1952) proposed **two dynasties**: a Buddhist Shailendra line and a Shaivite Sanjaya line, ruling in rivalry or parallel — [Wikipedia: Sanjaya dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanjaya_dynasty).
  - Poerbatjaraka countered that there was only **one dynasty** (Shailendra), with Sanjaya and his descendants belonging to it, originally Shaivite and later shifting to Mahayana Buddhism under Rakai Panangkaran — [Wikipedia: Shailendra dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shailendra_dynasty).
  - J.G. de Casparis proposed the two families shared or alternated power between 732 and 882 CE — cited via [Wikipedia: Borobudur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borobudur) (citing Dumarçay's account of de Casparis's argument).
  - A 2025 ISEAS working paper argues the Shailendra were of Javanese origin and that there is no real difference between the "Sanjaya dynasty" and the Shailendras — supporting the single-dynasty reading: [ISEAS Working Paper, 2025 (PDF)](https://www.iseas.edu.sg/wp-content/uploads/pdfs/nscwps12.pdf).
  - **Bottom line:** No scholarly consensus exists on whether "Sailendra" and "Sanjaya" denote two separate, sometimes-rival royal lines, a single dynasty under two names, or two houses that intermarried and shared power. This dataset should not assert a single settled answer.

### 2. Kahuripan / Airlangga's Kingdom (1019–1045)

- **Start:** 1019, when Airlangga (a half-Balinese prince, son-in-law of the destroyed Medang king) was hailed ruler of Pasuruan following his 1017–1019 ascetic period; he began a military reunification offensive in 1028, achieving final success no earlier than 1035 — [Britannica: Central Java from c. 700 to c. 1000](https://www.britannica.com/place/Indonesia/Central-Java-from-c-700-to-c-1000).
- **End:** 1045 (division of the kingdom), with Airlangga's death/abdication and the split dated variably to 1045 or with his death recorded as 1049 in some secondary sources.
- **Founding/ending event:** Airlangga rebuilt the kingdom (Kahuripan) from the ruins of Medang, reportedly after it had been crushed by Srivijaya. Before his death he divided the realm between his two sons: a western portion, **Kaḍiri/Panjalu** (capital Daha), and an eastern portion, **Janggala** — per the Pararaton ("Book of Kings").
- **Sources:** [Britannica: Kadiri](https://www.britannica.com/place/Kadiri); [Wikipedia: Kahuripan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kahuripan); [New World Encyclopedia: Kediri (historical kingdom)](https://www.newworldencyclopedia.org/entry/Kediri_(historical_kingdom)).
- **Dispute flagged:** Sources disagree on whether the division occurred in 1042 or 1045, and on whether Airlangga's death should be dated 1045 or 1049. This dataset should record the division as "1045 (sometimes given as 1042)."

### 3. Kediri Kingdom (1042–1222)

- **Start:** 1042 (per the Airlangga division above; alternate readings place the operative founding in 1045).
- **End:** 1222, when Ken Angrok (Ken Arok) defeated King Kertajaya of Kadiri. A brief revival occurred in 1292–93 under Jayakatwang before Kediri was finally absorbed into the new Majapahit polity.
- **Native name/script:** Also known as Kadiri or Panjalu; in Javanese script, Panjalu is rendered ꦥꦚ꧀ꦗꦭꦸ.
- **Capital:** Near modern Kediri city; the ancient capital's name is given as Dahanapura or Daha.
- **Sources:** [Wikipedia: Kediri kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kediri_kingdom); [Britannica: Kadiri](https://www.britannica.com/place/Kadiri).

### 4. Singhasari Kingdom (1222–1292)

- **Start:** 1222, when Ken Angrok (Ken Arok) defeated King Kertajaya of Kadiri and founded a new polity, also called Tumapel.
- **End:** May/June 1292, when the last king, Kertanagara (r. 1268–1292), was killed during a rebellion led by Jayakatwang of Kediri/Gelang-Gelang.
- **Founding/ending events:** Founded by Ken Arok (c. 1182–1227/1247); under Kertanagara it briefly united eastern Java before his overthrow and killing.
- **Native name/script:** Javanese ꦏꦫꦠꦺꦴꦤ꧀ꦱꦶꦔ꧀ꦲꦱꦫꦶ (Karaton Singhasari).
- **Sources:** [Britannica: Singhasari](https://www.britannica.com/place/Singhasari); [Wikipedia: Singhasari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singhasari); [Wikipedia: Kertanagara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kertanagara).

### 5. Majapahit (1293–1527, conventional; end date contested)

- **Start:** Raden Wijaya (a prince of Singhasari) established the kingdom in 1292 after fleeing Jayakatwang's coup; his coronation as Kertarajasa Jayawardhana on **10 November 1293** (Javanese Śaka calendar) is used as the official founding date — [Wikipedia: Majapahit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majapahit).
- **Founding event — the Mongol/Yuan expedition:** In 1292, Kublai Khan dispatched a Mongol Yuan punitive expedition (20,000–30,000 soldiers, roughly 1,000 ships) to avenge an earlier insult to a Yuan envoy by Kertanagara of Singhasari, who by the time the fleet arrived (landing at Tuban, January 1293) had already been killed by Jayakatwang. Wijaya allied with the Mongol force to defeat and execute Jayakatwang (late May 1293), then turned on his erstwhile allies and routed them, forcing their retreat to China — described as "a strategic victory for the new state, Majapahit." Sources: [Wikipedia: Mongol invasion of Java](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_invasion_of_Java); [Wikipedia: Jayakatwang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jayakatwang); [Britannica: Majapahit empire](https://www.britannica.com/place/Majapahit-empire).
- **Native name/script:** Javanese ꦩꦗꦥꦲꦶꦠ꧀ (Måjåpahit); also known by the Sanskrit-derived synonym **Wilwatikta**, Javanese ꦮꦶꦭ꧀ꦮꦠꦶꦏ꧀ꦠ (Sanskrit विल्वतिक्त, vilvatikta) — [Wikipedia: Majapahit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majapahit).
- **Capital:** Trowulan — [World Heritage Site tentative-list entry: Trowulan](https://www.worldheritagesite.org/tentative/trowulan-former-capital-city-of-majapahit-kingdo/).
- **End — genuinely contested, not a clean date:**
  - The conventional/popular end date is **1527**, tied to the Sultanate of Demak's conquest of Kediri (the last Hindu-Javanese remnant state), per [Wikipedia: Majapahit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majapahit).
  - The same Wikipedia article states plainly: "Dates for the end of the Majapahit Empire range from 1478... to 1527." The 1478 date derives from a Javanese chronogram (*candrasengkala*), "Sirna ilang kertaning bhumi" (= Śaka year 1400 = 1478 CE), traditionally cited as marking the fall — but the article notes 1478 more likely corresponds to an earlier succession crisis or civil war than to a final conquest.
  - A 2024 academic article in an Indonesian university journal argues Majapahit's decline is better dated to **1513–1528**, and states it is "unlikely that an attack from Demak Bintara was the primary cause" of the empire's end — i.e., direct scholarly pushback on the simple "Demak conquered Majapahit in 1527" narrative: [Paramita journal, Universitas Negeri Semarang, 2024 (PDF)](https://journal.unnes.ac.id/nju/paramita/article/download/50310/pdf).
  - Britannica's own account is internally inconsistent on this point: its narrative text says the empire ended "in the late 15th or early 16th century" due to the spread of Islam and the rise of Islamic coastal states, while its Quick Facts box gives a flat date range of **1201–1550** — neither of which matches the more commonly cited 1293–1527 window used elsewhere. This dataset should treat Britannica's date range as an outlier/inconsistency, not a corroborating source for either 1478 or 1527: [Britannica: Majapahit empire](https://www.britannica.com/place/Majapahit-empire).
  - **Recommended treatment:** Record 1293–1527 as the conventional dataset range, but flag explicitly (as this document does) that 1527 is a conventional endpoint tied to Demak's conquest of Kediri, that an earlier 1478 chronogram date is also cited in the literature for an internal crisis, and that at least one 2024 academic source dates the actual decline to 1513–1528 while disputing Demak's direct causal role.

### 6. Demak Sultanate (1475–1554, end date contested) and Mataram Sultanate (1587–1755)

**Demak Sultanate**
- **Start:** Traditionally attributed to **Raden Patah** (r. 1475–1518), a Javanese noble related to Majapahit royalty, also known by the Chinese name Jin Bun (靳文). He was a Majapahit vassal until 1478 and later took the title Panembahan Jimbun after the Wali Sanga (nine Islamic saints) legitimized him as sultan and successor to Majapahit — [Wikipedia: Raden Patah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raden_Patah); [Wikipedia: Demak Sultanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demak_Sultanate).
- **Native name/script:** Jawi script کسلطانن دمق ("Kesultanan Demak") — [Wikipedia: Demak Sultanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demak_Sultanate).
- **End — disputed:** Sources disagree on the exact end date. The French Wikipedia article on the sultanate gives **1548** (death of the third sultan) as the endpoint, while other secondary compilations give **1554** or describe a longer decline extending toward 1588 before the Sultanate of Pajang absorbed Demak's territory — [French Wikipedia: Sultanat de Demak](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultanat_de_Demak). This dataset should flag the end date as contested between roughly 1548 and the mid-1550s, rather than presenting 1554 as certain.

**Mataram Sultanate**
- **Start:** Independence from the Pajang Sultanate is dated 1584 by some sources; the more commonly cited founding date is **1587**, when Sutawijaya took the regnal title Panembahan Senapati — [Wikipedia: Mataram Sultanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mataram_Sultanate).
- **End:** 1755, via the **Treaty of Giyanti** (signed 13 February 1755) between Prince Mangkubumi, the Dutch East India Company (VOC), and Sunan Pakubuwono III. The treaty split the sultanate into the Surakarta Sunanate (east, under Pakubuwono III) and the Yogyakarta Sultanate (west, under Mangkubumi, who took the title Hamengkubuwono I) — [Wikipedia: Treaty of Giyanti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Giyanti); [Wikipedia: Mataram Sultanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mataram_Sultanate).
- **Capital sequence:** Kotagede (founded late 16th century by Panembahan Senapati) → Kerta (under Sultan Agung, r. 1613–1646) → Plered → superseded by Surakarta and Yogyakarta after the 1755 split — [Wikipedia: Kotagede](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kotagede).

## OTHER MARITIME STATES

### 7. Srivijaya (c. 650/683–1377; identity and nature disputed)

- **Start:** The earliest firm inscriptional evidence is the **Kedukan Bukit inscription**, dated 605 in the Śaka era (1 May 683 CE, though some sources give 16 June 682), found near Palembang, South Sumatra, written in Old Malay using Pallava script. It records a *siddhayatra* (sacred journey) by **Dapunta Hyang Sri Jayanasa**, founding a settlement (*wanua*) — [Wikipedia: Kedukan Bukit inscription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kedukan_Bukit_inscription); [Wikipedia: Srivijaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Srivijaya).
- **End:** A Chola naval raid in 1025 (Rajendra I captured Palembang and the Srivijayan king) severely weakened the polity. Its final end is often placed in 1377, when Majapahit forces reportedly sacked the capital — but this 1377 date rests more on secondary/popular sources than on rigorously cited academic literature encountered in this research; treat it as provisional.
- **Native name/script:** Old Malay, written in Pallava-derived script (as in the Kedukan Bukit, Talang Tuwo, Telaga Batu, and Kota Kapur inscriptions) — [Wikipedia: Srivijaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Srivijaya).
- **Capital:** Conventionally Palembang, South Sumatra.
- **Critical dispute #1 — Srivijaya was not identified as a historical polity until 1918:** French historian **George Cœdès**, of the École française d'Extrême-Orient, was the first to formally postulate Srivijaya's existence as a distinct kingdom, synthesizing Chinese textual sources and Old Malay inscriptions that had previously been read separately — [Wikipedia: Srivijaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Srivijaya). Before 1918, the entity later called "Srivijaya" simply did not exist as a named historiographical category; it is entirely a 20th-century scholarly reconstruction from fragmentary sources.
- **Critical dispute #2 — is the Palembang/Chinese-source identification itself sound?** A serious, sustained revisionist critique (the "From Cœdès to Manguin: Srivijaya and the Chinese Sources" series, Parts 1–5, at [Le Minh Khai's SEAsian History Blog](https://leminhkhaiblog.com/)) argues Cœdès's 1918 linkage of the Chinese terms *Shilifoshi* and *Sanfoqi* to the name "Srivijaya," and to Palembang specifically, rested on flawed philological assumptions, and that "no one has produced solid evidence to link those two Chinese names to the name 'Srivijaya.'" This line of argument proposes the polities described in the Chinese sources may instead be located near Lake Songkhla on the Thai-Malay Peninsula. This is an active, unresolved scholarly dispute, not a fringe view that has been dismissed.
- **Countervailing mainstream view:** Britannica states that archaeological surveys near Palembang since the late 20th century have made it "practically certain" the area was Srivijaya's heartland during the 7th–10th centuries — [Britannica: Srivijaya empire](https://www.britannica.com/place/Srivijaya-empire); [Britannica: The Malay kingdom of Srivijaya-Palembang](https://www.britannica.com/place/Indonesia/The-Malay-kingdom-of-Srivijaya-Palembang). The *Oxford Handbook of Early Southeast Asia* likewise treats the epigraphic and archaeological record as supporting Palembang as the founding political center, while noting the polity's relationship to Java, the Thai-Malay Peninsula, and western Borneo remains "complex and still badly understood," and that the political center shifted from Palembang to Jambi in the late 11th century (per O.W. Wolters, 1966) — [Oxford Handbook of Early Southeast Asia](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/42054/chapter/355848271).
- **Critical dispute #3 — was Srivijaya a thalassocracy or a mandala?** Scholars disagree on the fundamental political-geographic nature of the polity: O.W. Wolters framed it as a **mandala** — a web of oath-bound, tributary relationships radiating from a shifting center rather than a bounded territorial state — while other scholars describe it as a **thalassocracy**, a sea-power exercising control over trade routes and port cities without contiguous land control. Both framings remain in active use, representing a live disagreement rather than a settled classification (discussed with reference to Wolters's framework via secondary summaries; the primary academic grounding is Wolters's own work as cited in the Oxford Handbook chapter above).
- **Minority view on capital location:** Indonesian scholar R. Soekmono has disputed Palembang as the capital altogether, proposing the Kampar River / Muara Takus area (Riau) instead — noted in connection with the broader Srivijaya historiography above.

### 8. Sultanate of Malacca / Melaka (c. 1400–1511)

- **Start:** Britannica itself flags the founding date as uncertain, giving the range **"1403?–1511"** and stating founder Paramesvara settled in Malacca "in the last years of the 14th century or early in the 15th century" — [Britannica: Sultanate of Malacca](https://www.britannica.com/place/sultanate-of-Malacca). A separate Britannica biography states the kingdom was founded "about 1400" when Paramesvara, a Sumatran prince who had fled Majapahit forces from Tumasik (Singapore), settled at Malacca — [Britannica: Paramesvara](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Paramesvara). A Portuguese-era source (Eredia) claims a founding date of 1411 — [Wikipedia: Portuguese Malacca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_Malacca). Paramesvara converted to Islam and took the title Sultan Iskandar Shah in 1414.
- **Native name:** Melaka.
- **End:** Portuguese governor **Afonso de Albuquerque** conquered Malacca in a campaign lasting July–August 1511; the city fell on 24 August 1511, Sultan Mahmud Shah fled, and the sultanate was destroyed as a political entity — [Wikipedia: Capture of Malacca (1511)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capture_of_Malacca_(1511)); [Britannica: Afonso de Albuquerque](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Afonso-de-Albuquerque); [Britannica: Sultanate of Malacca](https://www.britannica.com/place/sultanate-of-Malacca) (which states unambiguously that the end date of 1511 is not itself disputed, unlike the founding date).

### 9. Aceh Sultanate (c. 1496–1903; founding date contested)

- **Start — genuinely inconsistent across sources:** [Wikipedia: Aceh Sultanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aceh_Sultanate) is internally inconsistent: one infobox gives a founding year of 1480, while the same article separately lists **"Founded 1496"** and names the first sultan as Muzaffar Syah (r. 1465–1496), then separately lists Ali Mughayat Syah (r. 1514–1530) as "the unifier." The Indonesian-language Wikipedia article and a secondary compilation both give **1496** as the founding year under Ali Mughayat Syah, with a formal coronation as sultan dated **8 September 1507** (1 Jumadil Awal 913 AH) — [Indonesian Wikipedia: Kesultanan Aceh](https://id.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kesultanan_Aceh); [humansaga.net: Aceh Sultanate](https://humansaga.net/empire/aceh-sultanate). This dataset should record 1496 as the working founding date but flag it explicitly as contested, with 1480, 1507 (coronation), and 1514 (under a different framing of "the unifier") all appearing in the source record.
- **Official/native name:** Kingdom of Aceh Darussalam; Acehnese *Acèh Darussalam*; Jawi اچيه دارالسلام.
- **Capital:** Kutaraja (present-day Banda Aceh) — [Wikipedia: Banda Aceh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banda_Aceh); [Britannica: Banda Aceh](https://www.britannica.com/place/Banda-Aceh).
- **End:** The Aceh War (1873–1904) between Aceh and the Netherlands ended with the main resistance leaders, including Sultan Alauddin Muhammad Da'ud Syah II, capitulating in **January 1903**; the Dutch then formally abolished the sultanate and annexed Aceh into the Dutch East Indies. The last sultan was exiled to Ambon in 1907, and lower-level guerrilla resistance continued into roughly 1912–1914 — [Wikipedia: Aceh War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aceh_War); [Britannica: Acehnese War](https://www.britannica.com/event/Acehnese-War); [Wikipedia: Alauddin Muhammad Daud Syah II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alauddin_Muhammad_Daud_Syah_II).

### 10. Brunei Sultanate (c. 1368–present, with a major 1888–1984 interruption to full sovereignty) and Sultanate of Sulu (1405–1915)

**Brunei**
- **Start:** Sources give two close but distinct founding dates — **c. 1363** or **c. 1368** — for the sultanate's establishment under Awang Alak Betatar, who took the title Sultan Muhammad Shah; local historiography places his reign around 1400, a discrepancy the sources do not fully reconcile — [Wikipedia: Brunei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brunei); [Wikipedia: Sultanate of Brunei (1368–1888)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultanate_of_Brunei_(1368%E2%80%931888)).
- **Peak and decline:** Expanded substantially after the fall of Malacca to the Portuguese in 1511, extending its influence through coastal Borneo and into the Philippines; declined through the 17th–18th centuries.
- **Critical caveat on "present":** Brunei became a **British protectorate in 1888** and did not regain full independence until **1 January 1984** — [Britannica: Brunei – History](https://www.britannica.com/place/Brunei/History). This is not a continuous, unbroken sovereign sultanate through to the present; the 1888–1984 interval represents a genuine break in full independence that should be flagged wherever this dataset labels Brunei "1368–present."

**Sulu**
- **Start — contested:** Sources give **either 17 November 1405 or 1457** as the founding date, under Johore-born Sharif ul-Hashim — [Wikipedia: Sultanate of Sulu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultanate_of_Sulu); corroborated as a live discrepancy by [New World Encyclopedia: Sulu Sultanate](https://www.newworldencyclopedia.org/entry/Sulu_Sultanate). Sulu gained independence from Brunei in 1578.
- **Native/Jawi name:** سلطنة سولو دار الإسلام ("Sultanate of Sulu, Abode of Islam").
- **Capital:** Jolo (seat originally Astana Putih in Umbul Duwa, Indanan; moved to Maimbung in the 1800s) — [Wikipedia: Jolo, Sulu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jolo,_Sulu).
- **End:** Effectively ceased as a sovereign polity on **22 March 1915**, when Sultan Jamalul Kiram II signed the Carpenter Agreement with the United States, relinquishing temporal sovereignty while retaining religious authority — [Wikipedia: Sultanate of Sulu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultanate_of_Sulu).

### 11. Pre-Spanish Philippine Polities: Tondo, Cebu, Maynila, Butuan — documented vs. legendary

This is the area of greatest evidentiary disparity in the entire dossier: one polity (Tondo) rests on strong primary-source epigraphy, while several widely repeated "kingdom" claims rest on thin or fabricated sources.

- **Tondo — solidly documented.** The **Laguna Copperplate Inscription (LCI)**, dated Śaka 822 (21 April 900 CE), is written in Old Malay with Sanskrit, Old Javanese, and Old Tagalog elements. It records the cancellation of a debt owed by a man named Namwaran, authorized by "Jayadewa, ruler of Tondo" (rendered *Tundun*), and names related polities Pailah (identified with Pila, Laguna), Binwangan, and Puliran (identified with Pulilan, Bulacan). The scholarly consensus, following Antoon Postma's original identification and translation, accepts Tundun as Tondo. Tondo is separately attested as continuing as a polity to around **1589** — [Wikipedia: Laguna Copperplate Inscription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laguna_Copperplate_Inscription); [Wikipedia: Tondo (historical polity)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tondo_(historical_polity)); [Wikipedia: History of the Philippines (900–1565)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Philippines_(900%E2%80%931565)).
- **Butuan — documented but thinner.** A "Butuan" polity is listed in secondary compilations as existing "before 989–1521," with the **Butuan Silver Paleograph** cited as supporting archaeological/epigraphic evidence — [Wikipedia: Butuan (historical polity)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butuan_(historical_polity)); [Wikipedia: History of the Philippines (900–1565)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Philippines_(900%E2%80%931565)). However, more specific genealogical claims — e.g., that a "Rajahnate of Butuan" was founded by a named "Rajah Sri Bata Shaja" — come from a course-handout-style secondary compilation (Studocu) rather than an independently corroborated academic or encyclopedia source, and should be treated as weakly evidenced pending better sourcing.
- **Cebu — weakly evidenced for the pre-colonial "Rajahnate" framing.** The claim that a "Rajahnate of Cebu" was founded by "Sri Lumay," described as a minor prince of the Chola dynasty, appears in the same secondary compilation and was **not independently corroborated** by Britannica or any academic source encountered in this research. This should be flagged in the dataset as a legendary/genealogical claim rather than a documented historical fact, pending a stronger primary or academic source.
- **Maynila — not separately verified in this research pass.** No independent primary-source documentation for a pre-Spanish "Kingdom/Rajahnate of Maynila" was located beyond general historical-overview tables; this entity needs a dedicated follow-up search before being asserted with confidence (see "What I could not verify" below).
- **The Code of Kalantiaw — a documented forgery, important as a cautionary case:** The so-called "Code of Kalantiaw," long cited as a 16th-century legal code from a supposed ruler of Panay, is now understood to be a 20th-century fabrication. It was written in **1913 by Jose E. Marco** as part of a fictional work, *Las antiguas leyendas de la Isla de Negros*, and presented to the National Library as the "Pavon Manuscript" in 1914. It was exposed as a forgery by American historian **William Henry Scott** in his 1968 doctoral dissertation at the University of Santo Tomas, and the Philippines' National Historical Commission (formerly National Historical Institute) officially declared it a 20th-century fraud with no historical validity (sources differ slightly on whether this declaration came in 2004 or 2005; 2004 appears more frequently across corroborating sources) — [Wikipedia: Code of Kalantiaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code_of_Kalantiaw); corroborating detail at [University of Hawai'i ScholarSpace (PDF)](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/76d56a40-385d-4bf4-a896-368066502f43/content). Despite the forgery, the fake history became embedded in Philippine nationalist commemoration — President Ferdinand Marcos created an "Order of Kalantiaw" honor in 1971 based on it, and a Kalantiaw shrine still stands in Batan, Aklan. The same author, Jose E. Marco, separately fabricated another text, "La Loba Negra," falsely attributed to the Filipino priest Jose Burgos, which entered the Philippine educational curriculum for decades before being debunked — [Wikipedia: Jose E. Marco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jose_E._Marco). This case should stand in the dataset as an explicit example of colonial/nationalist-era historiography fabricating pre-colonial political history, and any Philippine "kingdom" entry should be checked against it for contamination.

### 12. Spanish Philippines (1565–1898) and the American Period (1898–1946)

- **Start:** **Miguel López de Legazpi** established Spanish dominion over the Philippines, reaching Cebu in April 1565 and founding the first permanent Spanish settlement there; he served as the first governor until his death in 1572 — [Britannica: Miguel López de Legazpi](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Miguel-Lopez-de-Legazpi).
- **End of Spanish rule:** The Spanish colonial period ended with the Philippine Revolution and Spain's defeat in the Spanish-American War — [Wikipedia: History of the Philippines (1565–1898)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Philippines_(1565%E2%80%931898)). The **Treaty of Paris**, signed **10 December 1898** between Spain and the United States, formalized Spain's cession of the Philippines (along with Cuba, Puerto Rico, and Guam) to the U.S. in exchange for $20 million in compensation — [Wikipedia: Treaty of Paris (1898)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Paris_(1898)); [Britannica: Treaty of Paris](https://www.britannica.com/event/Treaty-of-Paris-1898).
- **American period:** The Philippine-American War (1899–1902, with the Moro Rebellion phase continuing to roughly 1913) followed the transfer of sovereignty — [Britannica: Philippine-American War](https://www.britannica.com/event/Philippine-American-War). Full independence came on **4 July 1946**, when U.S. President Truman issued Proclamation 2695 recognizing Philippine sovereignty under the Tydings-McDuffie Act (Philippine Independence Act) and the Treaty of Manila — [Britannica: Independence Day (Philippines)](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Independence-Day-Philippines).
- **Nuance on Independence Day:** The Philippines later (1962) shifted its officially observed Independence Day to **12 June**, commemorating Emilio Aguinaldo's 1898 declaration of independence from Spain, even though full international sovereignty was not achieved until 1946 — [U.S. House of Representatives, Office of the Historian: "The Philippines"](https://history.house.gov/Exhibitions-and-Publications/APA/Historical-Essays/Exclusion-and-Empire/The-Philippines/). This dataset should record both dates (1898 declaration vs. 1946 full sovereignty) rather than collapsing them into one.

### 13. Dutch East Indies and the Republic of Indonesia — 1945 declaration vs. 1949 Dutch recognition

- **17 August 1945 — unilateral proclamation, not internationally recognized at the time:** The Proclamation of Indonesian Independence was read at 10:00 Tokyo Standard Time on Friday, 17 August 1945, in Jakarta at Jalan Pegangsaan Timur 56, and signed by **Sukarno** and **Mohammad Hatta**, who were appointed president and vice president the following day — [Wikipedia: Proclamation of Indonesian Independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proclamation_of_Indonesian_Independence). This was not recognized by the Netherlands at the time and triggered the Indonesian National Revolution (1945–1949).
- **27 December 1949 — Dutch government's formal transfer of sovereignty:** Following the Dutch-Indonesian Round Table Conference held in The Hague from 23 August to 2 November 1949 — [Wikipedia: Dutch–Indonesian Round Table Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch%E2%80%93Indonesian_Round_Table_Conference) — the **Hague Agreement** was ratified 2 November 1949 between the Netherlands and the Republic of Indonesia. The Dutch agreed to transfer sovereignty over the entire former Dutch East Indies (excluding West New Guinea/West Irian) to the Republic of the United States of Indonesia (a federal structure combining the republican state with fifteen Dutch-created autonomous states) by 30 December 1949; the actual transfer of sovereignty took place on **27 December 1949** — [Britannica: Hague Agreement](https://www.britannica.com/event/Hague-Agreement).
- **Background on the colonial predecessor:** "Dutch East Indies" (Netherlands Indies) is the historical name for the territory now constituting the Republic of Indonesia, administered from the era of the Dutch East India Company (VOC, founded 1602, dissolved 1799) through Dutch state administration until 1949 — [Britannica: East Indies](https://www.britannica.com/place/East-Indies).
- **Recommended treatment:** Both dates are legitimately citable depending on which definition of "founding" is used. This dataset should present 17 August 1945 as the date of unilateral proclamation and 27 December 1949 as the date of internationally recognized transfer of sovereignty, with the intervening National Revolution named explicitly, rather than picking one date as "the" founding of Indonesia.

## What I could not verify

- **The exact capital and core territory of Srivijaya.** Mainstream scholarship (Britannica, the *Oxford Handbook of Early Southeast Asia*) treats Palembang as "practically certain," but a serious revisionist critique (the Le Minh Khai blog series) argues the Chinese-source identification underlying this consensus is philologically unsound and may point instead to the Thai-Malay Peninsula near Lake Songkhla; a separate minority view (Soekmono) proposes the Kampar River/Riau area. I could not adjudicate between these; the dataset should represent Palembang as the mainstream view while flagging the dispute, not present it as settled.
- **Whether "Sailendra" and "Sanjaya" denote one dynasty or two**, and if two, whether they were rivals or an intermarried/power-sharing pair (de Casparis's model). No consensus exists among Bosch, Poerbatjaraka, de Casparis, and more recent scholarship (e.g., the 2025 ISEAS paper). I could not find a source that resolves this rather than merely restating a position.
- **The precise founding year of the Aceh Sultanate.** Sources conflict between 1480, 1496, 1507 (coronation), and 1514, and even a single Wikipedia article is internally inconsistent on this point. I could not find a single authoritative source resolving the discrepancy; treat 1496 as a working default with the conflict flagged.
- **The precise founding year of the Sultanate of Sulu.** Sources split cleanly between 1405 and 1457 with no clear resolution found.
- **The exact end year of the Demak Sultanate.** Sources give 1548 (death of the third sultan, per French Wikipedia) versus 1554 or a longer decline toward 1588 in other secondary compilations. I could not find a definitive academic arbitration between these.
- **The 1377 end date for Srivijaya.** This date (Majapahit sacking the Srivijayan capital) appears mainly in secondary/popular sources; I could not locate a rigorously cited primary-source or academic confirmation of this specific year within the scope of this research pass.
- **Pre-colonial "Rajahnate of Cebu" (Sri Lumay) and "Rajahnate of Butuan" (Sri Bata Shaja) genealogical claims.** These rest on a secondary course-handout-style source (Studocu) that I could not corroborate against Britannica, an academic source, or a national museum source. Given the documented history of fabrication in this exact area (the Code of Kalantiaw, "La Loba Negra"), these specific named-founder claims should be treated as unverified/legendary rather than documented fact until a stronger source is found.
- **A pre-Spanish "Kingdom/Rajahnate of Maynila" as a distinct, independently documented polity.** I did not locate dedicated primary-source or strong academic documentation for Maynila specifically (as distinct from Tondo) in this research pass; this needs a dedicated follow-up search before being added to the dataset with confidence.
- **Majapahit's exact territorial extent and the unity of its "ecumene."** Britannica states Majapahit under Vijaya controlled Bali, Madura, Malayu, and Tanjungpura, but the broader claim of a unified Majapahit "empire" spanning much of maritime Southeast Asia (per the Nagarakretagama's Desawarnana) is a separate and more contested claim that this research pass did not fully adjudicate; the mandala/loose-hegemony versus centralized-empire question (parallel to the Srivijaya thalassocracy/mandala dispute) likely applies here too but was not run down with dedicated sources in this pass.
