# Vietnamese Dynastic Sequence, 939–1954: Research for Historical Dataset Correction

**Purpose:** This document supports replacing a single flattened "Đại Việt (939–1804)" dataset entity with the actual dynastic sequence, matching the level of granularity currently given to China (individual reigns/dynasties). Every factual claim below is cited to a specific, fetched source. Where sources conflict or a detail could not be confirmed, this is flagged explicitly rather than smoothed over — see also the closing "What I could not verify" section.

---

## 1. Ngô dynasty (939–965/968)

- **Founding event:** Ngô Quyền, a general serving the Vietnamese lord Dương Đình Nghệ, defeated a Southern Han invasion fleet at the **Battle of Bạch Đằng River** in the winter of 938 (commonly dated December 938), using iron-tipped stakes planted in the riverbed to destroy the Chinese fleet at low tide. This victory ended roughly a thousand years of Chinese rule (Bắc thuộc) ([Wikipedia, Battle of Bạch Đằng (938)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_B%E1%BA%A1ch_%C4%90%E1%BA%B1ng_(938)); [Britannica, Ngo Quyen](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ngo-Quyen)).
- In the spring of 939, Ngô Quyền proclaimed himself king and established his capital at **Cổ Loa** (the old Âu Lạc capital near modern Hanoi) ([Wikipedia, Ngô dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ng%C3%B4_dynasty)).
- **Vietnamese name:** Nhà Ngô. **Chữ Nôm:** 茹吳. The polity was formally still styled "Tĩnh Hải quân" (靜海軍), a Tang-era administrative name retained into this period ([Wikipedia, Ngô dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ng%C3%B4_dynasty)).
- **Ending events:** Ngô Quyền died in 944. His brother-in-law Dương Tam Kha usurped the throne; Ngô Quyền's sons Ngô Xương Văn and Ngô Xương Ngập were restored as co-kings in 950. Ngô Xương Ngập died in 954, and Ngô Xương Văn was killed fighting rebellious lords around 965, precipitating the "**Anarchy of the Twelve Warlords**" (965–968), a period of fragmentation ended when Đinh Bộ Lĩnh unified the country in 968 ([Wikipedia, Ngô dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ng%C3%B4_dynasty)).
- **Date discrepancy flagged:** The task brief's date range (939–965) reflects the effective end of centralized Ngô rule with Ngô Xương Văn's death; Wikipedia's infobox instead frames the dynasty as lasting until 968 (i.e., including the Twelve Warlords interregnum as the dynasty's tail end before Đinh unification). Both are defensible periodizations of the same events — see "What I could not verify."

## 2. Đinh dynasty (968–980)

- **Founding event:** Đinh Bộ Lĩnh defeated the rival warlords of the Anarchy of the Twelve Warlords and unified the country in 968. He proclaimed himself emperor (**Đinh Tiên Hoàng**) and — per Wikipedia's etymology note — chose the state name when declaring himself emperor as early as 966, formally adopting **Đại Cồ Việt** (大瞿越, "the Great Viet of Gautama/Great Viet Land") as the country's name, with the capital at **Hoa Lư** ([Encyclopaedia Britannica, Dinh Bo Linh](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Dinh-Bo-Linh); [Wikipedia, Đinh dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%90inh_dynasty); [Wikipedia, Đại Việt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%90%E1%BA%A1i_Vi%E1%BB%87t)).
- **Vietnamese name:** Nhà Đinh (chữ Nôm: 茹丁); as a dynasty/court, triều Đinh (chữ Hán: 朝丁) ([Wikipedia, Đinh dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%90inh_dynasty)).
- **Ending event:** Đinh Bộ Lĩnh and his heir-apparent Đinh Liễn were assassinated in late 979. A child, Đinh Toàn, briefly ruled (979–980) before Queen Dowager Dương Vân Nga transferred the throne to the general **Lê Hoàn** in 980, founding the Early Lê dynasty ([Wikipedia, Đinh dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%90inh_dynasty)).

**Sources:**

## 3. Early Lê dynasty (Nhà Tiền Lê) (980–1009)

- **Founding event:** General Lê Hoàn received the throne from Queen Dương Vân Nga in 980, becoming Lê Đại Hành. He repelled a Song Chinese invasion in 981, and retained **Hoa Lư** as the capital ([Wikipedia, Đinh dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%90inh_dynasty) for succession context; Early Lê Song-war and Champa-campaign facts below).
- Lê Hoàn led a campaign against Champa in 982, killing the Cham king Paramesvaravarman I — an early instance of Vietnamese–Cham conflict pre-dating the Lý/Trần era.
- **Ending events:** Lê Hoàn died in 1005. A brief succession struggle followed: Lê Long Việt reigned only three days before being killed; Lê Long Đĩnh then ruled 1005–1009. Upon his death in November 1009, the court official **Lý Công Uẩn** was elevated to the throne two days later with support from Buddhist clergy, ending the Early Lê and founding the Lý dynasty ([Encyclopaedia Britannica, Ly Thai To](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ly-Thai-To)).

**Sources:**
- [Encyclopaedia Britannica — Ly Thai To](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ly-Thai-To)
- [Wikipedia — Đinh dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%90inh_dynasty)
- [Wikipedia — Lý dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%BD_dynasty)

## 4. Lý dynasty (1009–1225/1226)

- **Founding event:** Lý Công Uẩn (temple name **Lý Thái Tổ**) ascended the throne in November 1009 with the backing of the Buddhist monkhood ([Encyclopaedia Britannica, Ly Thai To](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ly-Thai-To)). In 1010 he moved the capital from Hoa Lư to Đại La, which he renamed **Thăng Long** ("Ascending/Soaring Dragon"), the site of modern Hanoi.
- **Đại Việt naming (the specific question the task asks about):** The state continued to be called **Đại Cồ Việt** from 1009 until **1054**, when **Lý Thánh Tông** (personal name Lý Nhật Tôn) dropped the vernacular Nôm word "Cồ" and shortened the name to **Đại Việt** (大越國, "Great Viet State") ([Wikipedia, Đại Việt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%90%E1%BA%A1i_Vi%E1%BB%87t); [Wikipedia, Lý Thánh Tông](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%BD_Th%C3%A1nh_T%C3%B4ng)). This traditional account derives from the 15th-century chronicle *Đại Việt sử ký toàn thư*.
  - **Important caveat on this name's history:** Đại Việt's Wikipedia entry itself notes the term "Đại Việt Quốc" (大越國) appears on brick inscriptions from Hoa Lư dating to the 10th century — i.e., before the "official" 1054 renaming — and that the name appears in the 15th-century *Đại Việt sử ký toàn thư* but not in the earlier (13th–14th century) *Đại Việt sử lược*. A cited scholar (Momoki Shiro) suggests "Đại Cồ Việt" may even be a transcription artifact or later invention in the compiled records ([Wikipedia, Đại Việt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%90%E1%BA%A1i_Vi%E1%BB%87t)).
  - A Vietnamese academic journal article goes further, arguing that Lý Thánh Tông may never have formally "renamed" the country in 1054 as traditionally recorded, and that "Việt," "Đại Việt," "Cự Việt," and "Nam Việt" may have been used somewhat interchangeably before this date, with the 1054 renaming being a later historiographical construction ([vjol.info.vn academic article](https://vjol.info.vn/index.php/ncpt-hue/article/view/31641)). This is a revisionist/minority position, not the mainstream account, and is flagged here as a documented dispute rather than adopted as fact.
- **Capital:** Thăng Long (1010 onward).
- **Ending events:** Lý Huệ Tông abdicated in 1224 in favor of his daughter, **Lý Chiêu Hoàng** (r. 1224–1225/1226), the only reigning empress in Vietnamese history. The powerful minister Trần Thủ Độ arranged her marriage to the 8-year-old **Trần Cảnh**; she ceded the throne to him, ending 216 years of Lý rule and founding the Trần dynasty ([Wikipedia, Lý Chiêu Hoàng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%BD_Chi%C3%AAu_Ho%C3%A0ng)).
  - **Date discrepancy flagged:** Sources give the transition year inconsistently as 1225 or 1226; this is a well-known ambiguity in the transfer's exact dating (formal abdication/ceremony vs. transfer of court authority) and is documented here rather than resolved by fiat.

## 5. Trần dynasty (1225/1226–1400)

- **Capital:** Thăng Long, moved temporarily to Thanh Hóa in 1398 under Hồ Quý Ly's influence shortly before the dynasty's end ([Encyclopaedia Britannica, Tran dynasty](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Tran-dynasty)).
- **Three Mongol invasions repelled** (the task specifically asks for these):
  1. **1257/1258:** A Mongol force under Uriyangkhadai (a general serving Möngke Khan/later Kublai Khan) invaded and briefly sacked Thăng Long; it was repulsed under Emperor Trần Thái Tông.
  2. **1284/1285:** Kublai Khan's son Toghan (Thoát Hoan) led a much larger force (contemporary figures cited around 300,000 troops) in a coordinated invasion; a joint Đại Việt–Champa resistance effort repelled it.
  3. **1287/1288:** A third invasion by land and sea was decisively defeated at the second **Battle of Bạch Đằng River** (1288), where **Trần Hưng Đạo** re-used the iron-stake river-ambush tactic (echoing Ngô Quyền's 938 victory) to destroy the retreating Mongol fleet ([Encyclopaedia Britannica, Tran dynasty](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Tran-dynasty)).
- **Relations with Champa:** Trần Anh Tông invaded Champa in 1312 and made it a subject state; Champa regained independence in 1326 and recovered further ground under the vigorous Cham king Chế Bồng Nga (r. 1360–1390); after his death the Trần reasserted dominance.
- **Ending events:** The powerful minister/general **Hồ Quý Ly** built a new capital at Tây Đô in 1399, forced Emperor Trần Thuận Tông to abdicate to his three-year-old son ("Prince An"), then had Thuận Tông executed. In 1400 Hồ Quý Ly deposed the child-emperor and declared himself emperor, ending the Trần dynasty.

## 6. Hồ dynasty (1400–1407)

- **Founding event:** Hồ Quý Ly deposed the Trần in 1400 and renamed the country from Đại Việt to **Đại Ngu** (大虞國, roughly "Great Peace/Repose," referencing the legendary Chinese sage-emperor Shun's clan name Ngu). He moved the capital to **Tây Đô** ("Western Capital," in modern Vĩnh Lộc district, Thanh Hóa) ([Wikipedia, Hồ dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%E1%BB%93_dynasty); [Wikipedia, Đại Việt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%90%E1%BA%A1i_Vi%E1%BB%87t)).
- **Vietnamese name:** Nhà Hồ (chữ Nôm: 茹胡); as a court, triều Hồ (chữ Hán: 朝胡) ([Wikipedia, Hồ dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%E1%BB%93_dynasty)).
- Two rulers: Hồ Quý Ly (1400–1401), then his son Hồ Hán Thương (1401–1406/1407).
- **Ending event:** In November 1406, a large Ming army (contemporary figures cite roughly 215,000 troops) under commanders Zhang Fu (Trương Phụ) and Mu Sheng (Mộc Thạnh) invaded, capturing the Hồ capitals by 19 November 1406. Hồ Quý Ly and his son were captured on 16 June 1407 and exiled to China, ending the Hồ dynasty ([Wikipedia, Fourth Chinese domination of Vietnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourth_Chinese_domination_of_Vietnam)).

## 7. Fourth Chinese domination / Ming occupation (1407–1427/1428)

- **Vietnamese name:** Bắc thuộc lần thứ tư ("Fourth Northern Domination"). The Ming renamed Annam to **Jiaozhi** (Giao Chỉ) in July 1407 and administered it as a regular Ming province, with reported figures of 15 prefectures, 41 subprefectures, and 210 counties, garrisoned by roughly 87,000 occupying troops ([Wikipedia, Fourth Chinese domination of Vietnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourth_Chinese_domination_of_Vietnam)).
- Provincial administrators named in the record: Huang Fu (Hoàng Phúc), 1407–1424; Chen Qia (Trần Hiệp), 1424–1426.
- **Resistance:** Early revolts by Trần Ngỗi and Trần Quý Khoáng (1407–1414) failed. The decisive resistance was the **Lam Sơn uprising**, led by **Lê Lợi**, from 1418 to 1427, including the major victory at the Battle of Tốt Động–Chúc Động (November 1426, with roughly 30,000 Ming troops reported killed or captured) and the decisive Battle of Chi Lăng–Xương Giang (1427) ([Wikipedia, Lam Sơn uprising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lam_S%C6%A1n_uprising); [Wikipedia, Fourth Chinese domination of Vietnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourth_Chinese_domination_of_Vietnam)).
- **Ending event:** In April 1428, Lê Lợi proclaimed himself emperor, formally re-establishing an independent state (restoring the name **Đại Việt**), and the Ming recognized him as "King of Annam." This founded the Later Lê dynasty ([Wikipedia, Đại Việt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%90%E1%BA%A1i_Vi%E1%BB%87t)).

## 8. Later Lê dynasty (Nhà Hậu Lê) (1428–1789)

- **Vietnamese name:** Nhà Hậu Lê / Triều Hậu Lê (chữ Hán: 朝後黎; chữ Nôm: 茹後黎) ([Wikipedia, Lê dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%AA_dynasty)).
- **Two internal periods**, which the dataset should distinguish:
  1. **Initial Lê / Lê sơ (1428–1527):** Emperors ruled in their own right. Founder Lê Lợi (temple name Lê Thái Tổ) was crowned 29 April 1428. The dynasty peaked under **Lê Thánh Tông**, who conquered the Cham capital of Vijaya in 1471 (a major blow to Champa, discussed further below). Decline set in after 1497.
  2. **Revival Lê / Lê Trung hưng (1533–1789):** After the Mạc usurpation (below), Lê loyalists restored a Lê emperor as a figurehead in 1533, but **real power lay with the Trịnh lords** (in the north) and, in the south, the **Nguyễn lords** — both nominally serving the Lê throne while ruling their own territories. This is the period the task specifically flags as requiring careful representation (see §14 below).
- **Capitals:** Đông Kinh (= Thăng Long) 1428–1527 and again 1592–1789; Tây Kinh (temporary) 1543–1592; a period of exile at Xam Neua in Laos, 1531–1540, during the Mạc ascendancy ([Wikipedia, Lê dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%AA_dynasty)).
- **Ending event:** The Tây Sơn brothers defeated both the Trịnh and Nguyễn lords in the 1770s–1780s. The last Lê emperor, **Lê Chiêu Thống**, fled to Qing China after Tây Sơn leader Nguyễn Huệ defeated a Qing intervention force at the **Battle of Ngọc Hồi–Đống Đa** (30 January 1789). Lê Chiêu Thống died in exile in China in 1793, marking the definitive end of the Lê dynasty (though the Tây Sơn had already effectively supplanted Lê authority in the north by 1788) ([Wikipedia, Lê dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%AA_dynasty)).

## 9. Mạc dynasty (1527–1592; rump state to 1677) — the usurpation overlapping the Lê

- **The usurpation:** Mạc Đăng Dung, a general who rose through Lê court ranks, forced the abdication of Emperor Lê Chiêu Tông in 1522, installed a puppet (Lê Cung Hoàng), then had both killed and proclaimed himself emperor on 15 June 1527 (reign title Minh Đức) ([Wikipedia, Mạc dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%E1%BA%A1c_dynasty)).
- **Vietnamese name:** Nhà Mạc / triều Mạc (Hán-Nôm: 茹莫/朝莫). The Mạc court continued to officially style the state **Đại Việt** (大越) — i.e., it did not rename the country, only replaced the ruling house ([Wikipedia, Mạc dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%E1%BA%A1c_dynasty)).
- **Capital:** Đông Kinh (Thăng Long), 1527–1592; then **Cao Bằng**, 1592–1677, as a rump state.
- **Overlap with the Lê:** Lê loyalists (led first by Nguyễn Kim, then by his son-in-law Trịnh Kiểm after Nguyễn Kim's 1545 poisoning) restored a Lê claimant, **Lê Trang Tông**, as a rival emperor at Tây Đô in 1533. This produced roughly six decades (1533–1592) of **two rival courts** — a period historians term the "**Nam–Bắc triều**" (Northern and Southern Dynasties, 南北朝) — with the Mạc controlling the north around Thăng Long and the Lê-restorationist/Trịnh faction controlling the south ([Wikipedia, Northern and Southern dynasties (Vietnam)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_and_Southern_dynasties_(Vietnam))). In 1540, facing pressure, Mạc Đăng Dung submitted nominally to the Ming, who proposed splitting the country at the Red River; the Lê/Trịnh side rejected this and continued fighting.
- **Ending event:** Trịnh Tùng recaptured Đông Kinh in December 1592 and executed the Mạc emperor Mạc Mậu Hợp. The Mạc retreated to Cao Bằng, surviving under Ming/Qing protection as a rump state until its final defeat in 1677 (Battle of Cao Bình; the last Mạc claimant, Mạc Kính Vũ, fled to China) ([Wikipedia, Mạc dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%E1%BA%A1c_dynasty)).

## 10. Tây Sơn dynasty (1778–1802)

- **Vietnamese name:** Nhà Tây Sơn / Triều Tây Sơn (chữ Hán: 朝西山; chữ Nôm: 茹西山). Like the Mạc, the Tây Sơn continued to officially call the state **Đại Việt** (大越) ([Wikipedia, Tây Sơn dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C3%A2y_S%C6%A1n_dynasty)).
- **Founding events:** Three brothers — Nguyễn Nhạc, **Nguyễn Huệ**, and Nguyễn Lữ — from Tây Sơn village, Bình Định province, launched a rebellion in 1771 against the ruling Nguyễn lord Phúc Thuần in the south. Nguyễn Nhạc proclaimed himself emperor in 1778, formally starting the dynasty (though the movement itself dates from 1771).
- Nguyễn Huệ, who later took the reign title **Quang Trung**, delivered the movement's major military victories: defeat of a Siamese invasion at the **Battle of Rạch Gầm–Xoài Mút** (1785); defeat of the Trịnh lords (1786); and defeat of a Qing invasion force at the **Battle of Ngọc Hồi–Đống Đa** (1789).
- Quang Trung died in 1792; his son Nguyễn Quang Toản (reign title Cảnh Thịnh) succeeded him, but real power was held by his uncle, Bùi Đắc Tuyên.
- **Ending event:** Nguyễn Ánh — the sole surviving heir of the defeated southern Nguyễn lords — captured Quy Nhơn (1800) and Phú Xuân (1801), then besieged and captured Thăng Long on 20 July 1802, executing Nguyễn Quang Toản and ending the Tây Sơn dynasty after roughly 24 years ([Wikipedia, Tây Sơn dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C3%A2y_S%C6%A1n_dynasty); [Encyclopaedia Britannica, Tay Son brothers](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Tay-Son-brothers)).
- **Capital(s):** Sources are not fully consistent on a single Tây Sơn capital — Quy Nhơn (1778–1788), Phú Xuân/Huế (1788–1801), and Thăng Long (briefly, 1801–1802) are variously cited. This is flagged in "What I could not verify."

## 11. Nguyễn dynasty (1802–1945) and French Indochina (1887–1954)

- **Founding event:** Nguyễn Phúc Ánh, the surviving heir of the southern Nguyễn lords, defeated the Tây Sơn and was crowned emperor (reign title **Gia Long**) on 1 June 1802, with the capital at **Huế** (Phú Xuân) ([Wikipedia, Nguyễn dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nguy%E1%BB%85n_dynasty)).
- **Vietnamese name:** Nhà Nguyễn (chữ Nôm: 茹阮).
- **State-name changes — directly relevant to the "Đại Việt scope" question (§12 below):**
  - 1802–1804: the state was still officially called **Đại Việt** (大越國).
  - 1804: Gia Long requested that the Qing Jiaqing Emperor grant him the title "King of Nam Việt" (referencing the ancient Nanyue); the Qing court, wary of the historical association of "Nam Việt/Nanyue" with territory in southern China, instead designated the country **Việt Nam** — and the name was used officially 1804–1813 (per the "Vietnam" Wikipedia summary) and, per the "Đại Việt" Wikipedia summary's chronology table, through 1839.
  - 1839: renamed again to **Đại Nam** (大南國) under Emperor Minh Mạng; this name persisted through the end of the dynasty in 1945.
  ([Wikipedia, Đại Việt — chronology table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%90%E1%BA%A1i_Vi%E1%BB%87t); [Wikipedia, Vietnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam))
- 13 emperors are listed from Gia Long (1802–1820) through Bảo Đại (1926–1945).
- **French colonization:** The Treaty of Huế (1883) and the Patenôtre Treaty (1884) established French protectorates over Annam and Tonkin; Vietnam's independent existence effectively ended on 9 June 1885. **French Indochina** (Union Indochinoise) was formally established in **1887**, combining Cochinchina (a direct colony) with the Annam and Tonkin protectorates and the Cambodia protectorate; Laos was added in the 1890s ([Wikipedia, French Indochina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Indochina); [GlobalSecurity.org, Union Indochinoise](https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/world/vietnam/union-indochinoise.htm)).
- Japan occupied French Indochina from 1940 and overthrew the French colonial administration on 9 March 1945; Bảo Đại proclaimed the "Empire of Vietnam" under Japanese oversight in March 1945, then abdicated on 25 August 1945 following the August Revolution — ending 143 years of Nguyễn dynastic rule.
- **French Indochina dissolved in 1954**, following the Geneva Accords that ended the First Indochina War ([Wikipedia, French Indochina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Indochina) states it was "established in 1887 and dissolved in 1954"). Bảo Đại's subsequent role as head of state of the State of Vietnam (1949–1955) is explicitly treated by Wikipedia as separate from, not a continuation of, the Nguyễn dynasty.

## 12. The correct scope of "Đại Việt" — is 939–1804 defensible?

**Short answer: No, "Đại Việt" 939–1804 is not defensible as the name of the polity for that entire span.** The name is anachronistic for 939–968 and 968–1054, and wrong for 1400–1407 (Đại Ngu) and 1407–1428 (Ming-administered Jiaozhi). The dataset conflates "the continuous Vietnamese state" (a legitimate historical-continuity concept) with "the state officially named Đại Việt" (a much narrower window). The two should not be the same node.

Per Wikipedia's chronology of Vietnamese state names, drawing on standard historiography ([Wikipedia, Đại Việt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%90%E1%BA%A1i_Vi%E1%BB%87t)):

| Years | Official name | Dynasty/regime |
|---|---|---|
| 939–968 | (No single fixed name yet; Tĩnh Hải quân administrative holdover) | Ngô |
| 968–1054 | **Đại Cồ Việt** (大瞿越國) | Đinh, Early Lê, early Lý |
| **1054–1400** | **Đại Việt** (大越國) | Lý (from 1054), Trần |
| 1400–1407 | **Đại Ngu** (大虞國) | Hồ |
| 1407–1427/1428 | **Giao Chỉ** (Ming province name; no independent state) | Ming occupation |
| **1428–1804** | **Đại Việt** (大越國), restored | Later Lê (incl. Mạc interregnum and Trịnh–Nguyễn era, both of which retained the name), Tây Sơn, Nguyễn (1802–1804) |
| 1804–1839 | **Việt Nam** | Nguyễn (Gia Long) |
| 1839–1945 | **Đại Nam** (大南國) | Nguyễn (from Minh Mạng) |

So the name "Đại Việt" genuinely applies to two non-contiguous windows — **1054–1400** and **1428–1804** — separated by the Hồ dynasty's Đại Ngu (1400–1407) and the Ming occupation's Jiaozhi (1407–1427/1428). It does *not* apply to 939–1054 (Đại Cồ Việt, a distinct if related name, was used 968 onward; before 968 there was arguably no single fixed state name during the Ngô period) or to 1804 onward (Việt Nam, then Đại Nam).

The dataset's specific choice of **1804** as an end-point is correct and well attested — that is precisely when Gia Long changed the name to Việt Nam ([Wikipedia, Đại Việt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%90%E1%BA%A1i_Vi%E1%BB%87t); [Wikipedia, Vietnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam)). The problem is entirely the start-point and the internal gaps, not the end-point.

**On the 1054 renaming specifically:** the traditional/mainstream account (from the 15th-century *Đại Việt sử ký toàn thư*) holds that Lý Thánh Tông shortened Đại Cồ Việt to Đại Việt in 1054 ([Wikipedia, Lý Thánh Tông](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%BD_Th%C3%A1nh_T%C3%B4ng)). This is the standard date to use. But note the documented complications: (a) the name "Đại Việt Quốc" appears on 10th-century Hoa Lư brick inscriptions predating 1054, suggesting informal/parallel use earlier; (b) a Vietnamese-language academic source explicitly challenges whether the formal 1054 renaming as recorded in the chronicles actually happened as described ([vjol.info.vn](https://vjol.info.vn/index.php/ncpt-hue/article/view/31641)). If the dataset needs a single canonical date, 1054 is the correct mainstream choice, but a footnote acknowledging the dispute would be more accurate than treating it as settled.

**Recommendation:** Model "Đại Việt" as two discrete, non-contiguous period-nodes (1054–1400 and 1428–1804), not one span from 939. Separately model Đại Cồ Việt (968–1054), Đại Ngu (1400–1407), and the Ming/Jiaozhi occupation (1407–1428) as their own distinct name-periods, each nested under the corresponding dynasty nodes described in sections 1–11 above.

## 13. Champa (192–1832): sourcing, political structure, and end

**Existence span:** Champa (or, in its earliest phase, its predecessor/associated polity **Lâm Ấp**/Linyi) is conventionally dated 192–1832 CE. In 192 CE, a local official named **Khu Liên** (Ou Lian) led a rebellion against Eastern Han rule at Xianglin, founding Lâm Ấp ([Wikipedia, Champa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Champa)). Wikipedia explicitly states that "the historical relationship between Linyi and Champa is not clear" — i.e., even the starting date rests on treating a predecessor polity as continuous with what later became known as Champa, which is itself a modeling choice, not an unambiguous fact.

**Was Champa one continuous kingdom, or a set of polities? This is the scholarly consensus position, and the dataset's implicit framing (as a single continuous "kingdom") is outdated:**
- Earlier 20th-century scholarship (Georges Maspero, George Coedès) presented Champa as a unified kingdom throughout its history.
- Revisionist historians of the 1980s onward — notably **Po Dharma** and **Trần Quốc Vượng** — rejected the single-kingdom model. Po Dharma's position, as summarized on Wikipedia, is that Champa "was not a single kingdom or centralized state in the manner of Đại Việt, but likely a confederation of kingdom(s) and individual city-states for most of its history" ([Wikipedia, Champa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Champa)).
- The *Cambridge History of Southeast Asia* itself takes this position directly: "Champa, it is concluded, is not a kingdom in the conventional sense of the word... Champa is a generic term for the polities organized by Austronesian-speaking peoples along what is now the central coast of Vietnam... Rather than signifying a 'kingdom' in the conventional sense of the word, Champa should more properly be understood as an archipelagically-defined cultural-political space" ([Cambridge History of Southeast Asia, Vol. 1, PDF](https://uodiyala.edu.iq/uploads/PDF%20ELIBRARY%20UODIYALA/EL45/Cambridge%20History%20of%20Southeast%20Asia%201.%20To%201800.pdf)).
- A middle position held by "a number of modern scholars" (per Wikipedia's summary) is that Champa was unified during some periods (e.g., under a "king of kings" or overlord model, akin to the Southeast Asian *mandala* polity structure attributed to O. W. Wolters) and disunited during others.
- Historically documented **regional principalities** (up to five, north to south): **Indrapura**, **Amaravati**, **Vijaya**, **Kauthara**, and **Panduranga**, each centered on its own river mouth/port and each with its own periods of prominence and its own eventual, separately-dated absorption into Vietnam ([Wikipedia, Champa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Champa); [vietnamkb.com, The Champa Civilization](https://vietnamkb.com/history/champa-civilization)).

**How and when Champa ended — this was a staged, centuries-long process, not one event:**
| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 1471 | Lê Thánh Tông's forces sacked the Cham capital Vijaya and destroyed most of northern Champa; contemporary Chinese reports describe massive casualties and captives. Older historiography (Maspero) treated this as "the end" of Champa. |
| Early 16th c. | Champa reduced to the remaining principalities of Panduranga and Kauthara. |
| 1653 | Kauthara annexed by Vietnam (under the Nguyễn lords). |
| 1697 | Panduranga (Cham) reorganized as a vassal state of Đại Việt. |
| 1757 | The "Principality of Thuận Thành" (the vassalized remnant of Panduranga) is the form in which the last Cham polity is recorded. |
| 1799–1832 | Panduranga's monarchy lost hereditary status; its kings were appointed directly by the Huế court. |
| **August–November 1832** | Emperor **Minh Mạng**, amid a political purge following the death of the Viceroy of Saigon Lê Văn Duyệt (who had previously shielded the Cham from direct annexation), ordered the arrest of the last Cham king **Po Phaok The** (also rendered Po Thak The) and fully annexed Panduranga, reorganizing it into the Vietnamese prefectures of Ninh Thuận and Bình Thuận. This is the conventionally cited end date of Champa as a political entity. |
| 1833–1835 | Two Cham revolts against Minh Mạng's assimilation policies followed; the second, led by the Cham Bani cleric Ja Thak Wa, briefly re-established a Cham state before being crushed. |

([Wikipedia, Champa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Champa); [Cham Studies, "Status of the Latest Research on the Date of the Absorption of Champa by Vietnam" — the scholar Po Dharma's own account of settling on 1832 after earlier proposing 1822](https://chamstudies.wordpress.com/2015/08/21/status-of-the-latest-research-on-the-date-of-the-absorption-of-champa-by-vietnam/); [Cham Studies, "The destruction and assimilation of Campā (1832–35)"](https://chamstudies.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/12/the-destruction-and-assimilation-of-campa.pdf))

**Recommendation:** The dataset's "Champa (192–1832)" node is defensible *as a date range* if it explicitly documents (a) that the 192 CE start date reflects the predecessor Lâm Ấp/Linyi polity, whose direct continuity with "Champa" proper is explicitly disputed in the sources, and (b) that the entity represents a shifting confederation/mandala of regional principalities rather than a single continuously governed kingdom — ideally with the five constituent principalities (Indrapura, Amaravati, Vijaya, Kauthara, Panduranga) modeled as sub-entities with their own dates of absorption (1471, 1653, 1832 respectively for the last three), matching the north-to-south piecemeal conquest.

## 14. Representing the Trịnh–Nguyễn division

**Recommendation: do not flatten this into a single "Đại Việt" span, and do not treat it as a separate dynasty either — model it as two co-existing, subordinate lord-domains nested beneath the Later Lê dynasty node**, distinct from the earlier Mạc usurpation period. Specifically:

- After the Mạc usurpation (1527) and the Lê restoration under a figurehead (Lê Trang Tông, 1533), actual power passed to the Lê-loyalist military strongman **Nguyễn Kim**, then, after his poisoning in 1545, to his son-in-law **Trịnh Kiểm**. This is where the Lê's status as figureheads truly begins, distinct from the Mạc conflict itself (which was outright dynastic usurpation, not lords-behind-the-throne).
- **Nguyễn Hoàng** (Nguyễn Kim's son) was made governor of the southern provinces (1558, later 1572) and broke from the Trịnh-dominated court around 1600, styling himself "Vương" (prince/lord). His descendants ruled the south as the **Nguyễn lords**.
- Formal, open war broke out in 1627 after the Nguyễn refused to remit taxes to Hanoi (an initial refusal occurred in 1620). This produced two rival, nominally Lê-loyal domains:
  - **Đàng Ngoài** ("Outer Region," the North) — under the **Trịnh lords**, ruling in the Lê emperor's name from Thăng Long.
  - **Đàng Trong** ("Inner Region," the South) — under the **Nguyễn lords**, nominally loyal to the same Lê emperor but functioning autonomously from Phú Xuân/Huế.
  ([Wikipedia, Trịnh–Nguyễn War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tr%E1%BB%8Bnh%E2%80%93Nguy%E1%BB%85n_War); [Vietnam Law Magazine, "The Nguyễn Family of Đàng Trong (1558–1786)"](https://vietnamlawmagazine.vn/the-nguyen-family-of-dang-trong-1558-1786-%E2%80%93-its-state-and-law-4330.html))
- The Vietnamese term for the conflict/division itself is **Trịnh–Nguyễn phân tranh** (鄭阮紛爭, "Trịnh–Nguyễn contention").
- The border between the two ran along the **Gianh River** (Quảng Bình), later shifting to the **Linh River** after an effective 1673 peace agreement — a line that geographically approximates the 17th-parallel division used in the 1954–1975 partition of Vietnam.
- **First Trịnh–Nguyễn War:** 1627–1672, ending indecisively with the Linh River border and roughly a century of subsequent peace. **Renewed conflict:** 1774–1777, in which the Trịnh temporarily allied with the rising Tây Sơn movement to crush Nguyễn lord rule (most of the Nguyễn family was killed at Saigon in 1777; only Nguyễn Ánh escaped).
- **Ending:** The Tây Sơn rebellion (from 1771) first destroyed Nguyễn lord rule (1777) and then Trịnh lord rule (1786, when Nguyễn Huệ captured Thăng Long) — both nominal Lê subordinates were eliminated by the same insurgent movement within a decade of each other, which is what then let the Tây Sơn move against the Lê throne itself.

**Practical modeling recommendation:** Represent 1533–1786 (or, for the strictly "two competing domains" phase, 1600/1627–1777/1786) as a sub-period under the Later Lê dynasty node, with two parallel child entities — "Trịnh lords / Đàng Ngoài" and "Nguyễn lords / Đàng Trong" — each carrying its own succession of lords, capital, and dates, both marked as nominally subordinate to (but factually independent of) the reigning Lê emperor. This is distinguishable in the data model from the earlier Mạc usurpation (1527–1592/1677), which was a rival claim to the *imperial* title itself, not a pair of subordinate lordly domains.

## What I could not verify

- **Ngô dynasty end date (965 vs. 968):** The task brief specifies 939–965; Wikipedia's Ngô dynasty infobox instead runs 939–968, treating the Anarchy of the Twelve Warlords period as part of the dynasty's tail rather than a clean break. I could not find a single authoritative source that definitively resolves this as one "correct" end date — both are used depending on whether the criterion is "last Ngô ruler's death" (965) or "final dissolution of any Ngô authority, however nominal" (968). Document both.
- **The 1054 Đại Việt renaming:** the mainstream account (from *Đại Việt sử ký toàn thư*, via Wikipedia) is that Lý Thánh Tông shortened Đại Cồ Việt to Đại Việt in 1054. I could not access the full text of the disputing journal article (only a search-result summary of `vjol.info.vn`) to assess how mainstream vs. fringe this revisionist position is within Vietnamese historiography — flag it as a documented minority position, not a settled fact, but I cannot rate its scholarly weight with confidence.
