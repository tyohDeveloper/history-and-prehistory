## 1. THE AUSTRONESIAN EXPANSION

### 1.1 Dabenkeng (Dapenkeng/Tapenkeng) culture, Taiwan

**Dates:** Start c. 5500–5000 BP (uncalibrated/conventional BP as reported in the source literature; roughly 3500 BC), i.e. "between 4000 and 3000 BC" per one summary; end c. 4500–4200 (4600–4500) BP, with a late/regional phase persisting to c. 2700–2200 BC in some areas. Some individual dated components (e.g. Bajiacun) run 4209–3844 and 3497–3238 cal BC.
- The Cambridge/Antiquity source states plainly: "The Dabenkeng Culture...commenced c. 5500–5000 BP" ([Cambridge/Antiquity, Carson & Hung 2022](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/preceramic-riverside-huntergatherers-and-the-arrival-of-neolithic-farmers-in-northern-luzon/018B349F98B18C1668C944B0B287A678)).
- A Frontiers paleobotany paper places Taiwan's first Neolithic rice/millet cultivators at "4,800 years ago" for the crossing from the SE China mainland ([Frontiers in Plant Science 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2022.962073/full)).
- Radiocarbon evidence from Nanguanli East (Tainan) indicates occupation "5,000 to 4,300 years before present" ([Taiwan Business TOPICS](https://topics.amcham.com.tw/2022/07/digging-into-the-past-archeology-in-taiwan/)) — locator-only-quality popular source but consistent with the peer-reviewed range above; treat 5,000–4,300 BP as the working range for the Nanguanli East component.
- A detailed Russian-language academic PDF (Novosibirsk State University vestnik) gives calibrated dates for individual Dabenkeng components: Bajiacun 4209–3844 and 3497–3238 cal BC; upper boundary "usually cited as 4,600–4,500 (4,200) BP"; late Dabenkeng phase 2,700–2,200 BC ([vestnik.nsu.ru PDF](https://vestnik.nsu.ru/historyphilology/files/a9ece2547f09fdaf3a8c5663b50a984c.pdf)).
- **Note on discrepancy:** the popular English Wikipedia infobox gives "4000–3000 BC" for Dapenkeng (locator only — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dabenkeng_culture)); the peer-reviewed archaeological literature (Tsang et al.; Hung & Carson) instead frames the culture in BP terms as beginning c. 5500–5000 BP, i.e. roughly 3550–3050 cal BC — close to, but not identical with, the Wikipedia figure. Use the cited academic range, not the Wikipedia box, for authoring.
- **Significance:** Dabenkeng is the founding Neolithic culture of Taiwan, bearing cord-marked pottery, polished stone tools, and (per aDNA/archaeobotany) rice and millet cultivation; it is the archaeological anchor for the "in Taiwan" phase that precedes the "Out-of-Taiwan" Austronesian dispersal.
- **Flags:** none additional to the discrepancy noted above.

### 1.2 The "Out-of-Taiwan" model — core claim and evidence

**Dates (per stage, as currently supported in the peer-reviewed literature):**
- Taiwan settled by Neolithic rice/millet farmers from southeast China: c. 5,500–5,000 BP / "about 5,000 years ago" (uncalibrated/conventional BP as reported) ([Oxford Academic, Austronesian archaeolinguistics chapter](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/60672/chapter/526636737)).
- Expansion out of Taiwan into the northern Philippines: "well dated as starting around 4,200/4,000 BP" ([same Oxford Academic source](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/60672/chapter/526636737)); genomic simulation independently converges on "4.1–4.2 kya" for the Out-of-Taiwan pulse, "consistent with the archeological record of an early contact with the Philippines at 4 kya" ([PMC, Early Austronesians: Into and Out of Taiwan](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3951936/)).
- Nagsabaran (Cagayan Valley, northern Luzon) — the key Philippine reference site: earliest ceramics calculated to have entered the record "5430–4290 cal BP (95.4%)" by one Bayesian model, though narrower and more recent published ranges exist (Hung et al.: 3950–3250 BP; Carson & Hung: 4150–3950 BP) — the site's own excavators favor the narrower/more recent end ([PMC, Bayesian chronology of ISEA pottery, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8171956/)). Directly dated domestic pig bone at Nagsabaran: 4448–4246 cal BP ([Cambridge/Antiquity 2022](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/preceramic-riverside-huntergatherers-and-the-arrival-of-neolithic-farmers-in-northern-luzon/018B349F98B18C1668C944B0B287A678)).
- Palau and the Marianas (first "Remote Oceania" landfalls): c. 3,500–3,400 years ago, per the Out-of-Taiwan synthesis in an Oxford Academic chapter ([Cochrane & Hunt, ISEA dispersal chapter](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/34645/chapter/295205670)). Directly, the Mariana Islands: earliest secure radiocarbon dates converge tightly around 1600–1420 BC / c. 3,500 cal BP from Unai Bapot, Saipan, with the oldest reported layer at cal 1612–1558 BC ([Archaeopress excavation report](https://www.archaeopress.com/Archaeopress/DMS/2B0B85F6AB3A4C7C806880BCE401AE48/9781784916657-sample.pdf); [Ethiopia repository chapter with detailed dates](http://ndl.ethernet.edu.et/bitstream/123456789/13360/1/9.pdf.pdf)). A Bayesian re-analysis using shell/charcoal gives first arrival "around 3200–3080 calBP (1250–1130 BC)" — noticeably younger than the raw radiocarbon range, reflecting hardwater correction issues ([Wiley/Archaeology in Oceania](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/arco.5108)). **Field disagreement flagged:** dates for first Marianas settlement range from as early as 1732–1560 BC (Unai Bapot earliest redware) to as late as c. 1250–1130 BC (isotope-corrected Bayesian model) — both are live positions in the literature; do not average.
- Lapita emergence in the Bismarck Archipelago (the bridge into Remote Oceania proper): now dated "3535–3234 cal BP" (95% probability) by a widely cited Bayesian model, or "3453–3283 cal BP" at 68% probability ([Rieth & Athens 2017 Bayesian model](https://iarii.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/03/Rieth__Athens_2017-Late-Holocene-Human-Expansion.pdf)); other groups favor a later start of 3300–3200 or even 3250–3150 cal BP ([Debating Lapita, OAPEN](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/id/a0ef75fa-3f03-4d94-b680-70cb781c03a5/book%20(1).pdf)). **This is a live, unresolved dating debate; see §4.6 below for full treatment.**
- **Significance:** the Out-of-Taiwan model (Bellwood, Blust) is the dominant synthesis linking the ten primary Austronesian language subgroups (nine Formosan, one Malayo-Polynesian), Taiwan's Neolithic archaeological package (red-slipped pottery, Taiwan nephrite/jade, rice and millet), and the staged migration into the Philippines, ISEA, and ultimately Remote Oceania — one of the largest and best-documented prehistoric human migrations.
- **Sources:** [Oxford Academic, Austronesian archaeolinguistics](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/60672/chapter/526636737); [PMC, Early Austronesians: Into and Out of Taiwan](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3951936/); [PMC, Bayesian ISEA pottery chronology 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8171956/); [Cambridge/Antiquity 2022](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/preceramic-riverside-huntergatherers-and-the-arrival-of-neolithic-farmers-in-northern-luzon/018B349F98B18C1668C944B0B287A678); [Archaeopress Unai Bapot report](https://www.archaeopress.com/Archaeopress/DMS/2B0B85F6AB3A4C7C806880BCE401AE48/9781784916657-sample.pdf).
- **Flags:** ⚑ COMMON MISCONCEPTION: the "Express Train to Polynesia" framing (a single rapid, uninterrupted migratory wave from Taiwan straight to Polynesia) oversimplifies what the evidence shows to be a staged process with regional stalling (e.g., a gap of centuries between the Philippines/4000 BP and Palau-Marianas/3500 BP, and further centuries before Lapita and Remote Oceania).

### 1.3 Critiques of the simple Out-of-Taiwan model

**1.3.1 The "Nusantao" / maritime-network alternative (Wilhelm Solheim II)**
- **Claim:** the Nusantao Maritime Trading and Communication Network (NMTCN) model locates the general origin area of Austronesian speakers in the southern Philippines–eastern Indonesia region, not Taiwan, and proposes multi-directional maritime spread (including northward into southern China) driven by trade and communication rather than a farmer migration wave. Solheim links Lapita-related pottery to the Sa-huynh–Kalanay complex of Vietnam rather than to Dabenkeng ([Academia.edu review of Nusantao vs Out-of-Taiwan](https://www.academia.edu/10134524/The_Austronesians_the_Nusantao_and_the_Lapita_Cultural_Complex_A_Review_of_Neolithic_migration_in_SEA_and_Oceania)).
- **Standing:** the source explicitly frames Bellwood's Out-of-Taiwan model as "the generally more dominant and accepted model," with Nusantao positioned as a minority critique that nonetheless "provides insight into theory formulation." This is a secondary/tertiary academic review (Academia.edu-hosted paper), not itself a peer-reviewed journal article — cite accordingly.

**1.3.2 The archaeobotanical/multidirectional critique (Cochrane & Hunt, and related ISEA specialists)**
- **Claim:** there is no single synchronous "event horizon" of farming dispersal from Taiwan across ISEA at 4,500–3,000 BP. Rice was not a common staple in ISEA before c. 2,000 years ago; foxtail millet is essentially absent archaeobotanically; domesticated pigs, dogs, and chickens did NOT move as a single "Neolithic package" — pigs reached northern Luzon from Taiwan by 4,500–4,000 years ago, but dogs do not appear at the same key site (Nagsabaran) until c. 2,500 years ago, implying separate, non-simultaneous introductions (possibly via a second, mainland-Southeast-Asian route for some domesticates) ([Oxford Academic, "The Austronesian Dispersal in ISEA: Steps Toward an..."](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/34645/chapter/295205670)).
- Words for major ISEA/Oceanic cultivated plants (banana, breadfruit, sago, taro, yam) do not reconstruct to Proto-Austronesian and only enter Proto-Malayo-Polynesian after the southward dispersal — read by these authors as evidence the vocabulary, and likely the crops, were picked up from populations already in ISEA rather than carried from Taiwan.
- **Standing:** this is a serious, peer-reviewed (Oxford University Press edited volume) critique that is compatible with a Taiwan linguistic homeland while rejecting a synchronous agricultural "package" migration. It does not deny the Out-of-Taiwan linguistic model outright but substantially complicates the "farming-language co-dispersal" version of it that is often popularized.
- **Sources:** [Oxford Academic edited volume, chapter 295205670](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/34645/chapter/295205670).

**1.3.3 What ancient DNA has added**
- Nature Communications (2014) genomic study: notes the Austronesian expansion "began 4,000–5,000 years ago" likely rooted in Taiwan, but states "the ancestry of present-day Austronesian-speaking populations remains controversial" — i.e., genetics broadly supports a Taiwan-linked source population but does not resolve whether this reflects large-scale demic migration versus more limited elite/language transfer with local absorption ([Nature Communications 2014](https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms5689)).
- A 2015 Human Genetics paper (Human Biology of Austronesian dispersal) concludes that beyond the Philippines, "the primary mechanism for the spread of both [red-slipped pottery Neolithic and Austronesian languages] was acculturation" rather than wholesale demographic replacement — supporting a hybrid model: genuine sea-faring dispersal from Taiwan through the Philippines, but subsequent spread further south and east achieved substantially through cultural/linguistic assimilation of existing populations ([Springer/Human Genetics 2015](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00439-015-1620-z)).
- A 2023 PNAS Nexus genomic diversity paper on Taiwanese Austronesian groups explicitly frames both "Into-Taiwan" (~4.8 kya arrival on Taiwan from the SE China coast) and "Out-of-Taiwan" (~4.2 kya into the Philippines) as separately dated, genetically supported stages, while noting the specific haplogroups involved (mtDNA E1a; Y-chromosome O1a2 subclades) ([Oxford Academic/PNAS Nexus 2023](https://academic.oup.com/pnasnexus/article/2/5/pgad122/7160857)).
- **Significance:** ancient/modern DNA broadly corroborates a Taiwan-linked genetic signal moving south through ISEA, but increasingly supports a mixed demic-plus-acculturation model rather than a pure "farmer replacement" wave — converging with the archaeobotanical critique in §1.3.2 rather than with a strict Out-of-Taiwan demic sweep.
- **Sources:** as cited inline above.
- **Flags:** ⚑ LITERATURE MOVED (ongoing, no single flagged year) — the genetics literature has shifted from early 2000s "demic diffusion" framings toward 2014–2023 "mixed demic/acculturation" framings; treat any source describing pure wholesale population replacement as outdated.

## 2. NEOLITHIC MAINLAND SOUTHEAST ASIA

### 2.1 Da But (Đa Bút) culture, Vietnam

**Dates:** the literature gives materially different ranges depending on source; treat as a genuine open question, not resolved:
- Waikato University thesis / Oxford Academic chapter (Vietnam Neolithic overview): early phase 7000–5000 BC (Hang Sáo, Hang Mòi, Mái Đá Vàng), middle phase to c. 4000–3000 BC (Đa Bút type site, Cồn Cổ Ngựa, Bản Thủy), late phase 3000–2000 BC (Gò Trũng, Làng Còng, Đồng Vườn) ([Oxford Academic, "The Neolithic of Vietnam"](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/42054/chapter/355844411); consistent thesis version at [Waikato Research Commons](https://researchcommons.waikato.ac.nz/bitstreams/47a8ef7b-c841-4478-87d4-0bfc008a590c/download)).
- Directly measured radiocarbon ages (uncalibrated BP) for named Da But sites: Da But site itself 6,430±60, 6,390±60, 6,095±60, 5,710±60 BP; Ban Thuy 5,860±95 BP; Con Co Ngua 5,520±95, 5,140±95, 5,560±95 BP; Lang Cong/Con Trung 4,790±50 BP ([Vietnamese journal, Nguyen Khac Su 2021](https://vjol.info.vn/index.php/VSS/article/download/74526/63331/)).
- A 2025 Radiocarbon/journal paper (Thạch Lạc transition study) states the chronology "accepted by Vietnamese archaeologists" runs "ca. 5000 BCE to 2500 BCE," but that no directly dated Da But site has produced a chronology younger than 3500 BCE — implying the true end date may be earlier than the 2500 BCE textbook figure ([Taylor & Francis, Thạch Lạc paper 2025](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15564894.2025.2474945)).
- An ANU Press chapter gives a narrower "6500 to 4000 BP" range for the shell-midden Da But culture overall, with Cồn Cổ Ngựa specifically "approximately 6000–5000 BP" ([ANU Press, ch022](https://press-files.anu.edu.au/downloads/press/p302491/pdf/ch022.pdf)).
- **Field disagreement flagged:** published start dates range from as early as "7000 BC" (Oxford Academic/Waikato, early cave phase) to as late as "6500 BP" (~4500 BC, ANU Press, midden phase only); end dates range from "2500 BCE" (Vietnamese consensus per Thạch Lạc paper) to "3500 BCE" (same paper's own radiocarbon ceiling) to "2000 BC" (Waikato/Oxford). Do not average — present as a genuinely contested chronology pending a comprehensive Bayesian re-dating (which as of the 2025 paper has not yet been done for Da But as a whole).
- **Significance:** Da But is the culture conventionally used to mark the "second layer" onset in northern/north-central Vietnam post-Hoabinhian — shell-midden sites with basket-impressed pottery and early edge-ground/polished stone tools, directly relevant to the two-layer hypothesis (§2.5).
- **Sources:** as cited inline above.
- **Flags:** ⚑ dating of Da But as a whole remains unresolved/pre-Bayesian; treat any single figure with caution.

### 2.2 Man Bac (Mán Bạc), Vietnam

**Dates:** 1900–1600 BC per the Waikato/Oxford Vietnam-Neolithic overview ([Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/42054/chapter/355844411)); a University of Aberdeen open-access thesis instead gives 2066–1523 cal BCE from radiocarbon on seven charcoal and four human-bone samples ([AURA, Aberdeen thesis](https://aura.abdn.ac.uk/server/api/core/bitstreams/a81db0e6-787c-4786-92e9-c53c59550fff/content)); other sources give "3800–3500 years BP" (≈1850–1550 BC) ([ANU Press ch022](https://press-files.anu.edu.au/downloads/press/p302491/pdf/ch022.pdf)) or "c. 3800–3600 BP" per a bioarchaeology newsletter ([JCU ResearchOnline PDF](https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/27058/1/Southeast_Asia_Bioarchaeology_Newsletter_2008_Issue_4.pdf)). All ranges cluster tightly around 2000–1500 cal BC; use 2066–1523 cal BC (the directly cited radiocarbon range) as the most precise figure, noting the narrower "1900–1600 BC" and "3800–3500 BP" figures as compatible summary approximations.
- **Significance:** Man Bac (Ninh Binh Province, northern Vietnam) is the single most important skeletal series for testing the "two-layer" population-history hypothesis in mainland Southeast Asia — its ~85 burials show a mixed craniometric/genetic population consistent with contact between resident Hoabinhian-descended foragers and incoming East Asian-affiliated Neolithic farmers.
- **Sources:** [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/42054/chapter/355844411); [AURA/Aberdeen thesis](https://aura.abdn.ac.uk/server/api/core/bitstreams/a81db0e6-787c-4786-92e9-c53c59550fff/content); [ANU Press](https://press-files.anu.edu.au/downloads/press/p302491/pdf/ch022.pdf).
- **Flags:** none beyond the narrow range variation noted above (not a genuine field dispute, just precision differences across radiocarbon reworkings).

### 2.3 Non Nok Tha, Thailand

**Dates (per the 2015 Bayesian re-dating, the current authoritative source):** initial Neolithic settlement (Early Period 1, burial 94) c. 1500–1300 BC (68.2% probability); Early Period 3 (burial 79, earliest socketed copper-base axe) 980–900 BC, described as "the 10th century BC"; Middle Period 2 (burial 62) 900–830 BC; Middle Period 3 (burial 38) 840–800 BC; Middle Period 4–6, 8th–6th centuries BC ([PMC, Higham, Douka & Higham 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4575132/)).
- **⚑ LITERATURE MOVED (major, 1966–2015):** the original 1960s/70s excavation (Solheim; Bayard) claimed a fourth-millennium BC date, aligning with the original Ban Chiang claim; this has been comprehensively superseded. A separate 2014 paper independently re-confirms "the initial Neolithic occupation took place during the 14th century BC. The earliest Bronze Age has been placed in the 10th century BC" via new AMS dates on human bone collagen ([JIPA, "The Chronology and Status of Non Nok Tha"](https://journals.lib.washington.edu/index.php/JIPA/article/view/14719)).
- **Significance:** Non Nok Tha is, with Ban Chiang, one of the two founding sites of the "long chronology" claim for Southeast Asian Bronze Age origins (see §3); its redating is inseparable from the Ban Chiang controversy.
- **Sources:** [PMC 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4575132/); [JIPA 2014](https://journals.lib.washington.edu/index.php/JIPA/article/view/14719).

### 2.4 Khok Phanom Di, Thailand

**Dates:** original/still-standard published range c. 2000–1500 BC (occupation founded "about 2000 BCE," seven-stage mortuary sequence ending "five centuries later") — directly stated by Higham & Higham (Bang Pakong River estuary site, Central Thailand): "its cultural layers...accumulated rapidly between about 2000 and 1500 BC," with Phase 1 dated "c. 2000–1900 BC" ([Journal of the Siam Society PDF, Higham](https://thesiamsociety.org/wp-content/uploads/2004/03/JSS_092_0c_Higham_OpposedHumanFigureAtKhokPhnaomDi.pdf)); consistent with a bioarchaeology thesis stating occupation "for up to 500 years from approximately 2000 BC" to "1500 BC" ([dokumen.pub, Health in Late Prehistoric Thailand](https://dokumen.pub/health-in-late-prehistoric-thailand-9781841712383-9781407352947.html)).
- A more recent JIPA paper (Higham & Higham) revisits the dating using human bone, shell, and structural-context material rather than only charcoal, and states this "suggests an earlier occupation than has previously been published" — but the exact revised start/end figures were not stated on the fetched page ([JIPA, "Khok Phanom Di: New Radiocarbon Dates and Their Implications"](https://journals.lib.washington.edu/index.php/JIPA/article/view/15913)). **Mark as: n.a. for the precise revised date — only the direction of revision (earlier) is confirmed from a fetched source; do not guess the new figures.**
- **Significance:** Khok Phanom Di is a key coastal/estuarine Neolithic site documenting early rice farmers' expansion along the Gulf of Siam coast, with an exceptionally rich mortuary sequence (including the well-known "Khok Phanom Di potter" burial) used in arguments about the coastal route of Neolithic dispersal into central Thailand.
- **Sources:** [Journal of the Siam Society](https://thesiamsociety.org/wp-content/uploads/2004/03/JSS_092_0c_Higham_OpposedHumanFigureAtKhokPhnaomDi.pdf); [dokumen.pub thesis extract](https://dokumen.pub/health-in-late-prehistoric-thailand-9781841712383-9781407352947.html); [JIPA revision abstract](https://journals.lib.washington.edu/index.php/JIPA/article/view/15913).
- **Flags:** revision in progress — exact new dates n.a.; standard citation remains 2000–1500 BC pending fuller publication of the revised chronology.

### 2.5 Ban Non Wat, Thailand

**Dates:** initial Neolithic settlement in the 17th century BC; transition into the Bronze Age in the late 11th century BC — both figures directly from the Bayesian modeling paper that redefined mainland SEA chronology: "the initial Neolithic is dated to the sixteenth century, and the transition into the Bronze Age took place in the eleventh century BC" is given for Ban Chiang, while for Ban Non Wat specifically, "Ban Non Wat initial Neolithic settlement: 17th century BC" and "Ban Non Wat transition into the Bronze Age: Late 11th century BC" ([PMC, Higham, Douka & Higham 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4575132/)). The original 2009 Bayesian framework paper for Ban Non Wat itself states: "Neolithic practice now begins in the second millennium and hierarchical state-forming activity is dated to a 'starburst' around 1000 BC" based on 75 radiocarbon dates ([Cambridge/Antiquity, Higham & Higham 2009](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/new-chronological-framework-for-prehistoric-southeast-asia-based-on-a-bayesian-model-from-ban-non-wat/4AA644BD284B1522DF1B4D193422600D)).
- **Significance:** Ban Non Wat is the single most important type-site for the "short chronology" model of Southeast Asian prehistory (see §3) — its long, well-stratified sequence and large, rigorously Bayesian-modeled radiocarbon dataset (75+ dates) underpin the current mainstream chronological framework used across the region.
- **Sources:** [Cambridge/Antiquity 2009](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/new-chronological-framework-for-prehistoric-southeast-asia-based-on-a-bayesian-model-from-ban-non-wat/4AA644BD284B1522DF1B4D193422600D); [PMC 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4575132/).
- **Flags:** ⚑ LITERATURE MOVED (2009) — this paper is the pivot point at which the "short chronology" model gained ascendancy over the earlier "long chronology" claims rooted in Ban Chiang/Non Nok Tha's original 1960s–70s dating.

### 2.6 The "two-layer" hypothesis and recent ancient-DNA conclusions

**Definition:** the two-layer model holds that Southeast Asia's population history involves (1) an initial "first layer" of Late Pleistocene hunter-gatherers (in MSEA, Hoabinhian-associated, related to Onge/Andaman and to Papuan/Australian lineages) and (2) a "second layer" of East Asian-derived Neolithic farmers expanding south from around 4,000 years ago, admixing with rather than simply replacing the first layer.

**What the 2018 ancient-DNA papers concluded (both in *Science*, published together, both fetched directly):**
- **McColl et al. 2018** ("The prehistoric peopling of Southeast Asia"): sequenced 26 ancient genomes (25 Southeast Asian, 1 Japanese Jōmon), spanning late Neolithic to Iron Age. Their explicit conclusion: "we show that neither interpretation fits the complexity of Southeast Asian history: Both Hòabìnhian hunter-gatherers and East Asian farmers contributed to current Southeast Asian diversity, with further migrations affecting island SEA and Vietnam." The two oldest Hoabinhian samples (Pha Faen, Laos, 7950–7795 cal BP; Gua Cha, Malaysia, 4415–4160 cal BP) cluster closely with present-day Onge. Despite the "neither simple interpretation fits" framing, the authors state their data ARE "consistent with the two-layer model" in the specific sense of observing "a change in ancestry by ~4 ka ago, supporting a demographic expansion from EA into SEA during the Neolithic transition to farming" — i.e. they refine/complicate rather than outright refute the two-layer model, identifying results consistent with four ancestral-population contributions overall ([Science 2018 fetched via Monash repository copy](https://researchmgt.monash.edu/ws/files/344873217/343042905_oa.pdf); [Science.org abstract](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aat3628)).
- **Lipson et al. 2018** ("Ancient genomes document multiple waves of migration in Southeast Asian prehistory"): sequenced ancient DNA from 18 individuals (screened from 100+) across five sites, spanning ~4100–1700 years ago. Found two mixing waves: (1) local hunter-gatherers with incoming Neolithic farmers from South China, and (2) a later, distinct Bronze Age-associated pulse of genetic material from China. This adds a THIRD population-history event beyond the simplest two-layer picture ([Science.org summary](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aat3628)).
- **Net assessment:** both 2018 papers are best described as refining, not simply confirming or refuting, the two-layer hypothesis — they retain its core structure (indigenous hunter-gatherer layer + East Asian farmer layer at ~4 kya) but add additional, chronologically distinct migration events (a Bronze Age pulse; separate ISEA/Vietnam-specific movements) that a strict two-layer model does not anticipate.
- **Post-2018 support:** a 2021 craniometric study on female skeletal remains from China, Vietnam, and Taiwan (16,000–5,300 BP) states its results provide new support for the two-layer model using previously underused female-derived data ([Nature Scientific Reports 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-00295-6)). A 2023 Nature Communications Biology paper combining Y-chromosome and tuberculosis-genome phylogeography also reports results "compatible with the Two Layer hypothesis" ([Nature Comm Biology 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42003-023-05388-8)). A 2026 Science Advances paper on Y-chromosome sequences reports "at least five distinct southward migration waves" as part of the second layer, further subdividing the model ([Science Advances 2026](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.ady1597)) — note this is a very recent (2026) publication continuing to refine, not overturn, the framework.
- **Significance:** the two-layer model remains the dominant framework for mainland/island Southeast Asian population history, but the ancient-DNA era (2018–present) has consistently shown it to be an underlying skeleton onto which multiple additional, regionally- and temporally-distinct migration pulses must be added — it is not falsified but is far more complex than the original two-population version.
- **Sources:** as cited inline above.
- **Flags:** ⚑ LITERATURE MOVED (2018) — the ancient-DNA revolution (McColl et al.; Lipson et al., both *Science* 2018) is the pivotal moment when the two-layer model shifted from a craniometric/linguistic hypothesis to one with direct genomic confirmation-with-elaboration. Note ongoing refinement continuing as recently as 2026.

## 3. ⚑ THE BAN CHIANG BRONZE CONTROVERSY (existing entry: revise to reflect below)

**The existing dataset entry gives Ban Chiang as 1600–505 BCE.** Below is the detailed history and current best evidence; the authoring pass should check the 1600 BCE start against the "late 3rd millennium BC" Bayesian Neolithic-settlement dates below, and the 505 BCE end against the Iron Age transition figures — both appear broadly compatible with (though not identical to) the current Bayesian short-chronology consensus.

### 3.1 The original claim (1970s)

Chester Gorman and Pisit Charoenwongsa's original 1974–75 University of Pennsylvania/Fine Arts Department excavation, first reported in 1976, claimed a **fourth-millennium BC** date for the earliest bronze at Ban Chiang — as early as **3600 BC** by some citations — based on thermoluminescence dating of pottery associated with bronze artefacts. This made international headlines as a claim that Southeast Asia had the world's earliest independently-invented bronze metallurgy, predating both China and the Near East ([PMC/PLOS review paper, Higham, Douka & Higham 2015, citing Gorman & Charoenwongsa 1976](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4575132/); [dokumen.pub review of Ban Chiang Vol. 2A](https://dokumen.pub/ban-chiang-northeast-thailand-volume-2a-background-to-the-study-of-the-metal-remains-9781931707442.html); [Oxford Academic, "Whose Prehistory? Thailand before the Thais"](https://academic.oup.com/hawaii-scholarship-online/book/14385/chapter/168343582?searchresult=1)). The 1971 thermoluminescence readings themselves are separately described as having "gave spurious results" ([World Heritage Site community page](https://www.worldheritagesite.org/community/solivagant/ban-chiang/)) — locator-only-quality source for this specific claim, but consistent with the peer-reviewed sources' framing of the original date as later discredited.

### 3.2 The first revision (1982, Joyce White)

Joyce White revised the dating in 1982, moving the earliest bronze grave good from the fourth to the **early second millennium BC** — specifically to a working estimate of **c. 2000 BC** ([Academia.edu / os.pennds.org PDF, "Early East Asian Metallurgy: The Southern Tradition"](https://os.pennds.org/archaeobib_filestore/pdf_articles/bookchapters/2008_White.pdf)). This 1982 revision is the point at which Thailand's claim to the world's-earliest-bronze title was effectively abandoned in mainstream archaeology.

### 3.3 The "long chronology model" (White and Hamilton, 2008–2009, still-current minority position)

White (2008) and White & Hamilton (2009) refined the c. 2000 BC estimate using previously unpublished AMS radiocarbon dates on rice phytoliths and organic temper in burial pottery, arriving at a "working chronology": Lower Early Period 2100–1500 BCE; Upper Early Period 1500–900 BCE; Middle Period 900–300 BCE; Late Period 300 BCE–200 CE. Under this model, bronze appears "as a fully developed technology" already in the Lower Early Period, c. 2000 BC, with the earliest metal grave good (a bent-tip socketed spear point) dating to around 1800 BC ([os.pennds.org PDF, White & Hamilton 2014 chapter](https://os.pennds.org/archaeobib_filestore/pdf_articles/bookchapters/2014_WhiteHamilton.pdf); [Allard review, ISEA Archaeology](https://iseaarchaeology.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/Allard-review.pdf)). White explicitly argues this early-second-millennium date is "too old to have originated from the Shang dynasty" and instead points to transmission from the Seima-Turbino metallurgical tradition of Central Eurasia/Siberian Altai ([PMC 2015 paper, summarizing White's position](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4575132/); [Higham-archive PDF](http://higham-archive.nz/HighamArchive/PDFs/Higham2.pdf)).

### 3.4 The "short chronology model" (Higham and colleagues, 2009–2015, current mainstream consensus)

Charles Higham and Thomas Higham's Bayesian re-analysis, using human and animal (pig) bone collagen rather than charcoal or rice phytoliths, places the Neolithic-to-Bronze-Age transition at Ban Chiang at **1050–955 BC** (Bayesian short chronology model, or SCM). The earliest bronze artefact — a finished bronze socketed spear from Burial 76 — dates to a statistically combined **1025–935 BC**. Across five dated Khorat Plateau sites (Ban Chiang, Ban Na Di, Ban Lum Khao, Non Nok Tha, and Ban Non Wat), the overall modeled transition into the Bronze Age falls in the **late 11th or 10th centuries BC** (1200–1000 BC at 68.2% probability, or 1570–900 BC at 95.4% probability) ([PMC, Higham, Douka & Higham 2015, "A New Chronology for the Bronze Age of Northeastern Thailand"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4575132/); [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26384011/)). The Higham team explicitly rejects White's seven key AMS determinations as relying on "a technique widely regarded on multiple grounds as unreliable" ([Higham-archive PDF](http://higham-archive.nz/HighamArchive/PDFs/Higham2.pdf)).

### 3.5 Current best dates for the onset of Southeast Asian bronze (overall regional consensus)

The current mainstream (short-chronology) consensus, per the Oxford Handbook of Early Southeast Asia and the Higham/Douka/Higham 2015 Bayesian paper, places:
- Initial Neolithic farmer settlement across the Khorat Plateau sites in the **late 3rd millennium BC / 16th–17th century BC** (site-specific).
- First bronze metallurgy region-wide in the **late 2nd millennium BC**, specifically the **11th–10th centuries BC** ([pennds.org PDF, Oxford Handbook of Early Southeast Asia](https://pennds.org/archaeobib/files/original/438f663f57f481f101bef02ad47be73a.pdf); [PMC 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4575132/)).
- A separate synthesis (JIPA "Dating the Bronze Age of Southeast Asia: Why Does It Matter?") states: "the first evidence for the casting of bronze by about 1100 BC," with first rice/millet farmers arriving "towards the end of the 3rd millennium BC" ([JIPA 2020](https://journals.lib.washington.edu/index.php/JIPA/article/view/15411)).

### 3.6 Is the dispute still live?

**Yes — explicitly confirmed as still live by a 2022 peer-reviewed academic source:** "The decades-long debate between the supporters of this long chronology (e.g., Bayard 1996; Solheim 1968; White 2008) versus the sustainers of a short chronology (e.g., Higham and Higham 2009; Higham et al. 2011) is **close to resolution**" — note "close to" rather than "resolved" ([Oxford Academic, "The Origins of the Bronze Age in Mainland Southeast Asia," 2022](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/42054/chapter/355846103?searchresult=1)). A 2021 book review states plainly: "the debate about the more precise timing of the first bronzes in Southeast Asia **continues to this date**, with White proposing a date closer to 2000 BCE and Higham suggesting the mid to late 2nd millennium BCE" ([ISEA Archaeology, Allard review](https://iseaarchaeology.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/Allard-review.pdf)). Higham's own 2020 Journal of the Siam Society piece frames the difference as the LCM's "circa 2000–1800 BC" versus his own "more than 400 radiocarbon dates...unanimously plac[ing] this transition in the range circa 1200–1000 BC," describing the gap as roughly 800–1000 years ([Higham-archive PDF](http://higham-archive.nz/HighamArchive/PDFs/Higham4.pdf)).

**Balance of evidence:** the overwhelming majority of independently reviewed radiocarbon/AMS determinations (over 400, per Higham 2020) support the short chronology; White's long chronology rests on a much smaller set of seven AMS determinations using organic temper and rice phytoliths, methods the short-chronology camp characterizes as unreliable. The short chronology (bronze onset c. 1100–1000 BC) is therefore the current mainstream position, but White and collaborators continue to actively defend the long chronology (c. 2000–1800 BC) in peer-reviewed publications as recently as 2018–2020 ([Academia.edu, White & Hamilton 2018 vol. 2B](https://www.academia.edu/114724130/Joyce_C_white_and_Elizabeth_G_Hamilton_editors_2018_Ban_Chiang_Northeast_Thailand_Vol_2B_Metals_and_Related_Evidence_from_Ban_Chiang_Ban_Tong_Ban_Phak_Top_and_Don_Klang)).

### 3.7 Flags

- ⚑ COMMON MISCONCEPTION: Ban Chiang is still sometimes popularly cited (especially in tourism/heritage contexts) as evidence of "the world's earliest bronze," a claim definitively abandoned by professional archaeology since White's 1982 revision — 40+ years ago. The current lower bound for SE Asian bronze (c. 1100–1000 BC under the mainstream short chronology, or c. 2000–1800 BC even under the rival long chronology) is centuries to millennia later than Near Eastern (c. 3300 BC) or Chinese Erlitou/Shang (c. 1900–1600 BC) bronze traditions either way.
- ⚑ LITERATURE MOVED (1982; then again 2009–2015): two major shifts — the 1982 White revision from 4th-millennium to 2nd-millennium BC, and the 2009–2015 Higham Bayesian short-chronology results that moved the consensus onset from c. 2000–1800 BC to c. 1100–1000 BC.
- The long-vs-short dispute is STILL ACTIVELY LIVE in the peer-reviewed literature as of the most recent sources fetched (2022 characterization: "close to resolution," not resolved).

## 4. OCEANIA BEFORE LAPITA

### 4.1 Near Oceania — Pleistocene colonization baseline (context for early Holocene continuity)

**Dates:** Sahul/Near Oceania first reached by c. 45,000 years ago (Bismarck Archipelago) per Oxford Academic overview, with AMS dates of "~43,000 years b.p." for earliest Bismarck occupation ([Oxford Academic, "The Peopling of Sahul and Near Oceania"](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/34645/chapter/295205363)). Colonization of the Bismarck Archipelago is elsewhere dated "44–39 ka" based on Matenkupkum and Buang Merabak, New Ireland ([Max Planck repository PDF](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_3520545_5/component/file_3520557/content); [Royal Society Phil Trans B 2022](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rstb.2020.0495)). Solomon Islands reached by c. 29,000–35,000 BP ([PMC, "Origins and dispersals of Pacific peoples"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC428491/); [Australian Museum publication](https://publications.australian.museum/2004-pleistocene-colonisation-of-the-bismarck-archipelago-new-evidence-from-west-new-britain/)). This pre-dates the dataset's window but is retained here for continuity into the early Holocene material below.
- **Note:** this range (~45,000–39,000 BP for Near Oceania entry) is not identical to the already-in-dataset Sahul entry's "to 10,050 BCE" figure — that figure evidently marks a different threshold (likely the end of the Pleistocene land-bridge phase, not first arrival); no contradiction, different events.

### 4.2 Bismarck Archipelago obsidian trade

**Dates and character of the trade, early Holocene through Lapita:**
- Obsidian trade in the Bismarck Archipelago has been continuously documented in the archaeological record from **20,000 years ago** onward, per a DOE/OSTI-hosted archaeological report; Talasea (West New Britain, Willaumez Peninsula) and Mopir sources dominate ([OSTI report AU9917997](https://www.osti.gov/etdeweb/servlets/purl/347110)).
- Specifically: Talasea obsidian reached the Buang Merabak site (central New Ireland) by c. **20,000 BP**, in small quantities; other-source obsidian reached the Matenbak site (southern New Ireland) at **20,000–18,000 BP** (late Pleistocene) and continuing through the Holocene ([National Museum of Ethnology Japan, Late Pleistocene–Holocene Seafaring paper](https://minpaku.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/2000374/files/KH_049_3_02.pdf)).
- Lou Island obsidian reached the Manus mainland by the terminal Pleistocene, **13,000 BP** ([same source](https://minpaku.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/2000374/files/KH_049_3_02.pdf)). By early Holocene, obsidian from Pam Island appeared in the Pamwak site "around 12,000 bp," with Lou Island's Wekwok/Baun sources following at "about 7000 bp" ([Australian Museum memoir, "Archaeological Studies of the Middle and Late Holocene"](https://media.australian.museum/media/Uploads/Journals/18058/1477_complete.pdf)).
- During the Pleistocene (20,000–10,000 years ago), the OSTI report specifies obsidian moved repeatedly to New Ireland (600 km distance), with the Mopir source predominating; from 3,500–1,500 years ago Mopir became rare, likely due to source disruption by a major volcanic eruption ([OSTI report](https://www.osti.gov/etdeweb/servlets/purl/347110)).
- In the Early Lapita phase specifically (3,300–3,000 BP), Talasea obsidian dominated all Bismarck Archipelago assemblages and was traded to New Ireland, Mussau, the Duke of York Islands, Nissan, and mainland New Guinea; Admiralties (Lou) obsidian in this phase was restricted to Manus Island itself ([National Museum of Ethnology Japan paper](https://minpaku.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/2000374/files/KH_049_3_02.pdf)).
- **Significance:** this is one of the longest continuously operating exchange networks documented anywhere in world prehistory (c. 20,000 years, Pleistocene into Lapita), demonstrating sustained maritime connectivity in Near Oceania millennia before the Neolithic/Lapita "package" arrives — directly relevant to arguments (cf. §1.3.1–1.3.2) that ISEA/Near Oceania societies were not passive recipients of a single southward wave but already networked, seafaring, and exchanging.
- **Sources:** as cited inline above.
- **Flags:** none — this is a well-established, non-controversial chronology across multiple independent excavation reports.

### 4.3 Early Holocene Near Oceania (post-Pleistocene continuity, pre-Lapita)

- Cave sites in the Bismarck Archipelago with late Pleistocene faunal remains "appear to be abandoned in the mid-Holocene," per a PMC genetics/archaeology review paper ([PMC, "Origins and dispersals of Pacific peoples"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC428491/)) — indicating some discontinuity in site occupation between the Pleistocene and the Neolithic/Lapita horizon, though obsidian movement (§4.2) continued.
- A 2022 Royal Society paper reports the oldest human/hominin fossil outside the mainland New Guinea-Aru area dates to approximately **11,800 years ago**, and documents persistent reliance on interior tropical-forest resources among Late Pleistocene–Holocene populations on Bismarck Archipelago and Vanuatu islands, complicating a "specialized coastal adaptation" model for these early seafarers ([Royal Society Phil Trans B 2022](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rstb.2020.0495)).
- **Significance:** establishes that Near Oceania was not empty or abandoned in the early-to-mid Holocene gap before Lapita, but shows a more complex settlement/resource-use pattern than either a purely coastal-specialist or purely Lapita-originated model would predict.
- **Sources:** as cited inline above.

### 4.4 Torres Strait

**Dates:** genuinely disputed and actively being revised. Two clusters of evidence:
1. **The "late Holocene colonisation" position (older consensus, still cited):** multiple radiocarbon-dated shell midden excavations (Sokoli, Ormi on Dauar; Badu) place secure, sustained occupation beginning at **c. 2,500 BP**, with some individual dates as early as 2,800–3,000 BP; Britannica and multiple Queensland Museum/JCU sources state Torres Strait islands "have been inhabited for at least 2,500 years" ([Queensland Museum memoir](https://www.museum.qld.gov.au/assets/media/project/qm/qm-website/collections-and-research/memoirs/cultural-memoirs/culture-volume-3/mqm-c3-1-1-barham-et-al.pdf); [Australian Archaeology, Carter 2001](https://australianarchaeology.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/11/Carter-2001.pdf); [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Torres-Strait-Islands)). A 2025 geoarchaeological study at Ormi/Dauar narrows the earliest occupation phase there to **2600–2250 cal BP** ([Taylor & Francis 2025](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14614103.2025.2522542)), and states explicitly: "there is currently no archaeological evidence of human occupation on the Torres land bridge during the Late Pleistocene and early Holocene."
2. **The "early-to-mid-Holocene phased occupation" position (revising the above upward, based on Badu and Dabangay):** excavations at Badu 15 (Badu Island) reveal three phases — permanent occupation **8000–6000 cal BP**, "fleeting presence" **6000–3500 cal BP**, and renewed permanent/recurrent occupation **after 3500 cal BP** ([ANU Open Research Repository, "Badu 15 and Papuan-Austronesian settlement of Torres Strait"](https://openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au/items/2eb1ae78-2587-49b5-beb6-9bf3e7d82720); [JCU journal PDF, re-excavation of Dabangay](https://journals.jcu.edu.au/index.php/qar/article/download/221/216/243)). Dabangay (Mabuyag Island) independently shows sustained settlement **7239–4901 cal BP**, described as "the earliest direct evidence for human subsistence activities in Torres Strait," including the earliest direct evidence of dugong/marine turtle hunting at **6480–6256 cal BP** ([Australian Archaeological Association PDF](https://australianarchaeologicalassociation.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2013/06/AA76-Dabangay-Wright-200dpi.pdf)). A 2024 academic review states occupation extends "back to the time of strait formation and marine resource use at least 7000 years ago," under the title "Torres Strait: Seascape Archaeologies Reveal 9000 Years of Dynamic Maritime Cultural History" ([Academia.edu 2024 paper](https://www.academia.edu/117142611/Torres_Strait_Seascape_Archaeologies_Reveal_9000_Years_of_Dynamic_Maritime_Cultural_History)).
- **Field disagreement flagged, NOT to be averaged:** the "c. 2,500 BP" figure (older, still widely cited, especially in eastern Torres Strait excavations like Sokoli/Ormi/Dauar) and the "8,000–6,000 BP earliest permanent occupation, with an intervening low-intensity/fleeting-presence phase 6,000–3,500 BP" figure (from western Torres Strait sites, Badu and Mabuyag) are BOTH currently supported by directly excavated, radiocarbon-dated sites — they are not necessarily contradictory once understood as describing different islands/sub-regions with different settlement histories (western vs. eastern strait), but the popular/tourism-facing "2,500 years" figure should not be presented as the definitive age for the whole Strait.
- Formation of the Strait itself (submergence of the Sahul/New Guinea–Australia land bridge): "approximately 8,000 years ago" per multiple sources ([Simple Wikipedia — locator only](https://simple.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torres_Strait); consistent with peer-reviewed [National Rock Garden/phys.org summary of Aboriginal oral-tradition/sea-level research](https://www.nationalrockgarden.com.au/featured-article/deep-time-and-not-so-deep-time-indigenous-stories-of-coastal-inundation/), which states post-glacial sea level reached its present level "around 7,000 years ago," and that Torres Strait — being very shallow — was "one of the last parts of Australia's continental shelf to be flooded"); some sources instead give "approximately 12,000 years ago" for the land bridge's final submergence ([Wikipedia locator only](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torres_Strait_Islands); consistent with a JCU peer-reviewed academic source: "current archaeological research suggests that first human occupation of the Torres Strait Islands occurred sometime between 2500 and 3000 cal b.p." — this source addresses first occupation, not land-bridge formation timing, so is not directly comparable). **Use "c. 8,000–7,000 years ago" (non-Wikipedia, peer-reviewed sourced) as the better-supported figure for strait formation/final flooding, treating "12,000 years ago" as a separate, less well-supported claim requiring further primary-source verification (n.a. for now on that higher figure).**
- **Significance:** Torres Strait is the critical maritime corridor between Sahul/New Guinea and Australia; its settlement history bears directly on models of cultural transmission (dingo, pottery, subsistence technology) between Papua New Guinea and mainland Australia, and on debates about when/whether early Holocene populations maintained trans-strait contact after the land bridge was severed.
- **Sources:** as cited inline above.
- **Flags:** ⚑ actively debated regional chronology — do not present a single "Torres Strait was first settled at X BP" figure without specifying which sub-region/site is meant.

### 4.5 The Australian mid-Holocene: dingo arrival, backed-artefact proliferation, and population change

**4.5.1 Dingo arrival — ⚑ RECENTLY RE-DATED**

- **Oldest directly dated (AMS radiocarbon on bone) evidence:** Madura Cave, Nullarbor Plain, South Australia — **3348–3081 cal BP** (i.e. roughly 1400–1130 BCE), reported in a 2018 Scientific Reports paper as "currently the oldest reliable date for the dingo in Australia," with the authors stating this date "is very likely close to the time of first arrival of this species" ([Nature Scientific Reports, Balme et al. 2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-28324-x); also reported by [UWA news](https://www.news.uwa.edu.au/archive/2018072010816/research/dingoes-may-provide-clues-understanding-how-australia-evolved/) as "3,348 and 3,081 years ago").
- **⚑ LITERATURE MOVED (2024):** a subsequent ancient-DNA study using 42 ancient dingo genome samples (up to 2,746 years old, per the underlying fossils) revised the arrival window: "researchers date the arrival of the iconic canines in Australia through Pacific traders to between 3000 and 8000 years ago" ([Science.org news article on the 2024 ANU-led study](https://www.science.org/content/article/ancient-dna-analysis-reveals-dingoes-have-been-australia-thousands-years)); the ANU press release states more specifically "dingoes arrived in Australia more than 3,000 years ago, most likely transported by seafaring people" ([ANU news 2024](https://www.anu.edu.au/news/all-news/ancient-dna-study-sheds-new-light-on-dingo-ancestry)).
- **Older/superseded molecular-clock estimates (for contrast — do not use as current):** mitochondrial control-region studies had suggested arrival "up to 5,000 years" or even "18,000 years ago" via whole-mitogenome divergence estimates of "6 to 10 ka" ([Monash repository PDF, review of dingo genetic evidence](https://researchmgt.monash.edu/ws/portalfiles/portal/274696943/273753356_oa.pdf); consistent with [Science.org, "How did the dingo get to Australia?"](https://www.science.org/content/article/how-did-dingo-get-australia), which cites "5000 and 12,000 years ago" from other DNA studies).
- **Field disagreement, current standing:** the directly-dated fossil evidence (3348–3081 cal BP, Madura Cave) is the most secure minimum age; the 2024 ancient-genome study's "3,000–8,000 years ago" range reflects that older genetic lineages may predate the oldest directly dated bone, but does not overturn the ~3,000 BP fossil floor. Both figures are legitimately citable but for different claims: **3348–3081 cal BP is the oldest DIRECTLY dated specimen; "up to 8,000 years ago" is a genetic upper-bound estimate for lineage divergence, not a specific arrival event.**
- **Significance:** the dingo's arrival (regardless of the exact date within this range) marks a documented instance of trans-oceanic animal translocation into Australia in the mid-to-late Holocene, evidencing contact with Island Southeast Asian/Wallacean seafarers (candidate source populations include Sulawesi and Borneo, per genetic sourcing studies) well before European contact, and closely tracks the same general timeframe as the backed-artefact proliferation and population-growth signal below.
- **Sources:** as cited inline above.
- **Flags:** ⚑ LITERATURE MOVED (2018 fossil re-dating; 2024 ancient-genome re-dating) — treat any citation of "5,000 years" or older as reflecting the superseded molecular-clock estimates rather than the current directly-dated fossil/genome evidence.

**4.5.2 Backed-artefact proliferation**

- Backed artefacts (small, deliberately blunted stone tools) are documented in Australia from the terminal Pleistocene/early Holocene in some regions (e.g., early Holocene deposits in southeast Australia; late Pleistocene deposits in northern Australia are also reported), but their **dramatic increase in abundance ("proliferation")** is dated to **c. 4,000–3,500 cal BP**, per a peer-reviewed Australian Museum publication: "after initially appearing at least 8,500 years ago, backed artefacts greatly increased in number between 4,000 and 3,500 years ago to the extent that they are found in numerous sites and are recorded in large numbers in individual sites from that time" ([Australian Museum publication](https://publications.australian.museum/the-changing-abundance-of-backed-artefacts-in-south-eastern-australia-a-response-to-holocene-climate-change/)).
- A companion academic paper on the same dataset frames the proliferation as "a response to Holocene climate change," with a further "dramatic increase in backed artefact production coinciding with a cooler-drier climate and intensified ENSO patterns between approximately 3,300 and 1,970 cal BP" ([Academia.edu paper](https://www.academia.edu/15441812/The_changing_abundance_of_backed_artefacts_in_south_eastern_Australia_a_response_to_Holocene_climate_change)).
- The Australian Museum journal (Changing Perspectives in Australian Archaeology) similarly links the timing to "the mid-Holocene increase in the magnitude and frequency of ENSO driven climate cycles" ([Australian Museum journal PDF](https://journals.australian.museum/media/Uploads/Journals/21557/1570_complete.pdf)).
- **Significance:** the backed-artefact proliferation is one of the most widely cited archaeological signatures of the Australian "mid-Holocene intensification" debate — interpreted by most researchers as evidence of technological/behavioral adaptation to a climatically more variable environment (heightened ENSO activity) rather than migration or population replacement, though it correlates suggestively with the population-growth signal below.
- **Sources:** as cited inline above.
- **Flags:** ⚑ COMMON MISCONCEPTION: backed artefacts are sometimes popularly framed as appearing suddenly at 4,000 BP; the peer-reviewed record instead shows low-level production from at least 8,500 years ago (and possibly Pleistocene in some regions, per other sources), with the 4,000–3,500 BP event being specifically a proliferation/intensification in quantity, not a first appearance.

**4.5.3 Australian mid-Holocene population change / "intensification"**

- A PMC paper using radiocarbon-date density as a population proxy concludes Australian population growth was "low for the first part of the Holocene but accelerated about 5,000 years ago," with two phases: "slow or negligible before 5,000 years ago, and faster since then" ([PMC, "Reconstructing the dynamics of ancient human populations," 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3203495/)).
- A separate, independently modeled population curve (PMC 2013) instead identifies population growth "beginning during the Holocene transition (approx. 12 ka)" with subsequent growth "in pulses (approx. 8.3–6.6, 4.4–3.7 and 1.6–0.4 ka)," concluding growth was "part of a longer process...beginning in the Terminal Pleistocene" rather than a sudden mid-Holocene event ([PMC, "A new population curve for prehistoric Australia," 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3652441/)).
- **Field disagreement flagged:** the 2011 paper frames population change as a single acceleration beginning c. 5,000 BP; the 2013 paper (different radiocarbon dataset/method) instead identifies a longer, pulsed growth pattern beginning as early as 12,000 BP, with one of several growth pulses in the 4,400–3,700 BP window. Both are peer-reviewed, PMC-hosted, and use different underlying datasets/methods — present both without averaging.
- The classic "intensification" thesis (Lourandos) — that Aboriginal society was transformed via increased economic/social complexity in the mid-Holocene — is described by a 2021 Oxford Academic chapter as supported by "archaeological temporal trends (radiocarbon dates), as well as skeletal, cemetery, and rock art data," which "all suggest increasing population in the Late Pleistocene, continuing throughout the Early Holocene, and peaking in the Mid- to Late Holocene" ([Oxford Academic, "Continuation of Intensification, Population Change..."](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/35424/chapter/303183126?searchresult=1)).
- A 2015 PMC paper frames the full sequence as: rapid expansion/growth 9,000–6,000 BP (Holocene climatic optimum); intensification of mobility/technology 4,000–2,000 BP correlating with ENSO onset/intensification ([PMC, "Holocene Demographic Changes and the Emergence of Complex..."](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4471166/)).
- **Significance:** the mid-Holocene "intensification" debate (backed artefacts + population growth + technological/social change, all clustering loosely around 4,000–3,000 BP) is one of the longest-running debates in Australian archaeology; current evidence supports real demographic and behavioral change in this window, correlated with (and likely partly driven by) the onset/intensification of El Niño–Southern Oscillation climate variability in the mid-to-late Holocene, though the precise start date and whether it was a single event or multiple pulses remains disputed (see above).
- **Sources:** as cited inline above.
- **Flags:** field disagreement on the exact timing/pulse-structure of Holocene population growth (noted above) — do not present a single uncontested date for "the" Australian mid-Holocene population increase.

### 4.6 ⚑ Earliest Lapita dating — a live, unresolved debate (cross-reference to §1.4)

Multiple independent, methodologically distinct Bayesian radiocarbon models currently disagree on the precise onset of Lapita in the Bismarck Archipelago:
- **3535–3234 cal BP (95% probability)** or **3453–3283 cal BP (68% probability)** — Rieth & Athens 2017 Bayesian model, Mussau Islands ([Rieth & Athens 2017 PDF](https://iarii.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/03/Rieth__Athens_2017-Late-Holocene-Human-Expansion.pdf)); consistent with Denham et al.'s earlier "3470–3250 cal BP, 68.2% probability" for Mussau ([cited within the same paper]).
- **3300–3200 cal BP**, or as early as **3450–3350 cal BP** per Specht 2007, per a synthesis in "Debating Lapita" ([OAPEN PDF](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/id/a0ef75fa-3f03-4d94-b680-70cb781c03a5/book%20(1).pdf)).
- A more conservative recent view states "the earliest dates for sites in the Bismarcks are now generally accepted as being no earlier than **3200 BP**" per Kirch 2021 ([Oxford Academic, "Lapita in the Southwest Pacific" 2023](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/46866/chapter/444253685?guestAccessKey=)).
- A 2025 Nature Scientific Reports paper cites the Bismarcks emergence date as **3535–3234 cal BP** while separately noting "recent researchers dismissing a posited Lapita source in the Marianas" as an alternative colonization-order hypothesis ([Nature Scientific Reports 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-20856-3)).
- **Standing:** this is explicitly NOT resolved — estimates for the earliest Lapita in the Bismarcks span roughly 3550 to 3200 cal BP depending on which site (Mussau vs. mainland Bismarcks), which calibration/Bayesian model, and which underlying radiocarbon dataset is used. Present as an open range (c. 3550–3200 cal BP / roughly 1600–1250 BCE) rather than a single figure, and flag any single-date citation as a simplification.
- **Cross-check against existing dataset entry:** the dataset's existing Lapita entry (1501–734 BCE) has a start point (1501 BCE ≈ 3451 cal BP) that falls within the disputed range above but is not itself the specific figure any one paper uses — flag for the authoring pass to verify which specific source underlies the existing 1501 BCE figure.

## 5. IF TIME PERMITTED: SUPPLEMENTARY TOPICS

### 5.1 Earliest pottery in Island Southeast Asia

**Dates:** per the most rigorous available Bayesian chronological synthesis (20 sites across six island regions), pottery is calculated to have entered the northern Philippines (Nagsabaran, Luzon) record at **5430–4290 cal BP (95.4%)**, and appears CONTEMPORANEOUSLY in western Borneo — the paper's central finding explicitly contradicts a simple "Out-of-Taiwan-first, then spreading south" sequence: "The results...do not conform to the expectations of the orthodox OoT or the Western Route Migration hypotheses and instead indicate contemporaneous pottery deposition in Borneo and the northern Philippines" ([PMC, "The first quantitative assessment of radiocarbon chronologies for..." 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8171956/)). Borneo (all sites): start 6680–3820 cal BP (95.4%); Sulawesi: 4550–3590 cal BP (95.4%).
- A separately dated site, Bukit Tengkorak (Sabah, Malaysian Borneo), yields pottery from a date "of 3300 B.C.E." (i.e., c. 5250 cal BP), described as "some of the earliest pottery in island Southeast Asia and the Pacific regions...presumably in the Austronesian tradition — pottery that substantially predates the Lapita cultural complex" ([EPrints USM PDF](http://eprints.usm.my/11542/1/The_Pottery_Technology_From_Bukit_Tengkorak_A_3000-5000_Year_Old_(Pamela_Vandiver_-_Stephen_Chia).pdf)).
- **Significance:** this multi-directional/contemporaneous pottery-dispersal pattern is one of the strongest archaeological arguments AGAINST a strict, single-source, staged Out-of-Taiwan sequence for ISEA (reinforcing the critique in §1.3.2) — pottery (and by extension, likely broader Neolithic cultural elements) appears to have moved through ISEA via multiple, roughly simultaneous routes rather than a single southward wave.
- **Sources:** [PMC 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8171956/); [EPrints USM](http://eprints.usm.my/11542/1/The_Pottery_Technology_From_Bukit_Tengkorak_A_3000-5000_Year_Old_(Pamela_Vandiver_-_Stephen_Chia).pdf).
- **Flags:** ⚑ this finding is a direct empirical challenge to the "textbook" staged Out-of-Taiwan sequence and should be read alongside §1.3.

### 5.2 The Toalean culture, Sulawesi, and the 2021 Leang Panninge genome

**Dates:** the Toalean technocomplex spans roughly **8,000–1,500 years ago** (a range repeated consistently across sources), with an internal three-phase sub-chronology per Bulbeck et al. 2000, cited identically across an ANU Press chapter and a PLOS ONE paper: **Early Toalean 7500–5500 BP** (bone points, microliths); **Late Preceramic Toalean 5500–3500 BP** (Maros points appear); **Ceramic Toalean 3500–2000 BP** (first Neolithic cultural traces) ([ANU Press, "The Archaeology of Sulawesi"](https://press-files.anu.edu.au/downloads/press/n4569/html/ch11.xhtml); [PLOS ONE 2021](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0251138); consistent with [Bulbeck 2004 PDF](https://oxis.org/downloads/bulbeck-david/bulbeck-2004a.pdf), which additionally notes pottery "probably arrived at around 4000 BP, and possibly as late as 3500 BP").
- **Leang Panninge genome ("Bessé'"):** a young female hunter-gatherer buried **7.3–7.2 kyr cal BP**, dated directly via ¹⁴C on a *Canarium* sp. seed from the burial context, associated with the Toalean technocomplex — the first genome ever recovered from a Toalean individual. Published in *Nature*, 2021 ([Nature, Carlhoff et al. 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03823-6); consistent PMC copy: [PMC 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8387238/)). Genetically, she shares most drift and morphological similarity with present-day Papuan and Indigenous Australian groups, but represents "a previously unknown divergent human lineage" that branched off "around the time of the split between these populations approximately 37,000 years ago," and carries an estimated **~2.2% Denisovan ancestry** ([Nature 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03823-6); Denisovan percentage per [Universitas Indonesia scholarhub paper](https://scholarhub.ui.ac.id/multikultura/vol5/iss3/2/)).
- **Significance:** this is the first and, as of this research pass, still the only published ancient genome from the Toalean culture — a distinctive, geographically restricted (c. 10,000 km² of South Sulawesi) hunter-gatherer technocomplex that persisted for millennia alongside, and eventually alongside/into contact with, Austronesian-speaking Neolithic farmers who spread into Wallacea from c. 4,000 years ago. It demonstrates a previously unsampled deep-branching human lineage in Wallacea, directly relevant to debates about the peopling of Sahul/Near Oceania.
- **Sources:** as cited inline above.
- **Flags:** ⚑ LITERATURE MOVED (2021) — the Leang Panninge genome is a major and recent (2021) addition to the human population history of Wallacea; treat any pre-2021 account of Toalean population affinities as incomplete.

### 5.3 Pleistocene/Holocene rock art traditions with secure dates

- Sulawesi rock art: ochre hand stencils, animal motifs, and a composite figurative hunting scene have been dated (via uranium-series dating of overlying speleothems, per the standard method used in this literature) to "at least 39.9–43.9 kya," per a source citing Aubert et al. 2014/2019 ([Walennae journal PDF](https://walennae.unhas.ac.id/index.php/walennae/article/download/427/360/1150)) — this pre-dates the dataset's 10,000 BCE window and is noted here only for completeness/context; black charcoal drawings in the same region are instead linked to "the period of Austronesian expansion into the area a few thousand years ago," i.e., within or near the dataset's window, though no more specific calibrated date was found in a fetched source for the charcoal drawings specifically — **mark as n.a. for a precise date on the charcoal-drawing tradition.**
- Torres Strait rock art: the Kabadul Kula site on Dauan Island shows people "living on Dauan for at least c. 1,350 years" based on associated occupation dating, doubling the previously known antiquity of human occupation on that island — this is a Torres Strait occupation date, not a rock-art-specific radiometric date, and falls after the dataset's window (post-1000 BCE); included for completeness ([ANU Open Research Repository](https://openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/f0edbf7b-7360-4eff-a4b3-65c39015d24a/content)).
- **Flags:** insufficient time/fetched-source coverage to responsibly populate this subsection further within the research pass; most rock art dating claims found in search snippets lacked a directly fetched primary source confirming the specific calibrated date — per the sourcing rules, these are left as n.a. rather than asserted from snippet text alone. A dedicated follow-up pass fetching Aubert et al. primary papers directly would be needed for a fuller rock-art entry.
