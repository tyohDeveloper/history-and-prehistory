# Song-Era States Research: Liao, Jin, Western Xia, and the Xia Dynasty

**Purpose:** This document supplies sourced data to fill four gaps in the history reference dataset, which currently contains the Song Dynasty split into Northern Song (960–1127) and Southern Song (1127–1279) but lacks the Liao, Jin, and Western Xia states, and omits the ancient Xia dynasty entirely (containing only the Erlitou archaeological culture). The 1127 Northern/Southern Song split is caused by the Jin dynasty's 1127 conquest of the Northern Song capital (the Jingkang Incident), documented in Section 2 below.

---

## 1. Liao Dynasty (Khitan)

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Chinese characters (traditional)** | 遼朝 (dynasty name); official name 大遼 "Great Liao"; the founding state was called 大契丹 "Great Khitan State" (Dà Qìdān) |
| **Chinese characters (simplified)** | 辽朝 |
| **Hanyu Pinyin** | *Liáo cháo* (dynasty); *Dà Liáo* (Great Liao); *Dà Qìdān* (Great Khitan State) |
| **Wade–Giles** | Liao (per Britannica) |
| **Ruling ethnic group** | The Khitan (Chinese: Qidan/契丹), described by Britannica as "nomadic Khitan tribes" and elsewhere as a Mongol/para-Mongolic people; ruling clan was the Yelü clan, with the Xiao clan as the imperial consort clan ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Liao-dynasty); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liao_dynasty)) |
| **Territory** | At its height: Manchuria, the Mongolian Plateau, the northern Korean Peninsula, southern Siberia, the Inner Mongolia region, and the northern tip of the North China Plain (including the "Sixteen Prefectures," present-day Beijing and part of Hebei, acquired 938). Estimated area ~2,600,000 km² (1,000,000 sq mi) in 947. Governed through a dual administrative system: a "southern government" for Chinese territories modeled on Tang administration, and a "northern government" ruling the steppe nomads on a tribal basis ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Liao-dynasty); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liao_dynasty)) |

### Founding date: 907 vs. 916 — documented disagreement

Sources genuinely disagree, and the disagreement is preserved here rather than resolved:

- **907 as the founding year:** Encyclopaedia Britannica states "Traditionally, the start of the Liao period is given as 907, the last year of the Tang" — this is when Khitan chieftain **Yelü Abaoji (Apaoki)** became khagan and supreme leader of the Khitans, ending nine generations of Yaolian rule, founding the "Great Khitan State" ([Britannica, "Liao dynasty"](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Liao-dynasty); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liao_dynasty); [Nouahsark](https://www.nouahsark.com/en/infocenter/culture/history/dynasty/liao_dynasty.php)).
- **916 as the founding year:** Britannica also notes "Chinese historians often place the beginning of the Liao period at 916, when Yelü Yi (or Abaoji) formally established himself as emperor" ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Liao-dynasty)). Wikipedia's infobox uses 916–1125 as the dynasty's dates, describing 916 as when Abaoji "assumed the title of Emperor," adopted a Chinese-style imperial title (*huangdi*) in place of *khagan*, proclaimed a Chinese era name, and built a Confucian temple ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liao_dynasty)). A Shandong University research paper on Liao chronology confirms that "today's scholarship on the Liao dynasty tends to fundamentally agree that Yelü Abaoji... officially proclaimed the founding of the dynasty with himself as emperor in the first year of Shence in 916," while noting the *Liaoshi* official history nonetheless dates the founding to 907 — a discrepancy the paper argues was deliberate rather than a historian's error ([Shandong University / lhp.sdu.edu.cn PDF](https://www.lhp.sdu.edu.cn/__local/C/16/CF/EADF85A008182C64BAE7A905CC6_F35AACA5_2BB5F.pdf)).
- **Berkshire Encyclopedia of China** similarly frames it as two counting conventions: "The Liao dynasty was founded in 907... Many historians have counted the founding by Yelü Abaoji of the Khitan Empire... in 916 as the start of this dynasty" ([Berkshire Publishing](https://www.berkshirepublishing.com/ecph-china/2018/01/08/liao-dynasty-907-1125/)).

**Recommendation for the dataset:** Record both dates with attribution — 907 (Britannica's "traditional" date; khaganate founding) and 916 (Chinese-historian convention; formal imperial declaration) — rather than picking one.

### Ending

- **End year: 1125.** Britannica states plainly: "The Liao dynasty was destroyed in 1125 by the Juchen [Jurchen] tribes," former Khitan subjects who rebelled "with the aid of the Song" ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Liao-dynasty)).
- **Ending event:** The decisive event was the capture of the last Liao emperor, **Emperor Tianzuo**, by Jin forces in early 1125, after a prolonged Jurchen military campaign that took the Liao supreme capital (1120) and central and western capitals (1122). Tianzuo fled westward, sought refuge with Western Xia's Emperor Chongzong in 1124 (who then submitted to Jin instead), and was finally captured by the Jin in early 1125, ending Liao rule; he died in Jin custody in 1128 according to the *History of Liao* ([Wikipedia, "Liao dynasty"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liao_dynasty)).

### Western Liao / Qara Khitai (1124–1218) — successor state

- Following the Liao collapse, a Khitan noble, **Yelü Dashi**, led loyalist remnants westward and founded the **Western Liao**, also called **Qara Khitai** (Chinese: 西遼, *Xī Liáo*), establishing himself as *gurkhan* ("universal khan") in Central Asia, generally dated **1124–1218** ([Oxford Research Encyclopedia of Asian History, Michal Biran](http://mongol.huji.ac.il/sites/default/files/Biran%202020%20Qara%20Khitai%20Oxford%20Research%20Encyclopedia%20of%20Asian%20History.pdf); [Wikipedia, "Qara Khitai"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qara_Khitai)).
- **Does it merit separate treatment?** Yes. Scholarly consensus treats it as a distinct successor state rather than a mere continuation: the Oxford Research Encyclopedia of Asian History describes it as "the only dynasty that did not [rule from within China proper]," ruling Central Asia for nearly 90 years, with governmental and cultural practices adapted to a mostly Muslim Central Asian population, distinct from the Liao's East Asian base ([Biran, Oxford Research Encyclopedia](http://mongol.huji.ac.il/sites/default/files/Biran%202020%20Qara%20Khitai%20Oxford%20Research%20Encyclopedia%20of%20Asian%20History.pdf)).
- **End date complication:** Sources distinguish two possible "end" dates. The empire was **usurped by the Naiman prince Kuchlug in 1211**, and "traditional Chinese, Persian, and Arab sources generally treat this usurpation as the end of the Khitan dynasty," but the Qara Khitai state itself was not destroyed until the **Mongol conquest in 1218** ([Wikipedia, "Qara Khitai"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qara_Khitai); [New World Encyclopedia](https://www.newworldencyclopedia.org/entry/Kara-Khitan_Khanate)). Recommend recording 1124–1218 as the state's span, with 1211 flagged as the "dynastic" end per traditional historiography.

## 2. Jin Dynasty (Jurchen)

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Chinese characters (traditional/simplified, same)** | 金朝 (dynasty name, "Jincháo"); official name 大金 (*Dà Jīn*, "Great Jin") |
| **Hanyu Pinyin** | *Jīn cháo*; official name *Dà Jīn* |
| **Wade–Giles** | *Chin¹ Ch'ao²* (dynasty); *Ta⁴ Chin¹* (Great Jin); Britannica separately gives "Chin" as the Wade–Giles form and "Nü-chen" or "Ju-chen" as Wade–Giles for the Jurchen ethnonym ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jin_dynasty_(1115%E2%80%931234)); [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Jin-dynasty-China-Mongolia-1115-1234)) |
| **Ruling ethnic group** | The Jurchen (Tungusic people of Manchuria, also called Juchen/Nüzhen/Ruzhen), ruled by the **Wanyan clan** (完顏/完颜, *Wányán*) ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Jin-dynasty-China-Mongolia-1115-1234); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jin_dynasty_(1115%E2%80%931234))) |
| **Territory** | At its height (1186): ~4,750,000 km² (1,830,000 sq mi), spanning Outer Manchuria in the north to the Qinling–Huaihe line in the south, covering all of present-day North China plus parts of Inner Asia; capitals moved from Huiningfu (near Harbin) to Zhongdu (Beijing, from 1153) to Kaifeng (from 1214) to Caizhou (1233–1234) as territory was progressively lost to the Mongols ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jin_dynasty_(1115%E2%80%931234)); [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Jin-dynasty-China-Mongolia-1115-1234)) |

### Founding

- **Founding year: 1115**, specifically **28 January 1115**, per Wikipedia's infobox and chronology. The founder, **Wanyan Aguda** (阿骨打; posthumously Emperor Taizu of Jin), was elected leader of the Jurchens in 1113 after rebelling against Liao mistreatment (including the notorious 1112 incident in which Aguda refused to perform a ceremonial dance for the Liao emperor Tianzuo), then declared himself emperor of the "Jin" ("Gold") dynasty in spring 1115 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jin_dynasty_(1115%E2%80%931234))).
- Britannica is slightly less precise, stating only that "the Juchen... established their own dynasty **between 1115 and 1122**" without committing to a single founding year ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Jin-dynasty-China-Mongolia-1115-1234)). The 1115–1234 span is nonetheless the standard designation used by both sources.

### Ending

- **End year: 1234**, specifically **9 February 1234**, with the fall of the last Jin capital, **Caizhou**, to a combined Mongol–Southern Song army. The last effective emperor, **Aizong**, abdicated to Wanyan Chenglin and then hanged himself rather than be captured; Chenglin (posthumously Emperor Mo) died in street fighting hours later, ending the dynasty ([Wikipedia, "Jin dynasty (1115–1234)"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jin_dynasty_(1115%E2%80%931234)); [Wikipedia, "Mongol conquest of the Jin dynasty"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_conquest_of_the_Jin_dynasty)). Britannica corroborates: "the dynasty was finally destroyed in 1234, when it was caught in the middle of a newly concluded alliance between the Mongols on the north and the Song on the south" ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Jin-dynasty-China-Mongolia-1115-1234)).

### The Jingkang Incident (1127) — the event that created the Southern Song

This is the critical missing link explaining the dataset's 1127 Northern/Southern Song split.

- **Chinese name:** 靖康事變/靖康事变 (*Jìngkāng Shìbiàn*), also known as the "Humiliation of Jingkang" (靖康之恥/靖康之耻, *Jìngkāng Zhīchǐ*) or "Disorders of the Jingkang Period" (靖康之亂/靖康之乱, *Jìngkāng Zhīluàn*) ([Wikipedia, "Jingkang incident"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jingkang_incident)).
- **What happened:** During the Jin–Song wars, Jin forces besieged and, on **9 January 1127**, sacked **Bianjing** (present-day Kaifeng, Henan), the capital of the Northern Song. Jin troops captured the reigning **Emperor Qinzong** and his father, the retired **Emperor Huizong**, along with a large part of the imperial family, nobles, and court officials. On 20 March 1127 the two emperors were formally stripped of imperial status and demoted to commoners; more than 14,000 captives, including much of the imperial family, were marched north into Jin territory ([Wikipedia, "Jingkang incident"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jingkang_incident); [Britannica, "Jingkang Incident (1126–27)"](https://www.britannica.com/event/Jingkang-Incident-1126-1127)).
- **Consequence:** The fall of Kaifeng and capture of both emperors is treated by all consulted sources as marking the end of the Northern Song. A surviving prince, **Zhao Gou** (Qinzong's ninth brother), escaped south and was proclaimed **Emperor Gaozong**, reestablishing the dynasty as the **Southern Song** with a new capital at **Lin'an** (present-day Hangzhou) — directly justifying the dataset's existing 1127 Northern/Southern Song boundary ([Wikipedia, "Jingkang incident"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jingkang_incident); [History Atlas](https://www.historyatlas.com/topics/jingkang-incident/)).
- Britannica separately confirms the same event and date range under the heading "Jingkang Incident (1126–27)": "In January 1127 Juchen steppe nomads captured the Chinese capital of Kaifeng and with it the Song emperor... Song Emperor Qinzong was carried off into the Juchen heartland" ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/Jingkang-Incident-1126-1127)).

## 3. Western Xia / Xi Xia (Tangut)

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Chinese characters** | 西夏 (Western Xia, both traditional and simplified); 大夏 (*Dà Xià*, "Great Xia," the state's Chinese short name); official state name 白高大夏國/白高大夏国 ("Kingdom of the Great Xia of the White and Lofty") |
| **Hanyu Pinyin** | *Xī Xià* (Western Xia); *Dà Xià* (Great Xia) |
| **Wade–Giles** | *Hsi¹ Hsia⁴* (Western Xia); *Ta⁴ Hsia⁴* (Great Xia) ([Wikipedia, "Western Xia"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Xia)) |
| **Ruling ethnic group** | The **Tanguts** (Chinese exonym: Dangxiang/党項), a Tibeto-Burman-speaking people originally from the Qinghai–Sichuan–Tibet border region; the state also employed Han, Tibetan, and Uyghur officials ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Xia); [Britannica, "Xi Xia"](https://www.britannica.com/place/Xi-Xia)) |
| **Territory** | At its height (~1100): roughly 1,000,000 km² (390,000 sq mi), centered on the Hexi Corridor (a key Silk Road route), covering parts of modern Ningxia, Gansu, eastern Qinghai, northern Shaanxi, northeastern Xinjiang, southwestern Inner Mongolia, and southernmost Outer Mongolia. Capital: Xingqing/Zhongxing, modern Yinchuan ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Xia); [Wikipedia, "Mongol conquest of Western Xia"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_conquest_of_Western_Xia)) |

### Founding

- **Founding year: 1038.** Britannica's biography of the founder states: "Li founded the Xia (or Daxia) dynasty (1038–1227)... in 1038 Li ended his people's tribute shipments to the Song (960–1279) rulers and proclaimed himself emperor of the Xia" ([Britannica, "Li Yuanhao"](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Li-Yuanhao)). Britannica's separate "Xi Xia" entry corroborates: "kingdom... established in 1038" ([Britannica, "Xi Xia"](https://www.britannica.com/place/Xi-Xia)).
- **Founder and event:** **Li Yuanhao** (also Weiming Yuanhao; posthumously Emperor Jingzong of Western Xia), leader of the Tangut Dingnan Jiedushi regime, declared himself emperor of "Da Xia" (Great Xia) on the 11th day of the 10th month of Song Baoyuan year 1 (1038 CE) ([Brill, Shi Jinbo and Li Hansong](https://brill.com/downloadpdf/display/book/9789004461321/BP000009.pdf); [Wikipedia, "Emperor Jingzong of Western Xia"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_Jingzong_of_Western_Xia)).
- **Was there an earlier founding?** Yes — sources distinguish the **1038 imperial proclamation** from an earlier **1032/982 Tangut proto-state**. Several sources trace Tangut autonomy back to **982**, when Li Jiqian rebelled against Song administration of the Dingnan Jiedushi and began building an independent Tangut polity; his grandson Li Yuanhao inherited and then formalized this into an empire in 1038 ([En Academic / Academic Dictionaries](https://en-academic.com/dic.nsf/enwiki/36986); [chinaknowledge.de](http://www.chinaknowledge.de/History/Song/xixia.html); [Wikipedia, "Western Xia"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Xia)). No source disputes 1038 as the year of the *imperial* founding; the disagreement (where it exists) concerns only when Tangut autonomy under the Li/Weiming family began, not when the "Xia"/"Western Xia" empire itself was proclaimed.
- **Tangut script:** Yuanhao (during his father Deming's reign, or shortly after his own accession) ordered creation of a dedicated **Tangut script**, attributed to Yeli Renrong, disseminated for government use by 1036 and used to translate Chinese and Tibetan classics; the script died out after the state's fall and was largely undeciphered until 20th-century scholarship ([Wikipedia, "Western Xia"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Xia)).

### Ending

- **End year: 1227.** The Western Xia was destroyed by the Mongol Empire under Genghis Khan after a long series of campaigns (1205–1210, then 1225–1227). In the final campaign, the Mongols besieged the capital **Zhongxing/Yinchuan**; the siege lasted about six months. **Genghis Khan died during the siege**, in August 1227 (his death was kept secret to avoid jeopardizing the campaign); the Tangut ruler **Emperor Mo (Li Xian)** surrendered in September 1227 and was promptly executed, after which the Mongols pillaged the capital, killed much of the population, and plundered the imperial tombs, completing the state's destruction ([Wikipedia, "Mongol conquest of Western Xia"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_conquest_of_Western_Xia); [Britannica, "Genghis Khan"](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Genghis-Khan)).

## 4. Xia Dynasty (traditional first dynasty, c. 2070–1600 BCE) — contested historicity

**This is a different entity from Western Xia (Section 3) and must not be conflated with it.** The dataset currently contains only the Erlitou archaeological culture; this section documents the historiographical dispute over whether a "Xia dynasty" existed as history and whether Erlitou represents it.

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Chinese characters** | 夏 (Xia); 夏朝 (Xia dynasty, *Xiàcháo*) |
| **Hanyu Pinyin** | *Xià*; *Xiàcháo* |
| **Wade–Giles** | *Hsia⁴* (Xia); *Hsia Ch'ao* (Xia dynasty) ([Wikipedia, "Xia dynasty"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xia_dynasty)) |
| **Traditional dates** | Various: c. 2205–1766 BCE (traditional chronology per Liu Xin); c. 1989–1558 BCE (per the "current text" *Bamboo Annals*); c. 1953–1555 BCE (David Pankenier, supported by David Nivison); **c. 2070–1600 BCE** (Xia–Shang–Zhou Chronology Project figure, now the most commonly cited) ([Wikipedia, "Xia dynasty"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xia_dynasty); [Britannica, "Xia dynasty"](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Xia-dynasty)) |

### Current scholarly status: a genuine, unresolved dispute

There is **no consensus verdict**, and the dispute runs largely (though not exclusively) along a China/West line, as follows.

**Position A — the Xia is a legendary/unproven construct, and Erlitou cannot be confirmed as "Xia":**
- Encyclopaedia Britannica frames the Xia cautiously as "early Chinese dynasty **mentioned in legends**" with a founder (Yu the Great) credited with mythic flood-control feats, and 16 rulers ending with the stock tyrant-figure Jie — a narrative pattern common to Chinese dynastic-founding myths ([Britannica, "Xia dynasty"](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Xia-dynasty)).
- A Britannica video summary states plainly: "Since no historical accounts exist, many scholars consider the Xia dynasty to be quasi-legendary" ([Britannica video](https://www.britannica.com/video/learn-about-Chinas-six-ruling-dynasties/-259717)).
- The **Cambridge History of Ancient China** (1999, eds. Michael Loewe and Edward L. Shaughnessy) begins its narrative of "historical" China with the **Shang** dynasty (dated there to c. 1570/1600–1045 BCE), implicitly declining to treat the Xia as established history ([Wikipedia summary of the volume's approach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xia_dynasty); [Cambridge University Press listing](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-history-of-ancient-china/1274DEAA9CE3A6D0AC700076B70D5C22)).
- A widely cited open-access academic volume, *Ancient China: A History*, states: "The first of the 'Three Dynasties,' the Xia, is even more problematical, because there is no inscriptional or paleographic evidence that attests to its existence... there are no dateable written references to the Xia dynasty earlier than the fourth century BCE... For now, many scholars remain cautiously agnostic on the historicity of the Xia, and we will refer to the period from about 2100 to 1600 BCE as the Early Bronze Age or the Erlitou Period rather than the Xia dynasty" ([OAPEN open-access PDF](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/id/60fc5c7c-2525-4559-b6e0-f9780038aa46/9781317503668.pdf)).
- Early-20th-century Chinese historian **Gu Jiegang**, leader of the "Doubting Antiquity School" (疑古派), argued that later periods progressively expanded and embellished a legendary early Chinese past ([Wikipedia, "Xia dynasty"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xia_dynasty)).
- Sinologist **Sarah Allan** has argued the Xia may be better understood as a mythological "opposite" of the Shang (associated with moons, the watery underworld, dragons, west, and death, versus the Shang's suns, sky, birds, east, and life) rather than a straightforwardly historical dynasty, and that the Zhou reinterpreted this myth as a real predecessor dynasty to legitimize their own conquest of the Shang via the Mandate of Heaven doctrine ([Wikipedia, "Xia dynasty," citing Allan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xia_dynasty)).
- Critically: **no oracle bone inscriptions from the Late Shang period (13th century BCE) mention the Xia at all**, and the earliest surviving textual references occur in early Western Zhou-era chapters of the *Book of Documents* — centuries after the Xia's supposed fall — which several scholars read as evidence the Xia narrative was a later Zhou political construction ([Wikipedia, "Xia dynasty"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xia_dynasty); [sociostudies.org PDF, "Erlitou and Xia: A Dispute between Chinese and Overseas..."](https://www.sociostudies.org/upload/sociostudies.org/journal/seh/2018_2/235-257.pdf)).
- On the archaeology specifically: Wikipedia's Erlitou-culture article states "there is no firm evidence, such as surviving, contemporary written records, to substantiate such a linkage [of Erlitou to Xia], as the earliest evidence of Chinese writing dates to the Late Shang period," and that "other scholars, particularly outside China, point to the lack of any firm evidence for such an identification, and argue that the historiographical focus of Chinese archaeology is unduly limiting" ([Wikipedia, "Erlitou culture"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erlitou_culture)).

**Position B — the Xia was a real dynasty, generally identified (by most Chinese archaeologists) with the Erlitou culture:**
- China's state-sponsored **Xia–Shang–Zhou Chronology Project** (夏商周斷代工程), commissioned by the Chinese government in 1996, concluded the Xia existed **c. 2070–1600 BCE**, assigned all four phases of the Erlitou culture to the Xia, and identified the Xia-to-Shang transition with the construction of walled cities at Yanshi and Zhengzhou around 1600 BCE ([Wikipedia, "Xia–Shang–Zhou Chronology Project"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xia%E2%80%93Shang%E2%80%93Zhou_Chronology_Project); [Wikipedia, "Xia dynasty"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xia_dynasty)).
- The Erlitou Site Museum of the Xia Capital (Luoyang, Henan), which opened in 2019, is named on the premise that Erlitou was the Xia capital, "represent[ing] a mainstream view," per Chen Xingcan, director of the Institute of Archaeology at the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences — though Chen himself cautioned that this remained "highly possible" rather than certain, saying the identification was "99 percent" rather than 100 percent proven and that "the question is still open-ended" ([China Daily, "Recasting the past"](http://www.chinadaily.com.cn/global/2019-10/29/content_37519145.htm)).
- Archaeologist **Zou Heng** first proposed in 1978 that Erlitou (not the previously assumed identification with the early Shang capital "Bo") was the Xia capital, sparking the ongoing Chinese academic debate over exactly which of Erlitou's four occupation phases belong to Xia versus Shang ([Wikipedia, "Erlitou"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erlitou)).
- Some Chinese scholars have proposed corroborating textual evidence: **Guo Moruo** suggested the "Tufang" state named in Shang oracle-bone inscriptions might be the Xia; **Shen Changyun** pointed to Shang-era inscriptions mentioning "Qi," matching the name of the state traditionally granted to Xia remnants after the Shang conquest; and proponents cite a bronze vessel (*Shu Yi Zhong*, unearthed in the Song dynasty) whose Spring-and-Autumn-period inscription references Tang of Shang's defeat of Xia's King Jie, which some scholars argue would be odd for a "later Zhou fabrication" to have been independently referenced by a descendant of Shang nobility ([Wikipedia, "Xia dynasty"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xia_dynasty)).
- A Shandong University-hosted academic paper states "It is now generally accepted among academics that the Erlitou culture, or most of it, is Xia culture," while separately acknowledging "a small minority of Chinese and foreign scholars continues to doubt the historical existence of the Xia dynasty" — illustrating that even pro-historicity sources acknowledge unresolved dissent ([Shandong University PDF](https://www.lhp.sdu.edu.cn/__local/6/B8/8C/EA4B173B5B4FB0019680B120F8F_E048963D_15A2C9.pdf)).

**On the Chronology Project itself — documented Western academic criticism:**
- Wikipedia's dedicated article states: "some scholars have disputed several of the project's methods and conclusions," including complaints that the project's approach "forces archeological evidence into a framework of a single sequence of similar dominant states" and criticism voiced at a 2002 Association for Asian Studies conference session, where the project's methods were criticized by sinologist **David Nivison**, among others ([Wikipedia, "Xia–Shang–Zhou Chronology Project"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xia%E2%80%93Shang%E2%80%93Zhou_Chronology_Project)).
- A Cambridge University Press *Early China* journal article on the project's Western Zhou chronology work concludes bluntly: "the chronology produced by the Project... not only cannot be replicated, but in fact... is wrong from the beginning of the period to its end," while adding "this is not to say that all of the contributions of the Project should be repudiated" ([Cambridge Core, *Early China*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/early-china/article/xia-shang-zhou-duandai-gongcheng-baogao-and-its-chronology-of-the-western-zhou-dynasty/78B7D45FE8B93DD70AB440B44A5FF2CA)).
- A CUHK (Chinese University of Hong Kong) review article notes that new bronze inscription evidence "left no doubt that the Chronology Project's dates for the reigns of Kings Li and Xuan were fundamentally flawed" ([CUHK review PDF](https://www.cuhk.edu.hk/ics/journal/articles/v52p269.pdf)).
- An OAPEN open-access academic monograph states the project's "chronology therefore is far too doubtful to justify the claims being made for it" and notes "the Project merely offers approximate dates for the beginning and end of Xia, without dates of reigns" ([OAPEN PDF](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/id/000865f2-5ec0-4f17-982f-7b35ad628bf6/9781501505393.pdf)).
- Beyond methodology, critics (per the Wikipedia summary) allege the project was motivated in part by nationalism, reflecting "a tradition of relegating archaeology to a role of verifying traditional histories" rather than treating archaeological evidence independently ([Wikipedia, "Xia–Shang–Zhou Chronology Project"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xia%E2%80%93Shang%E2%80%93Zhou_Chronology_Project); a Chinese state-media-affiliated commentary acknowledges "many scholars have legitimately critiqued its methodology, evidentiary standards" while cautioning against conflating fair critique with "culturally tinted derision" — [Chinese Social Sciences Net (CSSN), English edition](http://english.cssn.cn/skw_research/history/202506/t20250610_5878478.shtml)).
- A peer-reviewed comparative study frames this explicitly as a China/West divide in method and inference standards, without declaring a winner: "This research should not only hypothesize the existence of the Xia, but has to prove it... there are suspicio[ns] that the Xia is a fabrication by later generations" while also surveying the arguments on both sides ([Sociological Studies journal PDF, "Erlitou and Xia: A Dispute between Chinese and Overseas..."](https://www.sociostudies.org/upload/sociostudies.org/journal/seh/2018_2/235-257.pdf)).

**Museum framing (for reference, not verdict):** The Metropolitan Museum of Art's Bronze Age China materials describe the Xia, Shang, and Zhou together as "China's first three dynasties" in the context of ritual bronze vessels and legend, without taking an explicit position on Xia's independent historicity beyond noting the traditional framework ([Met Museum, "The Great Bronze Age of China"](https://www.metmuseum.org/met-publications/the-great-bronze-age-of-china-an-exhibition-from-the-peoples-republic-of-china)).

### Summary for the dataset

The current scholarly position is a **live, unresolved dispute**, not settled fact in either direction:
- **Majority of Chinese archaeologists/historians:** the Xia was a real dynasty, most likely represented archaeologically by some or all phases of the Erlitou culture (c. 1900–1500 BCE by radiocarbon dating, independent of the Chronology Project's rounder 2070–1600 BCE figure).
- **A significant body of Western Sinologists, and a minority within China:** the Erlitou–Xia identification, and arguably the historicity of "Xia" as a specific ruling dynasty at all, cannot be proven from currently available evidence, since no contemporaneous written record naming a "Xia" state has ever been found, and the earliest textual mentions of Xia postdate its supposed existence by centuries.
- The **Xia–Shang–Zhou Chronology Project** (1996–2000, PRC government-commissioned) is the source of the commonly cited "2070–1600 BCE" date range and the identification of all four Erlitou phases with Xia; it has drawn sustained methodological criticism from scholars (including at Cambridge, per the *Early China* journal) for chronology-building techniques, evidentiary overreach, and an alleged tendency to use archaeology to confirm pre-existing traditional narratives rather than test them independently.
- **Recommendation:** the dataset entry for "Xia dynasty" should present it as a *traditional/legendary-to-historical dynasty of disputed status* (c. 2070–1600 BCE per the state-sponsored chronology; c. 2205–1766 BCE per older traditional chronology), explicitly distinct from the archaeological Erlitou culture entry, with the relationship between the two flagged as contested rather than confirmed.
