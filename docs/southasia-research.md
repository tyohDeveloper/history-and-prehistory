# South Asian Prehistory, c. 8000–1000 BCE — Sourced Research Notes
### Indus Valley Civilisation and related entities (supplementing existing dataset)

**Scope note:** This file supplements entries already in the dataset (Lahuradewa, Mehrgarh, Bhirrana, Ravi phase, South Asian Chalcolithic, Vedic period — not re-researched here). ## 1. INDUS PHASES (Early / Mature / Late Harappan)

### 1.1 The textbook tripartite scheme
**Dates (secondary/textbook consensus, NOT a single primary citation):** Early Harappan c. 3300–2600 BCE; Mature Harappan c. 2600–1900 BCE; Late Harappan c. 1900–1300 BCE.
**Status:** This exact scheme is repeated across dozens of secondary/textbook sources. It is NOT independently confirmed here against one primary radiocarbon publication — treat as a convenient shorthand, not as an authoritative dating.
**Sources:** Wikipedia (locator only, not used for dates); widely repeated in course materials.
**Flags:** Use the site-specific primary sources below (1.2–1.5) for any date that must be defensible.

### 1.2 Harappa.com site-based chronology (Kenoyer/Meadow excavations, 1986–1996; scholar-run, non-Wikipedia)
**Dates (calibrated cal BC, from >70 new radiocarbon dates at Harappa):**
- Ravi/Hakra phase: c. 3300–2800 BC
- Kot Dijian (Early Harappan): c. 2800–2600 BC
- Harappan Phase (Mature): c. 2600–1900 BC, subdivided 3A (2600–2450 BC), 3B (2450–2200 BC), 3C (2200–1900 BC)
- Transitional: 1900–1800 BC
- Late Harappan: 1800–1300 BC

Direct quote: "In general the formation of large urban centers such as Mohenjo-daro and Harappa … can be dated from around 2600 to 1900 B.C."
**Significance:** This is the best site-anchored radiocarbon chronology for Harappa itself, and the source of the widely-copied tripartite scheme's actual dates.
**Sources:** [Harappa.com, Test Trench Chronology](https://www.harappa.com/beas/test-trench-chronology); [Harappa.com, "What has been the basis for determining the age of the Indus Valley Civilization?"](https://www.harappa.com/answers/what-has-been-basis-determining-age-indus-valley-civilization-archeologists)
**Flags:** None — internally consistent primary excavation data.

### 1.3 Kenoyer 2008 (Sackler Colloquium) — broader "Indus Tradition" eras
**Dates:** Foraging 10,000–2000 BCE (overlapping, regional survival); Early Food Producing 7000–5500 BCE (Mehrgarh horizon); Regionalization 5500–2600 BCE (= Early Harappan); Integration (Harappan/Mature) 2600–1900 BCE; Localization (Late Harappan) 1900–1300 BCE.
Direct quote: "Initial urban development in the Indus region began between approximately 2800 and 2600 BC during Early Harappan Kot Diji phase."
**Significance:** Frames Harappan urbanism as the culmination of a much longer regional trajectory rather than a sudden event.
**Sources:** [Kenoyer 2008, Sackler Colloquium PDF via Harappa.com](https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/Kenoyer2008%20Indus%20Cities%20Sackler%20Colloquiumfinalrevised.pdf)
**Flags:** None.

### 1.4 Cambridge (Bates et al.) supplementary chronology
**Dates:** Early village/early farming 6300–3200 BC; Early Harappan (pre-urban) 3200–2600 BC; Mature Harappan (urban) 2600–1900 BC; Late Harappan (post-urban) 1900–1300 BC.
**Sources:** [Cambridge supplementary material, static.cambridge.org](https://static.cambridge.org/content/id/urn:cambridge.org:id:article:S0003598X16002106/resource/name/S0003598X16002106sup001.pdf)
**Flags:** Broadly matches Harappa.com scheme but pushes Early Harappan start ~100 years later (3200 vs. 3300 BC) — minor discrepancy, not resolved here.

### 1.5 Contested duration: the "shorter chronology" argument
**Claim:** A Science paper argues radiocarbon evidence supports a substantially SHORTER total span for the Harappa culture — c. 2300–1750 BC (550 years) — versus the standard ~700-year Mature Harappan span (2600–1900 BCE).
**Sources:** [Science, doi:10.1126/science.143.3609.950](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.143.3609.950); [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17743929/)
**Flags:** ⚠ GENUINE UNRESOLVED DISAGREEMENT on span length, not just endpoints. Present both the standard (2600–1900 BCE) and shorter (2300–1750 BC) chronologies; do not average them.

### 1.6 ⚑ LITERATURE MOVED (2026): Mohenjo-daro pushed ~100–200 years earlier
**New dates (cal BCE, radiocarbon on charcoal from mud-brick cores and excavated contexts, dated in US laboratories):**
- Earliest perimeter-wall construction: **2708–2576 cal BCE**
- Subsequent street levels: **2460–2280 cal BCE**
- Narrative synthesis (Dawn): earliest wall built "around 2700–2600 BCE, approximately 100 years before the beginning of the Harappan Phase" (i.e., during the Early Harappan/Kot Diji phase, not the Mature phase); later phases of the wall built "beginning around 2600 BCE" (Harappan/Mature Phase); walls "expanded and maintained until around 2200 BCE and possibly even longer." Broader statement: "recent excavations and new radiocarbon dates confirm an earlier urban occupation at Mohenjo-daro during the Kot Diji Phase, circa 3300–2600 BC."
- For comparison, Harappa's own Early Harappan/Kot Dijian wall and occupation dates to c. 2800–2600 BCE (consistent with §1.2 above).

**Who/when:** Findings from the 2025–2026 Documentation & Conservation Programme at Mohenjo-daro, a joint mission of the Directorate General of Archaeology & Antiquities (Sindh) and the Sindh Exploration & Archaeological Society (SEAS Pakistan), with Dr. Asma Ibrahim, Dr. Jonathan Mark Kenoyer (University of Wisconsin–Madison), and colleagues; fieldwork in summer 2025 and winter 2025–26; radiocarbon dating performed in the US (no facility available in Pakistan); findings disclosed to *Dawn* (published 25 March 2026) and presented at a technical briefing reported by *The News* (30 July 2026). The wall itself was first found by Sir Mortimer Wheeler in 1950, who misidentified it as a flood-protection bund rather than a defensive city wall.
**Significance:** Reassigns the mud-brick structure Wheeler found in 1950 from a "flood bund" to a genuine defensive perimeter city wall, and shows Mohenjo-daro's urban infrastructure began during the Early Harappan/Kot Diji phase — roughly a century before the conventional "Mature Harappan" starting point of 2600 BCE, not after it as most textbooks imply.
**Sources:** [Dawn, "Fresh studies trace Mohenjo Daro's urban roots to 3300 BC," 25 March 2026](https://www.dawn.com/news/1985048); [The News (Pakistan), "Call for more research to unlock Indus Valley civilisation's secrets," 30 July 2026](https://www.thenews.pk/print/1429094-call-for-more-research-to-unlock-indus-valley-civilisation-s-secrets); [Archaeology.org summary](https://archaeology.org/news/2026/04/06/new-dates-push-back-occupation-of-mohenjo-daro/); [New Indian Express](https://www.newindianexpress.com/explainers/2026/Apr/25/mohenjo-daros-urbanisation-began-earlier-than-thought)
**Flags:** ⚑ LITERATURE MOVED (2026) — this is a genuine, very recent shift, not yet reflected in most secondary literature or textbooks. Neither source article gives the individual lab measurement/calibration details (sample IDs, ±error, calibration curve) for all five new dates — only the two ranges quoted above and one narrative range are available; treat any more granular figure as `n.a.` until a peer-reviewed paper is published.

### 1.7 Site-specific revisions from calibration (Kalibangan example)
**Claim:** Kalibangan's chronology was substantially revised after "MASCA calibration" correction of raw C-14 dates: earlier framework was pre-Harappan 2400–2250 BC / Mature 2200–1700 BC; revised framework is pre-Harappan settlement c. 2920–2550 BC (uncalibrated C14), a cluster of 6 dates 2550–2440 BC marking the start of Mature Harappan, and Mature Harappan spanning 2500–2000 BC.
**Sources:** [GKToday, Kalibangan](https://www.gktoday.in/kalibangan/); [Lucknow University PDF on Kalibangan](https://www.lkouniv.ac.in/site/writereaddata/siteContent/202003281454237730durgesh_Kalibangan.pdf)
**Significance:** A clean, explicit worked example of how calibration methodology alone can shift a site's dating by centuries — worth flagging generally whenever comparing older vs. newer Indus dates.
**Flags:** Note explicitly whether any given published date is calibrated or uncalibrated before using it.

## 2. INDUS SITES

### 2.1 Mohenjo-daro (Sindh, Pakistan)
**Dates:** See §1.6 for the 2026 revision. Earlier framework (Dales 1973, via secondary source): earliest occupation buried below the water table, estimated ~3500 BCE (Kot Diji phase, unconfirmed method); only 2 processed radiocarbon dates from mixed later contexts placed final occupation in the HR excavation area at 2200–1900 BCE; Late Harappan occupation elsewhere at the site may continue to 1700 or 1300 BCE (imprecise, mixed contexts).
**Significance:** Largest and most famous Mature Harappan city; site of the Great Bath, granary, and citadel mound; now understood (2026) to have Early Harappan urban origins predating the conventional Mature Harappan start.
**Sources:** [Studylib summary of Dales 1973 excavation data](https://studylib.net/doc/7379390/mohenjo-daro-article) — secondary, treat the "3500 BCE" figure as `n.a.`-grade (unconfirmed method/precision); see §1.6 for the confirmed 2026 primary figures.
**Flags:** See ⚑ in §1.6.

### 2.2 Harappa (Punjab, Pakistan)
**Dates:** See §1.2 for the full site sequence (Ravi/Hakra 3300–2800 BC through Late Harappan/Cemetery H 1800–1300 BC).
**Significance:** Type-site of the civilisation; longest continuously-excavated sequence (1986–1996 Kenoyer/Meadow project), providing the best-anchored radiocarbon framework used across the field.
**Sources:** [Harappa.com Test Trench Chronology](https://www.harappa.com/beas/test-trench-chronology)
**Flags:** A commonly cited Wikipedia figure (earliest radiocarbon date ~2725±185 BC uncalibrated, calibrated midpoint ~3251 BC) is locator-only and NOT independently confirmed here against a non-Wikipedia source; do not use that specific figure. Use the Harappa.com calibrated transition dates instead (~2870 BC at the Ravi/Hakra–Early Harappan transition; ~2445 BC and ~2280 BC at the Early-to-Mature Harappan transition), from the same Test Trench Chronology source above.

### 2.3 Dholavira (Great Rann of Kachchh, Gujarat, India)
**Dates — two independent sources, presented separately (do not average):**

*Sengupta et al. 2020, Journal of Quaternary Science* (radiocarbon dating of archaeological carbonates across 7 cultural stages): occupation began ~5500 years BP (pre-Harappan) and continued to ~3800 years BP (early Late Harappan); final collapse ~4300–4100 years BP, coinciding with the onset of the Meghalayan stage ("catastrophic drought"). Note: paper states these in "years BP" without an explicit cal BP label — flag as an ambiguity, not resolved here; no BCE conversion is given in the source as fetched.
**Source:** [Sengupta et al. 2020, NASA ADS abstract](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020JQS....35..382S)

*R.S. Bisht, "Excavations at Dholavira 1989–2005" (2015)* — the excavator's own report, 7 stages: I c. 3000–2900 BCE (nascence); II c. 2900–2800 BCE (adolescence); III c. 2800–2500 BCE (adulthood — major urban expansion, reservoirs and outer fortification built); IV c. 2500–2100 BCE (maturity/classical Harappan); V c. 2100–2000 BCE (ageing/decline); VI c. 1950–1800 BCE (transformation/Late Harappan); VII c. 1500–1450 BCE (de-urbanization).
Only two **thermoluminescence** (TL, not radiocarbon) dates are reported: DHR93/TL/4 = 2809±461 BCE (Stage III, internally consistent) and DHR93/TL/8 = 2302±480 BCE (nominally Stage II, but flagged by the excavator himself as unreliable/inconsistent with stratigraphy). Five radiocarbon samples were reportedly processed by D.P. Agrawal (Physical Research Laboratory) but their measured ages are not given in the fetched excerpt — treat those specific figures as `n.a.`.
**Source:** [Bisht 2015 excavation report, via pdfcoffee](https://pdfcoffee.com/excavations-at-dholavifra-1989-2005-rs-bisht-2015-pdf-free.html)

**Significance — water engineering (strong, extensively documented):** Dholavira is the paradigm case of Harappan hydraulic engineering. 16+ reservoirs cover roughly 10 hectares of the ~49-hectare walled city (≈20% of the city's area), forming a cascading system that exploits a 13 m natural gradient, fed by 3 dams on the Manhar channel and 2 on the Mansar channel. The monumental East Reservoir measures 73.4–73.5 m (top length) × 29.3 m (top width) × 7.2–7.5 m deep, rock-cut to 10.6 m in places; first built in Stage III, given monumental form in Stage IV, damaged by flood in Stage V, and ceased functioning by Stage VII. A separate southern rock-cut reservoir is described as the first rock-cut reservoir of its kind in a Harappan/prehistoric context. Other features include a castle well (13.6 m deep, 4.25 m diameter) with two attached tanks, and storm-water drains kept distinct from domestic sewage drains. The city had no perennial water source other than monsoon-fed artificial lakes/tanks, in a region receiving only 262 mm average annual rainfall.
**Sources:** Both above.
**Flags:** The ~10-hectare reservoir-coverage figure is a downward revision from an earlier ~17-hectare estimate cited in some secondary literature — use the Bisht excavation-report figure (~10 ha) as the better-sourced number.

### 2.4 Lothal (Gujarat, India) — the "dockyard" dispute
**Dates:** Harappan/Mature phase, 2600–1900 BCE (per the 2024 ScienceDirect abstract below); site is small, ~7 hectares.
**The dispute:**
- ASI excavator S.R. Rao (1955–1962 excavation, reported 1965) identified a large baked-brick basin (~215–222 m long, 37 m wide, ~4 m deep) as a dockyard for berthing ships 18–20 m long.
- Lawrence S. Leshnik (1968) challenged this, arguing the structure was a reservoir for drinking water/irrigation and that ship-draught requirements were not met.
- The structure's marine credentials were separately supported by National Institute of Oceanography (Goa) findings of foraminifera (marine microfossils), salt, and gypsum crystals inside it, consistent with the basin once holding seawater — but the primary NIO publication was not located in this pass; this specific claim currently rests on a Wikipedia citation (locator only) and should be sourced to a primary NIO paper before being treated as confirmed.
- ⚑ Recent contribution (2024): IIT Gandhinagar researchers (Ekta Gupta, V.N. Prabhakar, Vikrant Jain), using satellite and geomorphological data, published support for the dockyard interpretation in the *Journal of Archaeological Science* (vol. 170, Oct 2024) and *Journal of Maritime Archaeology* (vol. 19, issue 2). The ScienceDirect abstract itself is candid that "the debate surrounding the function of this structure remains unresolved."
- Critics (e.g., Gregory Possehl, cited via secondary press) note the absence of a plausible hinterland and shipbuilding remains, and favor an overland-trade interpretation; the site's modest size (7 ha) is cited as an argument against major-port status.
**Sources:** [Leshnik 1968, full text via safarmer.com](https://safarmer.com/Indo-Eurasian/Leshnik.pdf); [Indian Express, "Story of Lothal and new proof on its dockyard," 2024](https://indianexpress.com/article/opinion/columns/story-of-lothal-and-new-proof-on-its-dockyard-9593412/); [Archaeologymag, 2024](https://archaeologymag.com/2024/09/ancient-harappan-dockyard-at-lothal-india/); [ScienceDirect abstract, JAS vol. 170](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305440324001146)
**Flags:** Present BOTH the dockyard and reservoir/irrigation hypotheses; the field's own most recent primary-adjacent source (the 2024 ScienceDirect abstract) explicitly states the question is unresolved — do not present the dockyard interpretation as settled fact.

### 2.5 Rakhigarhi (Haryana, India)
**Dates:** AMS radiocarbon on charcoal samples at depths 9.1 m and 20.6 m gave calibrated dates of **2273±38 BCE** and **2616±73 BCE** respectively (Inter University Accelerator Centre, Delhi). The Rakhigarhi cemetery (RGR-7) is representative of the Mature Harappan and dated 2500–2000 BCE. ASI (via 2026 press) describes the site's overall date as "roughly 2600–1900 BCE."
**Significance:** The largest known Harappan site by area; alongside Dholavira, one of the two largest Harappan sites; source of the individual (I6113) analyzed in the 2019 aDNA studies (see §3).
**Excavation history:** ASI first excavated 1997–2000 (director Amarendra Nath, later convicted in 2015 for billing fraud related to that excavation — a fact about excavation administration, not about the site's dating); Deccan College (Vasant Shinde) 2012–2016; ASI resumed a multi-year program in 2021, ongoing.
**Sources:** [Kumar et al., PMC academic source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5821334/); [Indian Express, 2026, on Rakhigarhi DNA/facial reconstruction and ASI excavation](https://indianexpress.com/article/chandigarh/rakhigarhi-harappan-skeletons-dna-facial-reconstruction-asi-excavation-10750707/)
**Flags:** None on the dates themselves; note the excavation-fraud conviction is unrelated to date reliability but worth knowing for provenance questions about 1990s-era finds.

### 2.6 Kalibangan (Rajasthan, India)
**Dates:** See §1.7. Pre-Harappan settlement c. 2920–2550 BC (uncalibrated C14); Mature Harappan 2500–2000 BC.
**Significance:** Distinctive for a row of seven fire altars, interpreted as evidence of ritual/fire practices, and for what is described as the world's earliest attested ploughed field.
**Sources:** [GKToday](https://www.gktoday.in/kalibangan/); [Lucknow University PDF](https://www.lkouniv.ac.in/site/writereaddata/siteContent/202003281454237730durgesh_Kalibangan.pdf)
**Flags:** A claim attributed to B.B. Lal (former ASI Director-General) that radiocarbon dates show the site was abandoned ~2650 BC due to the drying of the Sarasvati/Ghaggar is currently sourced only to Wikipedia (locator only) in this research pass — treat this specific causal claim as `n.a.` pending a non-Wikipedia citation.

### 2.7 Ganweriwala (Cholistan Desert, Punjab, Pakistan)
**Status:** Discovered by Rafique Mughal in the 1970s via surface survey; largely unexcavated as of the sources gathered in this pass (excavation reportedly began or resumed around March 2024 per Pakistani press, but no excavation report was located/fetched).
**Size (disputed across surveys):** Mughal's original estimate 81.5 ha; Punjab University survey ~42 ha; Masih (2008) ~64 ha; most recent 2021 contour survey (considered most accurate) 66.7 ha.
**Dates:** Carbon samples from a surface mud-brick wall dated upper levels to Harappa Period 3C, approximately 2300–1900 BC. This figure currently traces to Wikipedia/Catalan Wikipedia (locator only); the best non-Wikipedia corroboration found is an academic conference PDF associating the site with the "urban phase (2600–1900 BCE)."
**Significance:** The third-largest known Indus site, believed contemporary with Mohenjo-daro and Harappa, situated near the ancient dry bed of the Sarasvati/Ghaggar.
**Sources:** [Dawn, on Cholistan excavation](https://www.dawn.com/news/1821052); [Tribune (Pakistan), Cholistan excavation report](https://tribune.com.pk/story/2468430/cholistan-excavation-report-likely-next-month); [EASAA conference PDF (Sidra Gulzar)](https://easaa.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/Sidra-Gulzar-3.pdf)
**Flags:** The 2300–1900 BC surface-wall date is `n.a.`-grade for citation purposes — no non-Wikipedia primary source with the specific figure was confirmed in this pass; use with that caveat, or omit the precise range and state only the general "urban-phase-contemporary" association from the EASAA PDF.

## 3. ⚑ THE 2019 RAKHIGARHI aDNA STUDIES (treated at length, per instructions)

This is the most politically sensitive material in the dataset. The notes below report **exactly** what each paper claims and does **not** claim, based on the full text of both papers as fetched from the Reich Lab repository.

### 3.1 Shinde et al. 2019, *Cell* — "An Ancient Harappan Genome Lacks Ancestry from Steppe Pastoralists or Iranian Farmers"
**Sample:** Individual I6113 (skeletal codes RGR7.3, BR-01, HS-02), female, from the Rakhigarhi cemetery, Haryana. Of 61 skeletal samples attempted, only I6113 yielded usable ancient DNA (200+ sequencing libraries; final analytical dataset 31,760 SNPs after damage restriction).

**Dating — direct dating of the individual FAILED.** Multiple attempts at AMS radiocarbon dating of I6113's own skeletal remains — including five attempts specifically on her skeletal elements — showed a carbon-to-nitrogen ratio outside the range appropriate for reliable dating. **There is no calibrated radiocarbon date for I6113 herself.** Instead, archaeological context is dated via 7 charcoal AMS radiocarbon dates from the main habitation area (depths 9–23 m), giving point estimates of **2800–2300 BCE**, described as falling "largely within the mature phase of the IVC." This charcoal-context range, not a direct date on the burial, is the only chronological anchor available.

**Ancestry findings:**
- I6113 fits as a mixture of ancient-Iranian-related ancestry (the larger component) and Southeast-Asian/Andamanese-hunter-gatherer-related ancestry (AASI/AHG).
- qpAdm modelling gives Iranian-related ancestry proportions of approximately 73%±6% (using western-Zagros + Andamanese proxies, p=0.103) or approximately 63%±6% (western-Zagros + East-Siberian proxies, p=0.24). The paper explicitly cautions that a statistically fitting model "does not mean it is the true source."
- This profile matches 11 other "outlier" individuals previously found at Gonur (Turkmenistan, 3 individuals) and Shahr-i-Sokhta (eastern Iran, 8 individuals); together with I6113 these 12 individuals are termed the "IVC Cline."
- **Critically, the Iranian-related ancestry in the IVC Cline is NOT the same as "Iranian farmer" ancestry.** The IVC Cline's Iranian-related lineage split from other Iranian-plateau lineages (including the farmer/herder populations of the Zagros) more than 12,000 years ago — predating even the oldest sampled Iranian hunter-gatherer (Belt Cave, ~10,000 BCE) and farmer (Ganj Dareh, ~8000 BCE) genomes. The paper uses this to explicitly reject the hypothesis that South Asian farming resulted from a large-scale westward-to-eastward migration of Zagros farmers after ~7000–6000 BCE.
- **No Steppe pastoralist-derived ancestry was detected** in I6113 or the wider IVC Cline. Direct quote: "These individuals had little if any Steppe pastoralist-derived ancestry, showing that it was not ubiquitous in north-west South Asia during the IVC as it is today." Statistically, the IVC Cline is "significantly depleted in Steppe pastoralist-related ancestry adjusting for its proportion of Iranian-related ancestry" (p = 0.018, two-sided Z test).
- **No Anatolian-farmer ancestry** was detected either, used as further evidence against the Zagros-farmer-migration hypothesis.
- Post-IVC gene flow into the Rakhigarhi region — from the northwest (more Steppe ancestry) and southeast (more southern-Indian-tribal-related ancestry) — means modern South Asians differ genetically from I6113's profile.
- Mitochondrial haplogroup U2b2 (I6113's) is nearly exclusive to South Asia today and was absent from ~400 sampled ancient Central Asians.

**What the paper explicitly does NOT claim:**
- Does not claim a qpAdm-fitting population is the true historical source population.
- Does not claim migrations were unimportant for the LATER spread of farming (leaves open whether an undocumented early South Asian farming-population expansion occurred; states this "is still unverified").
- Does not claim the single sample, or even the 12-individual IVC Cline, can "fully characterize a cosmopolitan ancient civilization."
- Does not claim Steppe ancestry was absent from every IVC individual or population everywhere — only that the documented IVC Cline profile shows "little if any."
- Notes that trace Anatolian-farmer-related ancestry seen in modern South Asians is consistent with having arrived via Steppe pastoralists (who themselves carried some Anatolian-farmer ancestry in admixed form) who the paper's own companion analysis places as spreading into South Asia c. 2000–1500 BCE.
**Source:** [Shinde, Narasimhan et al. 2019, *Cell*, full text via Harvard Reich Lab](https://reich.hms.harvard.edu/sites/reich.hms.harvard.edu/files/inline-files/2019_Cell_ShindeNarasimhan_Rakhigarhi_0.pdf); [journal page](https://www.cell.com/cell/fulltext/S0092-8674(19)31078-5)

### 3.2 Narasimhan et al. 2019, *Science* — "The Formation of Human Populations in South and Central Asia"
- Yamnaya steppe populations began spreading across the Eurasian Steppe c. 3300 BCE. Steppe-pastoralist-derived ("Western_Steppe_EMBA-related") ancestry reached Turan (the Central Asian/BMAC region) by 2100 BCE.
- "Indus Periphery Cline" = the same 11 outlier individuals referenced above (3 from Gonur, radiocarbon 2500–2000 BCE; 8 from Shahr-i-Sokhta, radiocarbon/context dates 3300–2000 BCE): no detectable Anatolian-farmer ancestry, elevated AHG-related ancestry (~11–50%), remainder (~50–89%) Iranian-farmer- and West-Siberian-hunter-gatherer-related. This contrasts with the "main" BMAC cluster (~60–65% Iranian-farmer, ~20–25% Anatolian, ~10% WSHG) and the main Shahr-i-Sokhta cluster (~16–21% Anatolian) — both statistically rejected as sources for the outliers (P<10⁻⁷).
- The Indus Periphery ancestry's own formation date (i.e., the admixture event between AHG-related and Iranian-farmer-related ancestry) is estimated at 71±15 generations before the sampled individuals lived, giving a 95% CI of roughly **5400–3700 BCE** (at 28 years/generation) — at least a millennium before the Mature IVC (2600–1900 BCE).
- The paper is explicit that, without ancient DNA from confirmed IVC burial contexts (i.e., data from inside the IVC's core, not its periphery), the authors "cannot make a definitive statement" that the Indus Periphery Cline is the same population as the IVC — they instead offer "six circumstantial lines of evidence" (including inferred direction of gene flow, argued to run from South Asia outward rather than the reverse).
- **Steppe ancestry timeline:** No detectable Steppe ancestry in the Indus Periphery Cline before 2000 BCE. The paper identifies a narrow window — "the first half of the second millennium BCE" — as when the Steppe ancestry (Central_Steppe_MLBA type) now widespread in South Asia must have arrived. A specific dated estimate: in Late Bronze/Iron Age individuals from Swat District, the relevant admixture is dated to a 95% CI of roughly **1900–1500 BCE** (26 generations before they lived). Between c. 2000–1000 BCE, Central_Steppe_MLBA-related people are inferred to have expanded toward South Asia and mixed with Indus-Periphery-Cline-related people to form the "Steppe Cline" (117 individuals sampled from Swat/Chitral, dated 1400 BCE–1700 CE).
- **ANI/ASI formation:** Neither the "Ancestral North Indian" (ANI) nor "Ancestral South Indian" (ASI) ancestry profiles were fully formed before the turn of the 2nd millennium BCE. ASI is estimated to have formed after 2000 BCE (a Palliyar-population-based estimate gives a 95% CI of 1700–400 BCE for the relevant admixture). Both ANI and ASI derive the majority of their ancestry (≥55% for ASI, ≥70% for ANI) from Indus-Periphery-Cline-related populations.
- **Indo-European language claims — explicitly qualified, not asserted as proven.** The paper states the Steppe ancestry found in South Asia matches a Bronze Age Eastern European profile and states this "likely" spread certain shared Indo-Iranian/Balto-Slavic linguistic features (the satem sound change, the ruki sound law) — but explicitly states "the path by which this ancestry arrived in South Asia is uncertain," and notes "there are so few material culture similarities between the Central Steppe and South Asia … that some archaeologists report no evidence of a connection." Brahmin and Bhumihar groups show statistically elevated Steppe-ancestry proportions (Z-scores as low as −7.9), offered only as "a possible explanation," not proof. The paper never uses the term "Vedic culture," never dates the emergence of Vedic culture, and never identifies any sampled individual as "Vedic."
- A noted but not over-interpreted asymmetry: most Steppe ancestry in modern South Asians is Y-chromosome-biased (associated with the R1a-Z93 subclade, a male-line signal), whereas in the ancient Swat individuals studied, Steppe ancestry was incorporated mostly via the female line — the paper flags this pattern without drawing further conclusions from it here.
**Source:** [Narasimhan, Patterson, Moorjani, Rohland, Bernardos, Mallick, Lazaridis, Reich et al. 2019, *Science*, full text via Harvard Reich Lab](https://reich.hms.harvard.edu/sites/reich.hms.harvard.edu/files/inline-files/eaat7487.full_.pdf)

### 3.3 ⚠ COMMON MISCONCEPTION
Indian press coverage (multiple outlets) framed the Shinde et al. finding as proof of "no Aryan gene" in the Harappans, effectively refuting the Indo-Aryan migration hypothesis outright. **This is a simplification.** The Shinde paper's "no steppe ancestry" finding applies specifically to the ONE directly analyzed Mature-IVC-period individual (and the 11 comparably dated IVC Cline individuals from Gonur/Shahr-i-Sokhta) — it is not a claim that steppe ancestry never entered South Asia at any time. The companion Narasimhan paper, published the same year by an overlapping author team, explicitly documents steppe-derived ancestry arriving in South Asia in the window c. 2000–1500 BCE — essentially the standard timeframe associated with the Indo-Aryan migration hypothesis, though modeled as gradual population mixture rather than "invasion." Both findings are correct and mutually consistent (no steppe ancestry in the sampled Mature-IVC-period individual; steppe ancestry arriving afterward, in the early 2nd millennium BCE) — the tension is between the precise, narrow claims of the papers and the broader "case closed" framing of much popular coverage.
**Sources for the misconception framing (secondary, for context only, not for dates):** contemporary Indian press coverage of the 2019 papers; the corrective is grounded in the primary papers themselves (§3.1, §3.2).

## 4. INDUS DECLINE: 4.2ka event, monsoon weakening, Sarasvati/Ghaggar-Hakra

### 4.1 The 4.2ka event and monsoon weakening
- Staubwasser et al. and a 2019 *Climate of the Past* paper: an oxygen-isotope record (planktonic foraminifera *G. ruber*) from the NE Arabian Sea shows an abrupt decrease in rainfall/Indus river discharge at 4.2 ka, described as "coherent with the termination of urban Harappan civilization." The greatest change occurred at 4.1 ka, when both the summer and winter monsoon weakened.
**Sources:** [Copernicus, *Climate of the Past*, 2019, full text](https://cp.copernicus.org/articles/15/73/2019/cp-15-73-2019.pdf); [Semantic Scholar record for Staubwasser et al.](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Climate-change-at-the-4.2-ka-BP-termination-of-the-Staubwasser-Sirocko/2a68fd2fd76feee92a8b9b3fc1a7e4af3b41b3a4)
- A 2023 *Nature Communications Earth & Environment* paper refines the pulse: a roughly 230-year period of increased summer-and-winter drought frequency between 4.2 and 3.97 ka, with multi-decadal aridity events centered at 4.19, 4.11, and 4.02 ka.
**Source:** [Nature Communications Earth & Environment, 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43247-023-00763-z)
- Liviu Giosan and colleagues (WHOI, 2012 and 2018 press releases) present a GRADUAL model: monsoon decline began gradually around 2500 BCE and weakened progressively, making inundation agriculture unsustainable and driving population eastward — not a single sudden collapse event.
**Sources:** [WHOI, 2012](https://www.whoi.edu/press-room/news-release/climate-change-led-to-collapse-of-ancient-indus-civilization-study-finds/); [WHOI, 2018](https://www.whoi.edu/press-room/news-release/climate-change-likely-caused-migration-demise-of-ancient-indus-valley-civilization/)
- Giosan et al. 2018 (*Climate of the Past*) further note that a decline in the winter monsoon 3300–3000 years ago (i.e., much later, roughly 1300–1000 BCE) may ALSO have played a role, implying multiple distinct climate pulses affected different phases of Harappan decline, not only the 4.2ka event.
**Source:** [Giosan et al. 2018, UCL repository PDF](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10047870/7/Goisan_Indus-NeoGlacial_cp-14-1669-2018.pdf)
**Flags:** Present the sudden-4.2ka-event model and the gradual-multi-pulse model as complementary but distinct hypotheses within the literature, not as a single settled narrative.

### 4.2 Sarasvati / Ghaggar-Hakra — a genuinely contested river history
Multiple, only partially reconcilable positions were found:
- **Dave et al. 2017, *Scientific Reports*:** Nd/Sr isotope analysis of sediment cores from the Great Rann of Kachchh suggests a large, Himalayan-glacier-fed river reached the Arabian Sea until roughly 10,000 years ago — i.e., this large glacial-river phase predates the entire Harappan period.
**Source:** [Nature, *Scientific Reports*, 2017](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-017-05745-8)
- **Saini et al. 2009** (via IIT Gandhinagar/Danino compilation): a study of buried channels in the Haryana Plains found evidence of channel activity during the mid-Holocene, describing a "second fluvial phase" dated roughly 6.0–2.9 ka (i.e., approximately 3900–2300 BCE) — spanning the Early Harappan through most of the Mature Harappan period.
**Source:** [IIT Gandhinagar compilation PDF, asc.iitgn.ac.in]
- **Peter Clift, 2008/2009** (cited via multiple secondary academic sources): "between 2000 and 3000 BCE, flow along a presently dried-up course known as the Ghaggar-Hakra River ceased, probably driven by weakening monsoon and possibly also headwater capture into the adjacent Yamuna and Sutlej Rivers" — a combined climate-and-tectonic (river-capture) explanation.
**Source:** cited via [epgp.inflibnet.ac.in course material]
- **Contested endpoint:** Some researchers (e.g., Francfort, using SPOT satellite imagery, cited via a lower-quality secondary source) argue the Sarasvati/Ghaggar-Hakra was already drying by the mid-4th millennium BCE — i.e., PRE-Harappan — which if correct would mean the river's decline could not have causally driven the IVC's end, since the IVC's urban phase hadn't yet begun. Other researchers date the river's final desiccation to 2000–1400 BCE, coincident with Late Harappan decline, supporting a causal link.
**Flags:** ⚠ GENUINE, UNRESOLVED DATING DISAGREEMENT on when the river dried — present both the pre-Harappan (mid-4th-millennium) and Late-Harappan-contemporary (2000–1400 BCE) desiccation claims side by side; do not average them. The Francfort-attributed claim in particular needs a better non-Wikipedia/non-secondary citation before being used as a firm data point — currently `n.a.`-grade for precise sourcing.
**⚠ COMMON MISCONCEPTION:** The idea that the Sarasvati/Ghaggar-Hakra "dried up overnight" or "vanished" is likely an overstatement. Multiple sources (of varying quality) describe the process as a centuries-long, gradual shrinkage from a perennial, Himalayan-glacier-fed river to an intermittent, monsoon-fed stream, with smaller channels and monsoon-driven flow persisting long after the main glacial-fed channel was lost. Flag this explicitly whenever the dataset discusses the river's "disappearance."

## 5. INDUS SCRIPT: undeciphered status and the Farmer–Sproat–Witzel debate

### 5.1 Farmer, Sproat & Witzel 2004 — "The Collapse of the Indus-Script Thesis"
**Citation:** Farmer, S., Sproat, R., & Witzel, M. (2004). "The Collapse of the Indus-Script Thesis: The Myth of a Literate Harappan Civilization." *Electronic Journal of Vedic Studies* 11(2): 19–57.
**Core argument (10 points, summarized):** (1) sign-frequency/repetition statistics are abnormal for a language; (2) inscriptions are too short (longest under 30 signs, average 4–5) to encode linguistic messages; (3) an unusually high proportion of rare "singleton" signs (about 1 in 5 signs used only once); (4) no sign repetition within single inscriptions; (5) no evidence that "lost" longer manuscripts on perishable media ever existed; (6) no cursive or evolving script variant, and no evidence of a scribal class; (7) no writing equipment (inkpots, styli) has been found; (8) by analogy with Near Eastern non-linguistic symbol systems, the signs are argued to be emblematic/religious rather than linguistic; (9) alternatively, writing may have been known but consciously rejected; (10) the authors argue this non-writing thesis explains features of the civilization better than the writing hypothesis. Their conclusion: the Indus symbols "were not even evolving in linguistic directions after at least 600 years of use."
**Sources:** [Full text via etana.org](https://etana.org/node/7709); [Harappa.com PDF summary](https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/indus-writing.pdf); [contemporary discussion thread](https://list.indology.info/pipermail/indology/2004-December/028822.html)

### 5.2 The response: Rao, Yadav, Vahia, Joglekar, Adhikari & Mahadevan, 2009, *Science*
**Title:** "Entropic Evidence for Linguistic Structure in the Indus Script."
**Finding:** The conditional entropy of Indus inscriptions is close to that of natural languages and far from non-linguistic systems (e.g., DNA, protein sequences) across different token-set sizes, which the authors argue is evidence FOR linguistic structure.
**Rao's own characterization (rebuttal PDF) is narrower than sometimes portrayed:** "our results provide evidence which, given the rich syntactic structure in the script, increases the probability that the script represents language" — explicitly NOT a claim of proof or decipherment.
**Sources:** [*Science*, doi:10.1126/science.1170391](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1170391); [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19389998/); [Full text PDF](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~rao/ScienceIndus.pdf); [Rao's rebuttal](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~rao/IndusResponse.pdf)

### 5.3 Farmer/Sproat's counter-rebuttal
Farmer and Sproat argue Rao et al.'s "non-linguistic" comparison baselines were two artificial, invented systems (200,000 randomly-ordered signs and 200,000 fully-ordered signs) rather than real-world non-linguistic sign systems, calling the comparison methodologically invalid, and argue conditional entropy alone cannot distinguish linguistic from non-linguistic sign systems.
**Sources:** [safarmer.com Refutation](https://safarmer.com/Refutation3.pdf); [safarmer.com follow-up](https://safarmer.com/more.on.Rao.pdf)
Ongoing academic exchange continued for years afterward (Sproat 2010 in *Computational Linguistics*, with Rao reply).
**Sources:** [ACL Anthology, Sproat 2010](https://aclanthology.org/J10-4017.pdf); [Rao reply](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~rao/IndusCompLing.pdf)

### 5.4 ⚑ Very recent (2026) contribution — debate still unresolved
A 2026 arXiv preprint, "How Non-Linguistic Is the Indus Sign System? A Synthetic-Baseline Scorecard" (NOT peer-reviewed), re-evaluates the four core Farmer-Sproat-Witzel structural arguments against a corpus of 1,916 deduplicated inscriptions (584 unique symbols) and finds the Indus corpus occupies an "intermediate position," matching neither a clean linguistic baseline nor a clean non-linguistic baseline.
**Source:** [arXiv preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.17828)
**Flags:** ⚑ As of 2026, the writing-vs-non-writing debate remains genuinely unresolved in the newest literature, not settled in either direction. Present as an open scholarly question, not as a resolved matter — regardless of how confidently either side is sometimes summarized in secondary sources.

### 5.5 Dates of attestation
- Harappa.com (non-Wikipedia, scholar-run): earliest Indus writing is attested from the Early Harappan Kot Diji period, dated at Harappa to c. 2800–2600 BCE. Precursor marks (pre-firing potter's marks and post-firing graffiti, which eventually fed into the later full script) appear earlier still, before roughly 3300 BC, during the Ravi Phase at Harappa (dated by this same source to 3800–2800 BC — note this is a WIDER range than the 3300–2800 BC given for the Ravi phase in §1.2/§2.2 above; this inconsistency across sources on the same site is unresolved here and should be flagged, not silently reconciled). The fully developed script is dated to c. 2600–1900 BC (Mature Harappan).
**Source:** [Harappa.com, "How old is the oldest ancient Indus writing?"](https://www.harappa.com/answers/how-old-oldest-ancient-indus-writing)
- Claims of a Balakot potsherd graffito and a Kunal seal each dated to c. 4000 BCE were found only in lower-quality secondary sources during this pass and could not be traced to a confirmable primary excavation report — treat both as `n.a.` pending better sourcing.
- Wikipedia (locator only) gives an overall attested range of c. 2800–1900 BCE for the developed script (with a possible proto-script from c. 3500 BCE), and states rare "graffiti" persisted until roughly 1300 BCE — this latest-attestation figure was not independently confirmed against a non-Wikipedia source in this pass and should be treated as `n.a.` for citation purposes until corroborated.
**Overall status:** The Indus script remains formally UNDECIPHERED. No source in this pass claims decipherment; all claim only evidence bearing on whether it represents language at all.

## 6. SOUTHERN NEOLITHIC: ashmounds, zebu pastoralism, millet agriculture (independent centre — new to dataset)

### 6.1 Overall chronology (Allchin & Allchin framework, still widely used)
- Southern Neolithic overall: **2500–1000 BC** (some sources: began "not earlier than c. 2800 cal BC" in northern Karnataka).
- Phase I: 2500–2000 BC (alternatively stated as "first half of the 3rd millennium BC")
- Phase II: 2000–1600 BC (alternatively "2300–1800 cal BC" in a different source)
- Phase III: 1600–1000 BC (alternatively "1800–1200 cal BC")
- Watgal Neolithic occupational sequence: c. 2700 BC to post-1500 BC.
**Sources:** [ebooks.inflibnet.ac.in, Southern Neolithic of India chapter](https://ebooks.inflibnet.ac.in/icp02/chapter/87/); [UCL EASAA 2001 paper (Fuller)](https://www.homepages.ucl.ac.uk/~tcrndfu/articles/EASAA2001a.pdf)
**Flags:** Note the two sources give somewhat different absolute-date brackets for Phases II and III (2000–1600/1600–1000 BC vs. 2300–1800/1800–1200 cal BC) — present both without averaging; this reflects real variation across published phase schemes, not an error.

### 6.2 Origins relative to the rest of South Asia
The Max Planck synthesis (Boivin, Fuller, Korisettar) states the South Deccan Neolithic began "sometime in the 3rd millennium BC" — explicitly NOT one of the world's earliest Neolithic transitions, nor the earliest in South Asia (i.e., later than the northwest South Asian Neolithic at Mehrgarh). By the mid-3rd millennium BC, Southern Neolithic sites show a mixed economy of pastoralism plus indigenous crop cultivation.
**Source:** [Max Planck repository, full text](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2248630/component/file_2248629/content)

### 6.3 Zebu cattle and pastoralism
- Sheep and goat, which have no wild ancestors in the region, were introduced from northwest India by the mid-3rd millennium BC.
- Cattle domestication may be partly indigenous to South India — a debated point (Allchin & Allchin 1974 vs. other researchers), not resolved in the sources fetched.
- The zebu (a longhorned, slender-bodied, humped cattle breed, *acutifrons*) holds "pre-eminent symbolic status," dominating Southern Neolithic rock art, and is "perfectly depicted" in rock bruisings at Velpumadugu (20 km southeast of Bellary, Anantapur District).
- Domestic-animal abundance order at Southern Neolithic sites: cattle > buffalo > sheep > goat > pig > ass > horse > dog. *Bos*, *Bubalus*, *Capra*, and *Ovis* occur at almost all Chalcolithic sites between central India and the southern Deccan from 2500 BC onward. Cattle, buffalo, and pig were possibly native to South India; goat and sheep likely arrived from the Near East (sheep possibly via Baluchistan).
- Zebu domestication genetics: zebu cattle were domesticated 8,000–9,000 years BP and had dispersed throughout northwest South Asia by 6,000 years BP; South India (and Gujarat) may represent an ADDITIONAL, independent domestication centre.
**Sources:** [ebooks.inflibnet.ac.in](https://ebooks.inflibnet.ac.in/icp02/chapter/87/); [UCL EASAA 2001](https://www.homepages.ucl.ac.uk/~tcrndfu/articles/EASAA2001a.pdf); [Oxford Academic, *Molecular Biology and Evolution*, 2010](https://academic.oup.com/mbe/article/27/1/1/1127118); [Nature *Scientific Reports*, 2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-36444-7)

### 6.4 Ashmounds — dating, sites, and function
**What they are:** Large accumulations of burnt cattle dung, chemically and microscopically confirmed as dung-derived (Zeuner 1960; Majumdar & Rajaguru 1966), often associated with cattle-penning sites marked by post-holes (e.g., at Utnur, where Allchin's excavation found a rectangular post-hole-defined enclosure interpreted as a cattle pen). Ashmound deposits contain silicified grass phytoliths consistent with grazing-animal dung.
**Sites explicitly identified as excavated ashmounds:** Utnur/Utnoor, Piklihal, Palavoy, Sanganakallu (Kupgal), Kodekal, Budihal, Hulikallu, Godekal, Chopadamagudda.
**Dates:**
- At the Sanganakallu complex, AMS radiocarbon dates and stratigraphy from ashmounds at Sannarachammagudda and Hiregudda show ash-deposit formation over a period of "perhaps less than 200 years," between **1950 and 1750 BC**; ashmound activity ended around 1750 BC; a buried secondary ashmound at Hiregudda Area A predates 1700 BC. After ash formation ceased, intensive village activity continued over the ashmound surface for 500–700 years. The broader Neolithic period at Sanganakallu is dated 1950–1350 BC, with settlement beginning "not earlier than 2000 BC."
- Kudatini (Budikanama ashmound, Bellary District) and Budihal: no site-specific radiocarbon date was found in the sources fetched for this pass — treat as `n.a.` Both are classified by settlement-pattern typology instead: Kudatini is grouped among ashmounds "with no stratified deposits around them," interpreted as short-stay seasonal encampments of single pastoral groups; Budihal is grouped among ashmounds "with some habitation deposits around them," interpreted as longer-stay seasonal encampments of pastoral groups, often containing multiple ashmounds and located near lithic raw-material sources.
**Function debate:** The chemical/microscopic dung evidence is solid; interpretation of WHY the dung was periodically burned is debated. One hypothesis is disease-prevention burning of pens; Allchin's own assessment is that the "most promising explanation" is ritual/symbolic significance, given the episodic, patterned nature of the burning.
**Archaeobotanical caution:** An early claim by Robert Foote that straw impressions in ashmound cinder slags near Kudatini represented "great millet" (*Sorghum bicolor*) is explicitly rejected by more recent, systematic archaeobotanical studies, which found no evidence of sorghum — flagged in the source itself as a claim that "must … be disregarded."
**Sources:** [ebooks.inflibnet.ac.in](https://ebooks.inflibnet.ac.in/icp02/chapter/87/); [UCL EASAA 2001](https://www.homepages.ucl.ac.uk/~tcrndfu/articles/EASAA2001a.pdf)

### 6.5 Millet agriculture
- Staple cereals were dominated by foxtail millet (*Setaria* sp., including the bristly foxtail *Setaria verticillata*) and, in the Southern Neolithic core, *Brachiaria ramosa* — both argued to be native South Indian species, "presumably domesticated within South India independent of the introduction of agriculture from elsewhere."
- Sawa millet (*Echinochloa colona*) may also have been present as a naturally occurring peninsular grass.
- Kodo millet (*Paspalum scrobiculatum*) appears in the Iron Age and "perhaps" came into cultivation during the later 2nd millennium BC (found at Daimabad in the Jorwe period and at Veerapuram, Kurnool District, in the Iron Age).
- Introduced crops present in the record include African pearl millet (*Pennisetum glaucum*) and finger millet (*Eleusine coracana*), both identified as introductions from elsewhere.
- Consistently recovered pulses: mung bean (*Vigna radiata*) and horsegram (*Macrotyloma uniflorum*), present from the earliest levels and, like the native millets, argued to be indigenously domesticated.
- Crop system had kharif (monsoon) seasonality: small millets and some mung bean matured in 3–4 months, horsegram in up to 6 months; the principal Neolithic harvest likely fell in September–October, with additional post-monsoon harvesting of millets and pulses October–December.
- Settlement-pattern model: intensive crop processing (grinding, de-husking) concentrated at permanent hilltop settlements (Sanganakallu, Kurugodu, Tekkalakota), while ashmound encampments show only limited, small-scale final food-preparation activity — the paucity of plant remains at ashmounds does NOT mean plants were unimportant in the diet, only that intensive processing happened elsewhere.
**Sources:** [ebooks.inflibnet.ac.in](https://ebooks.inflibnet.ac.in/icp02/chapter/87/); [UCL EASAA 2001](https://www.homepages.ucl.ac.uk/~tcrndfu/articles/EASAA2001a.pdf)

## 7. IF-TIME-PERMITS ITEMS (researched at lower depth; several figures remain `n.a.`)

### 7.1 Ochre Coloured Pottery (OCP) culture
**Dates — genuinely scattered across the literature, presented without averaging:**
- Commonly cited general range: "2000–1500 BCE" (locator-only Wikipedia framing).
- Early specimens near Jodhpura, Rajasthan: reported dates "about 3000 BCE" (Agrawala 1982), i.e., considerably earlier than the general range.
- Thermoluminescence dates from Atranjikhera, Lal Qila, Jhinjhana, and Nasirpur: range 2650–1500 BCE (some secondary sources instead cite this site group as 2600–1200 BC).
- Akinori Uesugi's proposed alternative: c. 1900–1300 BCE, treating OCP as a Late Harappan expansion continuous with the preceding Bara style (c. 2300–1900 BCE) of the Ghaggar valley — i.e., a "Bara-OCP cultural complex" rooted in the Indus Civilization rather than a separate culture.
- Copper Hoard artefacts (often associated with OCP) from one AMS study: 3610–4330 cal BP (≈1670–2380 BC).
- The Sinauli site, associated with OCP/Copper Hoard material, dates to ~4000 yr BP (≈2000 BCE) per 14C dating.
**Sources:** [Wikipedia, OCP culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ochre_Coloured_Pottery_culture) (locator only); [Facebook-linked summary of AMS copper-hoard dating study] (secondary, `n.a.`-grade — flagged, do not use as sole source); [Scribd, Sinauli excavation report summary] (secondary, `n.a.`-grade)
**Flags:** No single peer-reviewed primary source with a clean radiocarbon chronology for OCP as a whole was fetched in this pass — treat the overall OCP date range as unsettled and multi-valued, and flag any specific figure used as provisional. Charcoal samples for direct radiocarbon dating of OCP levels have historically been difficult to obtain (one source notes explicitly that OCP dating has relied on thermoluminescence rather than radiocarbon because no usable charcoal was recovered).

### 7.2 Painted Grey Ware (PGW) culture
**Dates — also contested, at least two distinct chronological camps:**
- Conventional/"short" chronology: c. 1200–600/500 BCE (Period I c. 1300–1000 BCE in the Ghaggar valley/upper Ganga; Period II c. 1000–600 BCE, spreading into the western Ganga valley; Period III c. 600–300 BCE, with eastern interactions). Some sites push the PGW/NBPW transition to c. 700 BCE.
- Radiocarbon anchors supporting an earlier start: Alamgirpur (near Delhi), sample OxA-21882, from a layer transitional between Late Harappan and earliest PGW, calibrated to 1647 BCE by one report and to a 2136–1948 BCE calibrated range by another secondary summary of the same excavation (an internal inconsistency in secondary reporting of the same sample — flag, do not resolve). Gosna (near Mathura), ASI excavation (B.R. Mani, Vinay Kumar Gupta, 2009): two radiocarbon dates of 2160 BCE and 2170 BCE from a PGW-associated deposit — but the same excavators caution there is a possibility this horizon may actually belong to a different, earlier cultural period, not true PGW.
- A "long chronology" school treats Period IA at some sites as dating to 2600–2200 BCE using Libby half-life conventions (which yield dates at least ~100 years younger than Cambridge half-life conventions applied to the same raw counts) — a methodological, not just interpretive, source of disagreement.
- Other radiocarbon anchors cluster PGW's core span later: Atranjikhera (11th century BCE), Noh (9th century BCE), with most PGW settlements dated to the 7th–6th centuries BCE.
**Sources:** [Wikipedia, PGW culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Painted_Grey_Ware_culture) (locator only for the overview, but reports several specific lab dates attributable to named excavators — those specific numbers, e.g. the Gosna and Alamgirpur dates, are usable if cross-checked, since they cite the named excavation reports directly); [Heritage University of Kerala PDF, "Painted Grey Ware Culture: Changing Perspectives"](https://www.heritageuniversityofkerala.com/JournalPDF/Volume5/23.pdf); [IJMRA PDF on PGW in the upper Ganga basin](https://www.ijmra.us/project%20doc/2016/IJRSS_JANUARY2016/IJRSSJan16Kam.pdf)
**Flags:** ⚠ Genuinely unresolved, actively debated span — some researchers place PGW's beginning as early as the mid-3rd millennium BCE (a "long chronology" reading of a small number of early dates at transitional sites), while the great majority of radiocarbon evidence supports a core span of roughly 1200/1100–500 BCE. Present both readings; the 2600–2200 BCE-range dates should be flagged as outlier/contested rather than representative of the culture's typical span.

### 7.3 Burzahom (Kashmir Neolithic)
**Dates:** Eight radiocarbon dates from TIFR Bombay (charcoal, run 1965–66) anchor the site's chronology. Under MASCA calibration, four periods are distinguished: Period IA (Aceramic Neolithic) c. 3000–2850 BCE; Period IIA (Early Ceramic Neolithic) c. 2850–2550 BCE; Period IIB (Late Ceramic Neolithic, wheel-thrown pottery appears) c. 2550–1700 BCE; Period III (Megalithic) c. 1500–1000 BCE. A commonly repeated shorthand states the overall Neolithic-to-Megalithic span at Burzahom runs roughly 3000–1000 BCE, with earliest occupation dated to "before c. 2920 BC" in one source and "before 2357 BC" in another (Wikipedia, locator only) — these two "before" figures are inconsistent with each other and with the MASCA-calibrated Period IA start of c. 3000 BCE; treat the precise earliest-occupation figure as unsettled pending a single authoritative source, though all cluster in the 3rd-millennium-BCE range.
Individual uncalibrated lab dates (half-life 5730 years, useful for anyone needing raw figures before calibration): TF-15 1530±110 BC; TF-129 1825±110 BC; TF-13 1850±125 BC; TF-14 2025±350 BC; TF-127 2100±115 BC; TF-123 2225±115 BC; TF-128 2375±120 BC.
**Sources:** [Swaveda, "The pit-dwellers of Burzahom"](https://www.swaveda.com/articles/burzahom-pit-dwellers/); [ikashmir.net PDF, "Earliest Settlers of Kashmir"](http://www.ikashmir.net/rnkaw/doc/earliest_settlers.pdf); [University of Arizona *Radiocarbon* journal, 1965, original lab-date table](https://journals.uair.arizona.edu/index.php/radiocarbon/article/viewFile/3302/2894)
**Flags:** Distinguish clearly between the raw uncalibrated TF-series lab dates (BC, half-life 5730 yr, base year AD 1950) and the MASCA-calibrated period boundaries — do not mix the two without noting which is which, per §1.7's general calibration caution.
